# Clarification 1. What the forgetting precedent establishes

The supporting example on p.5, section 4.1, proposes previewing paid information and forgetting rejected content, citing Weiss et al., COLM 2024 (OpenReview Zq9Dfj4nBo). Its strongest reading combines a general deletion guarantee with no loss to the publisher. The exact COLM final PDF was inaccessible (browser verification and HTTP 403). The accessible earlier preprint, arXiv:2403.14443v1, differs in title and author order and is identified separately, rather than substituted for the final text.

That earlier primary source explicitly assumes a marketplace regulator controlling buyer-agent behavior (section 2) and describes retaining purchased content for later use (section 3.2). The authors’ corresponding public repository was checked statically at commit 62e539900cc3d3d5fd76fef035d6de93bda77171. simulator.py lines349-353 retain rejected Quote objects in _rejected_quotes; lines575-588 include them in evaluation summaries. schema.py lines227-231 includes their answer blocks, whose summaries include raw content at lines402-403. Thus rejection does not erase every copy in that simulator.

Counterevidence is decisive for a fair interpretation: simulator.py lines403-420 gives only _accepted_quotes to final-answer synthesis and follow-up generation. The implementation therefore supports selective information flow within a trusted architecture. Retaining audit data inside a trusted simulator is compatible with restricting an agent’s designated downstream calls; it is not, by itself, a bug in that cited system.

The inference from controlled downstream access to a guarantee for arbitrary user-controlled software requires a defined trust boundary, allowed outputs, retained state and verification mechanism. Economic zero-loss also requires its own assumptions. This report records that stronger interpretation as Needs Evidence, without claiming an impossibility theorem or a broken target-paper experiment. Reading the passage as a conditional future mechanism is consistent with the paper’s acknowledged infrastructure and alignment requirements. No security repair was implemented or verified.

# Clarification 2. Pareto language is not a welfare theorem

The last paragraph of section5.3, p.8, describes the proposal as a Pareto improvement under appropriate safety and governance. A formal comparison would require a specified population, utilities, baseline and no-loss conditions. None is supplied. Reduced platform rents could disadvantage platform owners if they are included, while consumer-only evaluation would still need capability, convenience, cost and security tradeoffs. Those tradeoffs are already acknowledged in section5.

This observation is not an original-assumption counterexample: the formal assumptions are unspecified. The defensible reading is a proposed improvement on targeted platform harms, conditional on governance and technical feasibility. Formal universal dominance remains Needs Evidence. The central recommendation does not depend on a demonstrated welfare theorem, and no numerical or mathematical repair is asserted.
