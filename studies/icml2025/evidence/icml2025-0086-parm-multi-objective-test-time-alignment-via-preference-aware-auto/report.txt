# 2. Problem 1: the stated response law and decoder differ

Source claim and suspect step. Section 4.4, p. 6, defines the reward as the sum of ARM token log probabilities (Eq. 11), substitutes it into a response-level aligned distribution (Eq. 12), and then presents Eq. (13) as the next-token conditional obtained according to Eq. (12). The same transition appears in the GenARM background, p. 3, Eqs. (4)-(5). The defect is the marginalization over unseen suffixes, not the validity of either individual autoregressive model.

\[P^*(y)=Z^{-1}\prod_{t=1}^{T}b(y_t\mid y_{<t})q(y_t\mid y_{<t})^{1/\beta},\qquad Q(u\mid h)=\frac{b(u\mid h)q(u\mid h)^{1/\beta}}{\sum_v b(v\mid h)q(v\mid h)^{1/\beta}}.\]

Counterexample parameters. Fix one prompt, beta = 1, preference alpha = (1/2,1/2), binary tokens 0 and 1, and a horizon of two generated tokens. Let the frozen base b and the ARM q coincide, with the following conditionals. A terminal symbol may be appended after the fixed horizon.

Prefix | b(0 | prefix) = q(0 | prefix) | b(1 | prefix) = q(1 | prefix)

Prefix | b(0 | prefix) = q(0 | prefix) | b(1 | prefix) = q(1 | prefix)

Empty | 1/2 | 1/2

0 | 9/10 | 1/10

1 | 1/2 | 1/2

Assumption check. Every allowed token probability is strictly positive and each conditional sums to one. The base is fixed. The ARM is a normalized autoregressive model. The reward is exactly log q(y), beta is positive and matches the experimental setting, alpha lies in the interior of the simplex, and Z is finite. The claimed algebraic implication imposes neither b != q nor exact training optimality. The example audits that implication, not the actual outputs of an Alpaca checkpoint.

Response | b(y) = q(y) | Eq. (12): P*(y) | Eq. (13): Q(y)

Response | b(y) = q(y) | Eq. (12): P*(y) | Eq. (13): Q(y)

00 | 9/20 | 27/44 | 81/164

01 | 1/20 | 1/132 | 1/164

10 | 1/4 | 25/132 | 1/4

11 | 1/4 | 25/132 | 1/4

The unnormalized Eq. (12) weights are 81/400, 1/400, 1/16 and 1/16; Z = 33/100. Thus P*(first token = 0) = 41/66. Eq. (13) instead gives Q(first token = 0) = 1/2, a discrepancy of 4/33. These values are verified by the saved exact-arithmetic replay, not estimated by simulation.

# 3. Complete derivation, impact and scoped correction

Let w(u|h) = b(u|h) q(u|h)^(1/beta). Define H(h) as the sum over every allowed complete suffix after h of the product of its remaining w factors, and set H(terminal) = 1. Direct summation gives the exact conditional of Eq. (12):

\[H(h)=\sum_v w(v\mid h)H(hv),\qquad P^*(u\mid h)=\frac{w(u\mid h)H(hu)}{H(h)}.\]

Equation (13) drops H(hu). Equality holds only when H(hu) is constant across next tokens with positive weight. Separate normalization of b and q does not enforce that condition. In the example H(0) = 41/50 and H(1) = 1/2, so the missing factor changes the very first conditional.

A second check measures the precise consequence for the response-level regularized objective. For beta = 1 and J(P) = E_P[log q(y)] - KL(P||b), the Gibbs identity gives J(P*) - J(Q) = KL(Q||P*) > 0. The replay obtains J(P*) = -1.1086626245, J(Q) = -1.1389462402 and a gap of 0.03028361568 nats. This finite-model calculation establishes an objective mismatch; it is not a benchmark-performance claim.

Dependency and supported impact. Equation (13) is the proposed inference mechanism. Equation (12) is its connection to the response-level reward-aligned law introduced in Eq. (1). The invalid inference breaks that connection even at a two-token horizon. A demonstration that published HV or MIP scores deteriorate is unnecessary to establish this central derivation failure.

Alternative reading and counterevidence. The tilde on the decoder distribution can be read as indicating an approximate or surrogate policy. Such a policy is well-defined and may work empirically. However, Section 4.4 does not state the continuation-factor approximation and explicitly connects Eq. (13) to Eq. (12). If only a heuristic was intended, that intention narrows the claim; it does not make the exact conditionalization valid. The analogous background formula is attributed to GenARM, so this report does not claim the defect or its discovery originated with PARM.

Precise mathematical correction. Including H(hu) restores the exact conditional by the identity above. This correction is analytically verified for finite horizons, and reduces to Eq. (13) under the stated constant-continuation condition. Computing H generally requires summing over future completions; no equally efficient corrected implementation is claimed. Alternatively, retain Eq. (13) as a local heuristic and withdraw the exact response-level derivation. That is a scope correction, not verification of the original equality.

What survives. The PBLoRA parameterization, Theorem 4.1, the existence and local normalization of the decoder, and the use of one preference-conditioned ARM survive. The reported empirical comparisons have not been independently refuted. Measuring the empirical effect of exact or approximate continuation correction would require fresh experiments, which were not performed.

# 4. Checks that constrain the finding

Theorem 4.1 and Appendix C, pp. 4-5 and 13-14. The theorem assumes B has column rank r and A has row rank r. A zero linear combination of the claimed outer products is BCA = 0. Apply a left inverse of B and a right inverse of A to obtain C = 0. This verifies all r^2 products are independent and agrees with the appendix’s columnwise proof.

\[L B=I_r,\quad A R=I_r,\quad BCA=0\ \Longrightarrow\ C=L(BCA)R=0.\]

Expressiveness scope. With A and B fixed, arbitrary W spans r^2 directions and diagonal W spans r directions. The theorem supports that comparison. With freely trainable factors, however, BWA = B’A with B’ = BW, so no larger unrestricted rank-r matrix class follows. In the actual Eq. (9), a linear/affine W2(alpha) over the (k-1)-dimensional simplex traces at most k-1 affine directions after training. These qualifications preserve the valid theorem and the usefulness of preference conditioning; they are recorded as scope clarification, not another theorem defect.

Loss sign and code counterevidence. On p. 3 the text sets z = 1 when the first response is preferred, while Eq. (3) multiplies its log-probability difference by (-1)^z. Those two conventions disagree. The released trainer instead selects chosen and rejected responses from response IDs and uses the chosen-minus-rejected log-probability direction. This prevents an unsupported allegation that published experiments trained against preference labels. The isolated printed sign/label inconsistency is outside the requested substantive-finding scope.

Scalarization scope. The standard sufficient result uses strictly positive weights and an exact global scalar minimum. At boundary weights a scalar minimizer need only be weakly Pareto optimal; joint optimization over a shared conditioned model need not attain every independent per-preference minimum. Section 4.3’s practical approximation claim is therefore not treated as a verified guarantee of the entire true Pareto set. No additional neural-model counterexample was established here.

Empirical checks. The Table 2 HV improvement is 14.1333%; its MIP improvement is 223.75%. Table 3 improvements versus GenARM are 6.0735% and 91.1602%, and versus MOD-w2s are 26.0536% and 17.6871%. These agree with the reported rounded percentages. Thirty boundary plus six positive-interior preference points gives the stated 36-point evaluation design.

Appendix D gives a minimization-form hypervolume expression, while the reported reward axes favor larger values. A sign conversion and reference point are needed for replay. The inspected release does not supply a complete HV/MIP reproduction pipeline or the per-response outputs used in the tables. This limits empirical verification; it does not prove the reported metric values wrong. Oracle-reward results also do not by themselves establish human preference satisfaction.
