## 2. Outcome and evidentiary standard

The paper argues that protection of meaningful work should be part of AI safety, using economic mechanisms, prior studies and policy recommendations. Its P1-P6 statements are argumentative propositions, not formal theorems with a fully specified statistical or equilibrium model. This review did not impose an original experiment requirement on the normative recommendation.

One bounded factual error is demonstrated: a strong survey majority is reported as unanimity. Separately, an empirical source used to support unequal gains among scientists has been formally withdrawn. That invalidates reliance on that source as affirmative evidence, but does not establish the opposite substantive result.

The headline 21%, 17% and 55.8% figures can be traced to the cited primary studies. Their population, outcome and comparison conditions must accompany them. The review found no demonstrated Major invalid derivation or central empirical refutation within the inspected scope. This is not certification of the full paper, every citation, or the effectiveness of the policy package.

## 3. Problem 1: the author survey was not unanimous

Judgment: Minor, a local statistical overstatement.

Claim and location. Section 2.6, p. 6, describes a 2023 Authors Guild survey of more than 2,000 respondents as finding unanimous opposition to uncompensated, unconsented training use. The matching primary report, dated 21 December 2023, gives more than 2,400 respondents and a 96% / 3% / 1% response split: required consent and payment / accepted use without them / unsure [E4].

The observed nonzero dissent contradicts unanimity. This requires no hypothetical counterexample or estimation assumption. Reading the word as rhetorical emphasis explains the intended message but does not make the reported statistic exact.

Impact and repair. The strong-majority rationale survives. Replace unanimity with the stated 96% of survey respondents; do not generalize this sample to every author. This correction is verified against the primary summary. It has not been applied to the source paper, and author adoption remains unrequested.

## 4. Known source withdrawal: the scientific-productivity paragraph

Status: verified source withdrawal; affected empirical inference Needs Evidence.

On p. 5, Section 2.5.1, the paper invokes Toner-Rodgers (2024) for a mechanism in which leading scientists identify useful AI suggestions while others spend effort testing false positives. It then extends the concern to junior researchers. The cited work is Artificial Intelligence, Scientific Discovery, and Product Innovation, arXiv:2412.17866.

The primary record establishes two distinct events. MIT’s statement of 16 May 2025 disavowed confidence in the data and research and requested withdrawal [E5]. The arXiv record separately confirms administrator withdrawal on 20 May 2025, citing data-validity concerns and incomplete Institutional Review Board requirements [E6]. The April target preprint predates those events; the inspected publisher PDF and the separately saved July v2 retain the affected claim. This chronology is recorded without inferring author knowledge or intent.

The withdrawn study cannot presently establish the claimed distributional mechanism. Removing that reliance, or replacing it with independently credible evidence, is warranted. A replacement has not been supplied or validated here. Withdrawal does not prove that experienced scientists never benefit more from AI, nor does it invalidate the paper’s other learning-risk evidence. The unsupported local inference is therefore not upgraded to Major.

## 5. Primary-source checks that constrain interpretation

### Online labor demand

A preserved Demirci, Hannane and Zhu working paper dated 22 April 2024 reports 20.86% and 17.01%, matching the rounded figures. These are relative changes in job-post counts on one freelancing platform against manual-intensive categories, estimated using difference-in-differences. Tables 2-3 are on physical PDF pp. 12 and 14; the demand-versus-employment distinction is explicit on p. 4 [E7].

The target’s p. 3 correctly supplies the comparator. On p. 1, retain “job posts” for image-related work. These estimates are not counts of displaced workers or economy-wide employment losses. The review checked the estimand and numerical transformations, not the underlying dataset or every identifying assumption.

### Programming task performance

Peng et al., arXiv:2302.06590v1, reports a randomized study of 95 developers performing one JavaScript HTTP-server task. The headline figure concerns time among completers: 35 per arm, with means 71.17 and 160.89 minutes, giving a rounded 55.8% reduction [E8, pp. 3-5].

This supports the task-specific result, not a direct estimate of hiring, wages or full-project productivity. The study’s exploratory heterogeneity points toward larger benefits for less-experienced developers; it does not independently support the nearby high-skill-advantage prediction. The authors themselves discuss generalization limits on pp. 7-8. No numerical error in the cited headline was established.

### Learning risk and design dependence

The later published Bastani et al. study reports worse unaided performance after access to an unrestricted tutor, while a guarded tutor largely mitigated the harm. Its abstract and significance statement were checked in the PNAS record and author-hosted manuscript [E9]. This is compatible with the target’s concern that AI can harm learning. It does not justify treating all AI tutoring designs as equivalent. The complete experiment and its raw data were not reanalyzed.

## 6. Argument checks, alternatives and limits

Economic mechanisms. The life-cycle and permanent-income discussion identifies income uncertainty and adjustment costs as plausible pathways. It does not estimate a savings response or supply the preferences, borrowing constraints and income process needed for a unique prediction. Its expanded use of technical debt is a conceptual analogy, not a measured financial liability. These distinctions should remain explicit when presenting P1 as a risk mechanism.

Automation can displace some tasks while creating or complementing others. The paper itself acknowledges new-task mechanisms in Section 2.2 and discusses market adaptation in Section 4.1. Those passages are counterevidence against reading the paper as a formal assertion that every deployment necessarily destroys net employment. The categorical phrasing of P2 and the Section 3 opening should be understood with this context and the narrower empirical studies above. A general distributional forecast still needs more evidence than those studies alone.

Detection and watermarking. The p. 5 statement about impossibility after fine-tuning lacks a specified attack, model distribution, text length, false-positive constraint or detector access model. The subsequent paragraph concerns customized evasion, a narrower and defensible concern. The review therefore records a needed scope qualification rather than constructing a counterexample from an unrelated kind of fine-tuning. It establishes no universal impossibility theorem; the selected detector citations and their limits are recorded in the independent argument audit [E10].

Sections 2.5.2 and 3 explicitly acknowledge detector false positives and watermark bypasses; p. 5 cautions against using detection alone for discipline. Advocating improved watermarking despite imperfect robustness is not a logical contradiction. The collective-action discussion is an incentive analogy without a quantified payoff matrix, not an empirically established unique equilibrium.

Governance recommendations. Worker support, representation, licensing and disclosure are proposed interventions. Their desirability combines empirical expectations with value judgments. The paper does not establish that a single global licensing mechanism already exists or that Shapley allocation is universally fair under every normative criterion. No new proof of that cited mechanism is claimed here. Its technical details, jurisdiction-specific copyright questions, litigation outcomes and practical policy costs were not comprehensively audited in this review.

The retained analysis distinguishes these open questions from demonstrated errors. Absence of a new experiment alone is not used as a defect. Conversely, agreement with the broad aim of worker protection does not validate every factual premise or ensure that the proposed interventions will achieve it.
