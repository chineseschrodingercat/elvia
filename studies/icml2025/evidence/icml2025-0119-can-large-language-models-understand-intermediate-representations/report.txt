Abstract: Two supported Problems are identified. A Major construct-validity problem affects the execution-reasoning task: on the intended domain of valid reference-program tests, a constant `pass` predictor attains perfect label accuracy without inspecting the intermediate representation. This limits what the reported success rates can establish about execution understanding. A Minor reporting problem concerns inconsistent counts, denominators, and pass rates. Concrete examples of incorrect model reasoning remain informative, and the other three tasks are not invalidated by the shortcut. Historical model identity, evaluation scripts, unmatched completion sets, and missing result payloads are recorded as evidence limits rather than inferred experimental failures.

# Supported Problems

Problem 1 (Major: valid reference assertions admit a perfect label-only shortcut).  Claim and location. Section 3.1, p. 3, describes Task 4 as requiring dynamic interpretation of IR and accurate tracking of state. Section 3.5, p. 5, scores assertion predictions; Section 4.3, pp. 7–8, uses these scores to interpret execution understanding and the source-code/IR gap. Appendix C.4, p. 16, supplies IR and an assertion and requests a pass/fail answer with reasoning. Sections 3.3 and Appendix D identify the inputs as reference C++ programs and their HumanEval unit tests.

Construction and assumptions. Cell 0 of `src/Task4_Execute_Reasoning.ipynb` extracts lines beginning with `assert` from each JSONL record’s `test` field. It does not generate false assertions or mutate the tested reference program. The acquired file contains 164 distinct tasks; that exact line-selection rule yields 1,140 assertion lines, with at least one per task. This is a static inventory, not an execution audit. The paper’s stated average of 7.7 assertions and the inventory’s 6.951 require historical dataset reconciliation; neither number is silently substituted for the other.

The decisive argument assumes that these assertions are valid tests of their canonical reference implementations, that compilation preserves the relevant semantics, and that the quantitative score judges the final pass/fail label. These are the intended golden-data and scoring premises of the published design. No stronger assertion that all 164 programs were independently executed is made.

Decisive argument. Let \(A_{ij}\) be test \(j\) of reference program \(i\), with ground-truth label \(y_{ij}\). Under those premises, \(y_{ij}=1\) for every supplied test. The predictor \[h(\mathrm{IR}_i,A_{ij})=1\] ignores both inputs yet has \[\frac{\sum_{i,j}{\bf1}\{h=y_{ij}\}}{\sum_i m_i}=1,\qquad
 \frac{1}{N}\sum_i{\bf1}\{\forall j,\ h=y_{ij}\}=1.
 \tag{2.1}\] Thus both assertion-level accuracy and all-assertions-correct task accuracy can be perfect without any IR understanding. The result is independent of which of the two inconsistent rate definitions in Problem 2 is selected.

Three concrete cases were checked by direct reasoning from the pinned records. In CPP/2, \(3.5-\operatorname{int}(3.5)=0.5\), exactly representable. In CPP/121, the odd values at even positions of \((5,8,7,1)\) are 5 and 7, giving the asserted 12. In CPP/149, filtering odd-length strings from \((\texttt{aa},\texttt{a},\texttt{aaa})\) leaves only `aa`. These cases agree with the intended positive-test construction; they are not offered as a proof of every program’s correctness.

Alternative readings and counterevidence. An assertion testing that a Boolean function returns false is still a true assertion when its reference implementation is correct. It therefore does not supply a negative label for this task. Requesting a reasoning trace also does not remove (2.1) from a label-only score. The notebook’s cells 4 and 7 extract a pass/fail string; the reported quantitative metric does not independently verify every intermediate state.

Conversely, a model that answers incorrectly on a valid positive test has made a real prediction error. The paper’s qualitative examples of missed filtering or incorrect arithmetic remain useful evidence about those particular traces. This review does not claim that the tested models exploited the shortcut, that their recorded scores are fabricated, or that all their qualitative analyses fail. If the actual historical evaluation contains genuinely false oracle labels, its case-level labels and construction must be supplied; the retained construction does not document such a negative-test mechanism. Invalid canonical tests would instead call for an oracle audit.

Core dependency and precise impact. The construction underlies one of four core tasks and the RQ3 interpretation of measured execution reasoning. It demonstrates that high success, or a source-code/IR difference in such success, cannot by itself discriminate instruction-level understanding from a representation-dependent tendency to answer `pass`. The observed positive-case error rates remain descriptive. CFG, decompilation, and summarization results are outside this counterexample. The Major severity attaches to the task’s ability to measure its stated core construct, not to a claimed collapse of the whole study.

Correction boundary. Add independently verified false assertions or semantics-changing counterfactuals, remove trivial label cues, report label balance and a constant-predictor baseline, and verify selected execution states separately from the final label. With a balanced binary label set, a constant predictor’s accuracy is exactly \(1/2\); this removes this particular perfect-score shortcut, not every possible shortcut or ambiguity about understanding. New measurements are required before attributing a corrected score gap to execution capability.

Problem 2 (Minor: incompatible execution counts and pass-rate definitions).  Claim and location. Section 3.5, p. 5, defines overall pass rate using correctly predicted assertions across cases. Table 5, p. 7, instead says its denominator is the number of completed tasks and presents full-pass counts. Table 9, p. 17, repeats the counts and rates for three runs. The original pages were visually inspected to exclude an extraction artifact.

Arithmetic under the table definition. For a completed-task denominator, full-pass rate is \(n_{\rm pass}/n_{\rm completed}\). Independent exact-fraction calculation gives: \[\begin{array}{lrrr}
\hline
\text{Row}&n_{\rm pass}/n_{\rm completed}&\text{Recomputed}&\text{Reported}\\
\hline
\text{Table 5, DeepSeek SC}&119/164&0.725610&0.804\\
\text{Table 9, Run 1 SC}&133/164&0.810976&0.811\\
\text{Table 9, Run 2 SC}&146/164&0.890244&0.817\\
\text{Table 9, Run 3 SC}&139/164&0.847561&0.804\\
\hline
\end{array}\] The first, third, and fourth discrepancies are approximately 7.84, 7.32, and 4.36 percentage points, respectively, far larger than rounding at the displayed precision. Run 1 is a useful countercheck that the ratio interpretation is numerically consistent for an adjacent row. Table 9 also prints \(119/164\) as \(0.720\) for Llama 3.1; ordinary rounding to three decimals gives \(0.726\).

Alternative reading. An assertion-weighted rate need not equal the proportion of fully correct programs. That reading could explain some numerical differences, but would contradict Table 5’s stated denominator and cannot be reconstructed from its pass/partial counts without per-case assertion totals and correct-label counts. Thus at least one of the definition, counts, or reported rates needs correction; the evidence does not identify the intended replacement for every cell.

Dependency, impact, and correction. These numbers feed Section 4.3’s quantitative comparisons and Appendix E’s stability claim. The appendix’s DeepSeek SC counts exceed Code Llama’s main-table count of 131, whereas the main text calls Code Llama the SC leader. Different runs or metrics might explain this, but the historical mapping is not supplied. Repair requires a case-level ledger and a declared aggregation rule, not choosing whichever number supports a ranking. Under the displayed alternative full-pass counts, the broad SC-over-IR direction still holds; this arithmetic finding alone does not reverse that direction or invalidate the other tasks. Its supported severity is Minor.

# Other checks, counterevidence, and unresolved evidence

CFG construction and attribution. The compile/CFG script uses Clang and LLVM’s CFG pass, consistent with the stated ground-truth strategy. Figure 4 is an example of model output, not explicitly a certified correct CFG: its one-basic-block explanation followed by three DOT nodes is not treated as proof of a wrong golden graph. The standalone graph-similarity script is a demonstration, not a verified reconstruction of Table 2’s full evaluator. Node-label normalization, single-node density handling, loop counts based on metadata, and case-level scoring need the actual evaluation records. The causal claim that CoT improves CFG accuracy needs a within-model controlled comparison; cross-model results alone do not isolate prompting.

Decompilation. The distinction between producing text, compiling/running, and passing tests is scientifically meaningful. The read notebooks generate outputs and record processing, but do not supply the full historical re-execution scoring pipeline. No executed-code replication was attempted. Figure 5’s semantic mismatch is qualitatively plausible; a global claim that every reported successful reconstruction preserves all program semantics would require more than finitely many tests.

Summarization and completion. Table 4’s best average for DeepSeek is conditional on 49 completed cases, while GPT-4 completes 164. It supports a conditional-score statement, not an unqualified comparison on a common population. For illustration only, assigning zero to the uncompleted DeepSeek cases changes a conditional BLEU of \(0.42\) to \(0.42(49/164)=0.12549\), while GPT-4’s \(0.35\) is unchanged. This is not a replacement metric or an assertion that the missing outputs should scientifically receive zero; it demonstrates why the completion policy matters. A common-case comparison, coverage-aware reporting, and independent semantic judgment are needed to interpret score rankings. Table 4’s DeepSeek metric tuple also does not reproduce a single displayed Appendix E run exactly; raw aggregation provenance is needed before diagnosing the cause.

Model and prompt identity. The retained notebooks name `deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B-free`, while tables use the broader label DeepSeek R1. They also contain specific temperatures and prompts whose exact historical relationship to every model/table is not documented. This is an unresolved mapping, not proof that an incorrectly named model produced every result. Current documentation gives additional illustrative scores that are not the proceedings tables. The snapshot is pinned for this audit, not asserted to be the experiment-time commit.

Source and version limits. Separate supplement availability was undetermined after the acquisition’s OpenReview HTTP 403 response; embedded appendices were available and read. Most result payloads are excluded from the supplied source snapshot; the single new JSONL does not restore them. Metadata identifies arXiv:2502.06854v2, 5 June 2025 [version]; no complete cross-version scientific audit is claimed. Novelty against all prior IR research was not exhaustively checked. Missing historical logs, oracle labels, exact checkpoints, evaluator code paths, and prompt-selection records remain Needs Evidence.
