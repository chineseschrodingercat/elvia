\# 3. Problem 1 - Absolute unacceptability does not erase relative preference\par
Source claim and location. Section 4.1, p. 5, Source 5 infers that when both candidates give harmful advice, there is no justified harmlessness ranking and an annotator without a both-bad option must select randomly. Source 6 makes a similar inference for two poor or inaccurate answers. Appendix A.1, p. 16, treats chosen-better, rejected-better, both-good and both-bad as four alternative labels.\par
The suspect step is the inference from two threshold failures to equality or incomparability. A binary pairwise preference asks which candidate is better according to a criterion. Absolute acceptability asks whether a candidate meets a threshold. These questions can have different answers without contradiction.\par
Complete counterexample. Fix one scalar quality criterion q, with larger values better, and an acceptability threshold tau = 0. Choose q(A) = -1 and q(B) = -2. Both candidates fail the threshold, while A strictly outranks B. An annotator who consistently follows this criterion can choose A on every repetition. No random decision is required. All quantities are finite, the criterion and threshold are fixed, and there is no hidden change in context or comparison standard.\par
\[q(A)=-1<0,\quad q(B)=-2<0,\qquad q(A)-q(B)=1>0.\] The distinction also holds under stochastic preference modeling. For the optional Bradley-Terry-Luce model with unit scale, the probability of selecting A is about 0.731, rather than 0.5. Adding any common constant to both qualities leaves this probability unchanged, while it can change whether the candidates meet a fixed absolute threshold. This is a mathematical illustration, not a fit to the paper’s annotations.\par
\[P(A\succ B)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(q(A)-q(B))]}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-1}}\approx0.731059.\] Primary protocol check. Bai et al., arXiv:2204.05862v1, §2.2 p. 11, describes relative helpfulness or harmfulness comparisons, preference-strength input and exclusion of the weakest comparisons/ties from retained data. Thus it does not mechanically turn every forced-looking pair into a retained arbitrary label. This does not establish that all retained labels are reliable; it narrows the target’s causal claim about the absence of a both-bad category. [S2]\par
Strongest alternative reading. The authors may intend equally poor, indistinguishable or incomparable candidates. Indeed, the cited historical Yeh v1 uses equal-poorness language in §3.2 p. 6. Under an actual tie, a separate tie/abstention option can be informative. Poor response candidates can also fail to teach acceptable behavior despite supplying a valid relative ranking. These interpretations preserve a useful concern, but require conditions not entailed by both candidates being bad. [S3]\par
Impact and repair. Judgment: Major. The abstract and introduction identify qualitative analysis of human-feedback unreliability as a contribution. On p. 5, Sources 5 and 6 use the invalid both-bad-to-arbitrary-preference inference to classify harmful or inaccurate response pairs as sources of unreliable feedback. Table 3 assigns these themes 39\% and 36\% of its both-bad column and uses that branch to argue that poor response quality triggers unreliable supervision. Absolute response quality does not establish that the original relative label lacks information. This breaks the inferential interpretation of a substantial branch of the empirical contribution; it does not establish that the recorded category counts, kappa or every pairwise label are false. Distinguish absolute acceptability from relative preference and tie or insufficient-evidence responses. Amend the causal interpretation and check the original annotation instructions and coding matrix before asserting which cases provide invalid relative labels. If both-bad was operationally defined as equally bad, supply that definition and analyze its use separately. The counterexample verifies the logical distinction; no corrected frequencies, improved agreement or downstream training effect is claimed. Broader feedback-quality recommendations survive. Adoption was not requested.\par

For a single code per item and denominator 40, every percentage is a multiple of 2.5. No integer count rounds to 39\% or 36\% under ordinary nearest-integer rounding. \# 5. Scope clarifications and negative checks\par
The 25\% reversed preference labels are disagreements with the original annotators, not independently established objective error rates. Three experienced lab members offer another judgment under an expanded label set. Table 2 itself preserves ambiguity in an example prompt. Likewise, kappa = 0.46 cannot be recalculated without the rating matrix; no inspected evidence independently disproves it. The closing paragraph on p. 5 expressly calls for work to establish how broadly the themes apply.\par
Section 3, p. 2, categorically describes algorithm-centered alignment as assuming perfect data. That framing should be narrowed: the paper itself cites Provably Robust DPO, whose method explicitly addresses random preference flips. The source assumes a specified noise model and known flip rate below one half; its guarantees require a log-linear policy class and feature coverage. It is a counterexample to the broad characterization, not a demonstration that algorithms solve arbitrary human disagreement. Classify this as a scope clarification supporting the paper’s own complementary framing. [S4]\par
The proposed four-option annotation is demonstrably more expressive, but the supplied analysis does not isolate a causal improvement in reliability against a matched two-option control. Similarly, data-cleaning recommendations and claims about smaller selected datasets are literature-based, context-dependent arguments. This review did not rerun those experiments or treat these recommendations as established universal guarantees.\par
