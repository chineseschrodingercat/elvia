Abstract: The normal–inverse-Wishart calculation gives different degrees of freedom from those stated for the FIV-BL approximation. The claimed ground-truth predictive collapse also fails with nonzero intrinsic return noise. These affect stated theoretical properties of the proposed models. A released implementation uses the predictive covariance in place of the posterior covariance when forming the mean; historical experimental provenance is unresolved. The table-level percentage summaries check out. The overall supported assessment is Major; adjudication remains pending.

# Background and positive checks

Equation reference: eq:slp. For Gaussian prior \(N(\theta_0,\Sigma_0)\) and a Gaussian feature measurement centered at \(a=\alpha^F+F\beta^F\), completing the square gives \[
 V=(\Sigma_0^{-1}+(\Omega^F)^{-1})^{-1},\quad
 b=\Sigma_0^{-1}\theta_0+(\Omega^F)^{-1}a,\quad
 E[\theta\mid F]=Vb.\] If \(r\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\Sigma)\) with conditionally independent return noise, then \(r\mid F\sim N(Vb,\Sigma+V)\). These are the formulas in Theorem 3.2 and Appendix E.3. The law of total variance justifies the addition of \(\Sigma\) to covariance, not to the mean multiplier. Theorem 3.1’s Gaussian quadratic calculation similarly requires \(\gamma^2 P^T\Omega^{-1}P\) in its precision.

The ten paired Sharpe ratios in Tables 1–2 yield a mean relative improvement of \(49.7545\%\); the ten paired turnover rates in Table 6 yield a mean reduction of \(55.0528\%\). Thus the abstract’s rounded \(49.8\%\) and \(55.1\%\) summaries are arithmetically supported. This check does not validate the underlying backtests or establish performance on new data.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The FIV-BL Student-\(t\) approximation has incorrect degrees of freedom).  The affected core approximation is Corollaries 3.3.1–3.3.2 (p. 8), supported by Lemma D.3 (p. 18) and Appendix E.5 (p. 23).

Equation reference: eq:tcorrect. The paper assumes \(\theta\mid S\sim N(\mu',S)\) and \(S\sim IW(\Psi',\nu')\), using in (D.8) the density proportional to \(|S|^{-(\nu'+m+1)/2}\exp[-\operatorname{tr}(\Psi'S^{-1})/2]\). Take \(\Psi'\) positive definite, \(\nu'>m-1\), and \(\mu'\) fixed, exactly as required for this approximate model. Integration gives, for \(u=\theta-\mu'\), \[\begin{aligned}
 p(\theta)&\propto\int |S|^{-(\nu'+m+2)/2}
 \exp[-\operatorname{tr}((\Psi'+uu^T)S^{-1})/2]\,dS\\
 &\propto|\Psi'+uu^T|^{-(\nu'+1)/2}
 \propto(1+u^T(\Psi')^{-1}u)^{-(\nu'+1)/2}.\end{aligned}\] The kernel of an \(m\)-variate \(t_\kappa(\mu',C)\) is \((1+u^TC^{-1}u/\kappa)^{-(\kappa+m)/2}\). Matching both factors forces \[
 \kappa=\nu'-m+1,\qquad C=\frac{\Psi'}{\nu'-m+1}.\] The paper instead specifies degrees of freedom \(\nu'\) explicitly and writes \(t_{\nu'}\) with the same scale matrix. Its intermediate \(\nu=\nu'-m+1\) does not justify changing the final subscript back to \(\nu'\). This is not an unspecified alternative \(t\) convention: both the density of \(IW\) and the claimed degrees of freedom are explicit.

For a concrete admissible example, set \(m=2,\nu'=5,\Psi'=I_2,\mu'=0\). The correct distribution is \(t_4(0,I_2/4)\) with covariance \(I_2/2\). The printed \(t_5(0,I_2/4)\) has covariance \(5I_2/12\). Their densities at \((1,0)\) are respectively \(0.07957747\) and \(0.08136289\), checked independently. Both distributions are well-defined; their inequality is substantive for uncertainty and tails.

Appendix A (p. 14) also calls \(\Psi'/(\nu'-m+1)\) the inverse-Wishart mean, with existence threshold \(\nu'>m-1\). Under its stated density, the mean is \(\Psi'/(\nu'-m-1)\) and requires \(\nu'>m+1\). This follows from the same mixture: \(E[S]=\operatorname{Var}(\theta)
=\kappa C/(\kappa-2)\). The displayed denominator is the \(t\) scale, not \(E[S]\). Equation [eq:tcorrect] (#eq:tcorrect) repairs the marginal law of the stipulated approximate hierarchy, and the corrected mean repairs that moment only. Neither proves the hierarchy is a faithful approximation of the original \(\Omega\) mixture. The empirical study uses SLP-BL, so these errors do not directly invalidate its reported scores. They do invalidate a core FIV-BL predictive derivation, supporting Major.

Problem 2 (Perfect views do not force the stated predictive point mass).  Remark 3.3 (p. 5), repeated in the conclusion (p. 9), states that \(\Omega\to0\), \(q\to Pr^*\) and \((\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0,0,1)\) imply \(\widetilde r\Rightarrow\delta_{r^*}\).

Use \(m=k=1\), \(P=1\), \(\Sigma=\Sigma_0=\Omega^F=1\), \(\theta_0=0\), feature center \(\alpha^F+F\beta^F=0\), \(r^*=q=2\), and \(\Omega=\varepsilon>0\). Every covariance is positive definite, the stated regression parameters are classical, views are accurate, and their uncertainty tends to zero. Theorem 3.1 gives \[\mu_\varepsilon=\frac2{1+2\varepsilon},\qquad
 V_\varepsilon=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2\varepsilon},\qquad
 \widetilde r\sim N(\mu_\varepsilon,1+V_\varepsilon).\] Consequently \(\widetilde r\Rightarrow N(2,1)\), not \(\delta_2\). For example, its limiting characteristic function at frequency one is \(e^{2i-1/2}\) rather than \(e^{2i}\), directly ruling out the claimed weak limit without relying on convergence of moments. The latent mean \(\theta\) does collapse in this scalar example; the future noisy return \(r\) does not.

The mean claim also needs an identifiability condition on \(P\). For \(m=2,k=1,P=(1,0)\), prior and feature precision both \(I_2\), their centers zero, and \(r^*=(0,1)^T,q=0\), the second posterior coordinate remains \(N(0,1/2)\) as \(\Omega\to0\). The theorem allows this rectangular \(P\). Appendix A’s practical choice \(P=I\) avoids this second obstruction but not the intrinsic-noise obstruction above.

Under full-column-rank \(P\) with \(\Omega=\varepsilon\Omega_1\), \(\Omega_1\succ0\), accurate views and bounded prior/feature terms, the repaired conclusion is \(\theta\Rightarrow\delta_{r^*}\) and \(\widetilde r\Rightarrow N(r^*,\Sigma)\). This follows because the posterior precision diverges in every parameter direction and its covariance tends to zero. A predictive point mass additionally requires \(\Sigma\to0\). This is a major defect in the asserted general recovery property; it does not refute ordinary Bayesian shrinkage or the separate empirical SLP-BL comparisons.

Problem 3 (Released code uses the predictive covariance to form the mean).  The supported release-level discrepancy is Minor; its effect on the historical paper results Needs Evidence.

At the pinned commit, `models/black_litterman.py` computes `middle_inv` \(=V\) and `adjusted_cov`\(=\Sigma+V\), then `adjusted_mean = adjusted_cov @ rhs`. The same expression occurs in `old_version/main.py`, lines 237–238. It therefore returns \((\Sigma+V)b\) rather than [eq:slp] (#eq:slp)’s \(Vb\).

For \(\Sigma=\Sigma_0=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\), \(\Omega^F=I\), \(\theta_0=0\), and \(a=(1,1)^T\), all matrices are positive definite and \(V=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,4/5)\). The correct mean is \((1/2,4/5)\); the released expression gives \((3/2,24/5)\). With predictive covariance \(C=\operatorname{diag}(3/2,24/5)\), the unconstrained positive Sharpe direction is \(C^{-1}\mu\); its normalized entries are already long-only. Thus the theoretical weights are \((2/3,1/3)\), versus \((1/2,1/2)\) for the released expression. This is an independent matrix calculation, not execution of the author’s program or a historical backtest.

Replacing the multiplier of `rhs` by `middle_inv` repairs this algebraic discrepancy. The repository describes itself as a refactor and includes an older file, but neither proves which exact code and inputs generated Tables 1–2. In addition, the code uses linear SVR while Appendix A derives Gaussian-regression estimators. A new documented backtest and parameter correspondence are needed to determine historical empirical consequences. No score degradation is inferred here.

Problem 4 (Two predictive corollaries are inconsistent with their antecedent posteriors).  These local formula defects are Minor, given the correct adjacent derivations and no demonstrated additional empirical dependency.

Corollary 3.1.1 omits \(\gamma^2\) from the precision retained by Theorem 3.1 and its proof; Corollary 3.2.1 prints \(G^F\) rather than \((G^F)^{-1}\) in the mean. For the first, take scalar \(P=\Sigma_0=\Omega^F=\Omega=1\), zero prior and feature centers, \(\alpha=\beta=0\), \(q=1\), and \(\gamma=2\). The correct precision is \(6\), giving mean \(1/3\) and latent variance \(1/6\); the corollary’s precision \(3\) gives mean \(2/3\) and latent variance \(1/3\). For the second, take \(\Sigma_0=\Omega^F=1\), \(\theta_0=0\) and feature center \(a=1\): \(G^F=2\) and \(b=1\), so the printed mean is \(2\) instead of \(1/2\). All variances are positive. Restoring the squared coefficient and inverse, respectively, recovers the completed-square identities. The first discrepancy disappears when \(\gamma=1\); neither formula alone establishes which implementation produced the experiments.

# Remaining qualifications and conclusion

The FIV mixture’s conditional-independence interpretation also needs a more explicit joint model: Figure 3 draws \(\theta\to q\leftarrow F\), while Appendix E.4 assumes \(\theta\perp F\mid(q,\Omega)\). That independence does not follow from the displayed graph. This remains a scoped Needs Evidence item; the explicit mixture can itself be adopted as a different model. No later erratum comparison was performed.
