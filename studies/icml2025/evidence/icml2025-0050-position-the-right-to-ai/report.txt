# 2. Checked claims and outcome

The paper proposes a governance right for people affected by AI, argues that AI functions as societal infrastructure, develops a four-tier participation framework, and uses nine cases to illustrate opportunities and constraints. The main normative premises are explicit. The paper also makes concrete empirical claims about what particular participatory projects accomplished; those claims require faithful attribution even when the broader governance recommendation is normative. [E1, pp. 1-9, 18-21]

Major is supported for the healthcare inference in Section 6 and Table 1. Correcting this case changes both the attributed intervention and the evidentiary role of its alleged outcomes. The failed link is part of the stated empirical synthesis supporting participation’s ability to mitigate bias. The general right to participate and the four-tier proposal need not be false for that inference to require substantial correction.

A second, bounded concern is Table 1’s attribution of faster resource allocation to Project Dorian. Its cited case description supports a narrower result and does not establish the operational endpoint asserted. Other checked cases provide substantive counterevidence against rejecting the entire synthesis.

# 3. Operative premises and distinctions

The consolidated argument is: AI increasingly functions as infrastructure; affected people have a moral or political entitlement to help govern consequential infrastructure; therefore an AI-specific participatory governance right is warranted. The second premise is normative rather than a consequence of data production alone. Conditional on these declared premises, the inference is coherent. Disagreement about their moral force is not a mathematical counterexample. [E1, pp. 18-19]

The four tiers distinguish consumer access, private-organization leadership, government control, and citizen control through agency, transparency, and inclusivity. They are explicitly nonexhaustive, allow expert/community hybrid governance, and describe an aspirational trajectory. No universal ordering of real systems by measured performance or formal optimization objective is supplied. The p. 2 word ’optimal’ is read in this qualified normative context, not upgraded into a proved effectiveness theorem. [E1, pp. 2, 5-6, 9]

For the case audit, identifying risks, consulting experts, including affected people in design, transferring decision authority, and demonstrating improved outcomes are distinct propositions. An assessment can motivate an intervention without showing that the intervention was implemented or caused a benefit. The checks below are documentary comparisons and logical dependency analysis, not clinical replication or mathematical counterexamples.

# 4. Problem 1: the healthcare case does not support its attributed participatory intervention and benefits

Source claim and location. Section 6, p. 6, presents a healthcare co-design case involving patients, clinicians, and ethicists, with enhanced accountability. Table 1, p. 7, specifies iterative design with patients and clinicians and lists reduced diagnostic bias and enhanced trust as achieved impacts. Appendix B(e.1), p. 20, again invokes Zicari et al. (2021) for collaborative diagnostic-bias tools. These are concrete claims about a cited case, not merely recommendations for future participation. [E1]

Decisive source comparison. The exact cited Frontiers article, DOI 10.3389/fhumd.2021.673104, describes Z-Inspection expert assessment. Its p. 9 states: ’Callers and patients have not been involved in the system design’. Dispatchers were consulted. Fairness concerns remain in p. 12 and issue E4, p. 16; issue E6, p. 17, discusses dispatcher distrust. Recommendation 3, p. 19, proposes future patient/caller involvement. The conclusions, pp. 21-22, describe possible benefits of assessment and transparency, rather than a measured patient-co-design intervention with the two attributed outcomes. [E2]

Complete check. Define P as patient participation in the reported system-design process, B as demonstrated reduction in diagnostic bias from that process, and T as demonstrated increase in trust from it. The reviewed case entry uses P together with B and T as empirical support for participatory healthcare benefits. The source comparison contradicts P and does not establish B or T. Thus the proposed evidentiary chain cannot be obtained from that study. No numerical effect is inferred, and an absence of demonstrated benefit is not converted into a claim that the intervention could never work.

The identity condition is satisfied: the bibliography gives this precise article and DOI, not a different patient co-design trial. The intervention condition is not rescued by changing ’patients’ to any expert or practitioner, because the reviewed paper specifically lists patients and distinguishes affected-community power from expert oversight. The outcome condition is not rescued by a recommendation or by identifying a bias: detecting a problem and showing its reduction are different results.

Alternative reading and counterevidence. A broad usage of ’participation’ could include professional consultation and ethical assessment, and those activities can be valuable. This would support a narrower account of the case. It cannot retain the specific patient-inclusive design claim or treat anticipated improvements as achieved diagnostic-bias and trust results. The source’s positive discussion of assessment is preserved; the review does not allege that the project lacked value.

Dependency and severity. The abstract explicitly advertises nine-case analysis and the ability of grassroots methods to mitigate biased outcomes. Section 6 presents these cases as empirical insights, and Table 1 makes the healthcare intervention/outcome link explicit. Correcting the account removes that claimed successful healthcare demonstration from the synthesis. This is substantive empirical support tied to a stated contribution, so Major is appropriate under the requested standard. It is not merely an incorrect label, and its severity does not depend on overturning the normative proposal or every other case.

Scoped repair. Recast the entry as an expert assessment identifying unmet participation needs, and remove the unsupported achieved-outcome attribution. Alternatively, provide a different, precisely identified study that demonstrates the intervention and benefits claimed. The first option is supported as a narrowing of the evidentiary claim; it does not restore the original successful-case inference. No replacement evidence was supplied, manuscript edited, or author adoption obtained.

# 5. Problem 2: Project Dorian’s achieved allocation-speed outcome is unsupported

Source claim and location. Table 1, p. 7, describes Project Dorian’s key impact as faster resource allocation. The cited source is Berditchevskaia et al. (2021). The question is whether the case description supports an achieved operational effect, not whether faster response would be desirable. [E1]

Primary-source check. Nesta’s case study, printed pp. 18-19, labels Dorian a one-off research proof of concept. Faster response appears as a possible benefit of remote damage assessment. Its impact paragraph reports classification errors identified by community responders, targeted improvements, and better understanding of potential tool value. It does not report an allocation-time comparison or demonstrated acceleration of resource delivery. [E3]

Invalid step and limit. The case supports participation in validating damage classifications. Turning that into a completed resource-allocation speed benefit changes the endpoint and evidentiary status. Error identification can plausibly help later operations, but it does not establish that resource allocation was faster. This review does not conclude that acceleration failed to occur; the attributed outcome is unsupported by the cited account.

Alternative reading, impact, and repair. ’Facilitated’ could be intended as potential usefulness. If so, the table should state that scope. Minor is supported for this local outcome overstatement: the participation process itself remains substantiated, while the specific operational endpoint is not used to derive the four-tier framework or the healthcare bias claim. A supported replacement is that responder validation identified classification weaknesses and informed targeted improvements. Direct operational evidence would be needed to retain the stronger endpoint. No such measurement or author adoption occurred in this review.

# 6. Counterevidence and coverage of the nine cases

WeBuildAI. The original study describes actual stakeholder design and historical-data evaluation against human allocation. Its Sections 9.2-9.3, pp. 23-25, support the reviewed paper’s direction of distributional changes toward higher-poverty, lower-income, and lower-food-access recipients; the primary study also reports participant perceptions. This is evidence for the bounded historical comparison, not a new estimate produced here. The main p. 6 account therefore remains supported. [E4]

MID-Space. The author-hosted paper supports workshops, community-selected criteria, sixteen annotators, and urban-visualization data. Its outcomes concern data and generated images. Appendix E uses a majority rule with random tie-breaking for conflicting annotations. These facts support the community-informed visualization example, but do not by themselves establish interpersonal mediation, increased trust, or transfer of planning authority. Section 6 also cites Sloane for the mediation clause; that separate support was not fully audited, so no demonstrated falsehood is assigned to that clause. [E5]

African-language translation. Nekoto et al.’s abstract and Section 4.2 support coverage exceeding thirty languages: forty-five benchmarks include English into thirty-two African languages plus other directions. The community-development account is also supported. The reviewed paper’s p. 8 assertion that external actors repackaged these efforts commercially is not documented in the inspected source; no claim that such appropriation never occurred is made. That historical assertion remains Needs Evidence. [E6]

The remaining four case entries were read in the reviewed paper but were not independently reconstructed from all of their original evidence. Their status must not be inferred from the healthcare finding.

# 7. Framework checks, legal context, and remaining limits

Several tempting objections are contradicted by the paper itself. Section 2.4, p. 3, says participation alone need not empower. Sections 5.4 and 8 allow expert participation and complementarity with state and market governance. Sections 6-7 discuss resource constraints, tokenism, fatigue, conflict, and local bias. The conclusion and Appendix B explicitly leave comparative evaluation and institutional feasibility to future work. These qualifications preclude treating the paper as claiming automatic benefit from any participation, expert exclusion, or completed validation of a universal ladder. [E1]

Collective data governance is an advocated institutional arrangement, not a demonstrated present legal ownership rule. Introduction p. 2 and Appendix A discuss reconciliation with private rights. The restaurant analogy in Appendix F illustrates a concern about agency; it is not an independent empirical proof of monopoly or universal preference manipulation.

A limited legal-source check found that the cited January 2025 order discusses revoked Executive Order 14110 and review of actions under it; this alone does not establish the Appendix overview’s specific claim that the 2022 Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights was rescinded. The latter attribution remains unresolved. The broad p. 2 internet-right statement also was not conclusively audited: the UN primary PDF retrieval was blocked. These are recorded as source/scope limits, not elevated to additional demonstrated findings or current legal advice. [E7]

No inference about clinical safety, model efficacy, or the universal effects of participation is made. No author software, training runs, participant data, or original analyses were executed. This review does not independently assess all cited philosophical works, reconstruct every national legal regime, or certify all nine case descriptions.
