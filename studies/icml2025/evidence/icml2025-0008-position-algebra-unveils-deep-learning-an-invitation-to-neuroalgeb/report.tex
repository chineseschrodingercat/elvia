\# 2. Problem 1 — a smallest-singular-value truncation is a saddle\par
Judgment: Minor, demonstrated mathematical error. Location: Appendix A, p.13, paragraph discussing the Eckart–Young–Schmidt theorem after Eq.(10). The text assigns maxima to truncations retaining the smallest singular values. This is false for distance on the smooth rank-constrained manifold, although that truncation can have the largest objective value among the finite list of stationary SVD candidates.\par
Use exactly the two-layer linear model of Eq.(8), with N0=N2=2 and bottleneck N1=1. Its function class M consists of real 2\(\times\)2 matrices of rank at most one. Let the target W=diag(2,1), which has distinct positive singular values, and take F0=diag(0,1), the truncation retaining the smaller singular value. F0 is a nonzero, smooth rank-one point. The squared Frobenius loss is L(F)=||F\(-\)W||\textasciicircum{}\{2\}, with L(F0)=4.\par
\[\begin{gathered} F_+(t)=\operatorname{diag}(0,1+t),\qquad L(F_+(t))=4+t^2,\\ v(\theta)=(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^{\mathsf T},\quad F_-(\theta)=v(\theta)v(\theta)^{\mathsf T},\\L(F_-(\theta))=4-2\sin^2\theta.\end{gathered}\] Both paths stay on the smooth rank-one locus for sufficiently small nonzero t or \(\theta\) and converge to F0. The first increases loss, while the second decreases it. Thus F0 is a saddle, not a local maximum. It is indeed stationary: the tangent space at F0 has zero (1,1) entry, and \(\nabla\)L(F0)=diag(\(-\)4,0) is normal to it. The same classification holds for unsquared Frobenius distance because the square root is strictly increasing and smooth at loss 4. Global maxima also cannot exist on this unbounded rank-constrained class.\par
Repair: state that the top-singular-value truncation is the unique generic minimizer and that other generic rank-r SVD truncations are saddles, or explicitly restrict a comparison to the finite candidate set when discussing its largest loss. Independent symbolic checks verified the two displayed path losses and rank constraint. This correction changes the toy example’s critical-point classification; it does not invalidate the usual best-approximation result or the stated generic Euclidean distance degree.\par
\# 3. Problem 2 — Theorem B.1 has an invalid finite-sample exponent as written\par
Judgment: Major, demonstrated counterexample under the literal signed-loss assumptions (calibrated from Minor). Location: Theorem B.1, p.14, Eqs.(12)-(13), with the rate consequence in Eq.(14) and p.15. The theorem assumes |ell|<=D and a common C-Lipschitz population error for every distribution, then asserts the displayed tail bound 2 N\_\{epsilon/(4C)\}(M) exp(-n epsilon\textasciicircum{}2/D\textasciicircum{}2). Section 3, p.3, defines a real-valued loss; nonnegativity is not an explicit hypothesis of B.1.\par
The core dependency is explicit: the abstract and Table 1 foreground sample complexity as a geometric connection; Section 4.1, p.5, provides the covering-number relation in Eq.(4) and directs statistical learnability to Appendix B. Theorem B.1 supplies the uniform population-versus-empirical deviation guarantee in Eq.(13); Eq.(14) and the following discussion on pp.14-15 turn that guarantee into a sample-size/ERM claim and combine it with Eq.(4). Thus the invalid finite-sample derivation participates directly in a core geometry-to-learning claim. Its being a recalled statistical result does not remove that dependency.\par
Complete admissible construction: take X=Y=R, ambient function space V=\{fc:fc(x)=c\}, metric d(fc,fc\(^{\prime}\))=|c\(-\)c\(^{\prime}\)|, and the compact polynomial model M=\{f1\}. Define h(z)=max(\(-\)1,min(1,z)) and \(\ell\)(a,b)=h(a)h(b). The loss is bounded in absolute value by 1. For every probability distribution \(\pi\), L\(\pi\)(fc)=h(c) E\(\pi\)[h(Y)] is 1-Lipschitz, so C=D=1 satisfies every displayed bound. Let \(\pi\) put mass 1/2 on each of (x,y)=(\(-\)1,\(-\)1) and (1,1); labels are the deterministic function y=x.\par
At f1, population loss is zero and sample losses are independent \(\pm\)1 variables. A singleton has covering number one at every positive radius. For n=20 and \(\varepsilon\)=1/2, the event in Eq.(13) is exactly that at most five or at least fifteen of the twenty signs are positive:\par
\[\begin{aligned}\Pr(|\overline Z|\ge\tfrac12)&=\frac{2\sum_{k=0}^{5}\binom{20}{k}}{2^{20}}=\frac{43400}{1048576}\approx0.0413894653,\\2e^{-20(1/2)^2}&=2e^{-5}\approx0.0134758940.\end{aligned}\] The actual probability exceeds the asserted upper bound: 43400/1048576 equals 5425/131072 exactly. The existing finite enumeration, independently confirmed by the controller, is not a simulation or an asymptotic approximation. A charitable interpretation that restricts B.1 to nonnegative losses excludes this example, but adds a hypothesis absent from its literal statement. The earlier conditional positive-definite-loss discussion on p.13 identifies the interpolation set with the zero empirical-loss locus; it does not state that condition as a hypothesis of B.1. This finding does not establish failure for every conventional squared-loss application, nor does excluding this example by a new assumption alone validate the published exponent.\par
A safe repair under the original absolute bound follows directly. An \(\varepsilon\)/(4C)-net changes the population-minus-empirical gap by at most \(\varepsilon\)/2, because both errors are C-Lipschitz. A gap of at least \(\varepsilon\) therefore implies a gap of at least \(\varepsilon\)/2 at some net point. Applying Hoeffding to losses in [\(-\)D,D] and taking a union bound yields:\par
\[\Pr\!\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal M}|\mathcal L_\pi(f)-\mathcal L_D(f)|\ge\varepsilon\right)\le2\mathcal N_{\varepsilon/(4C)}(\mathcal M)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/(8D^2)}.\] This repaired bound is not claimed optimal. The qualitative logarithmic-covering and inverse-square-tolerance dependence survives with corrected constants and tolerances; an ERM excess-risk guarantee additionally needs a uniform-deviation tolerance of half the desired excess risk. The false finite-sample confidence guarantee in Eq.(13), used in the stated statistical bridge, is the demonstrated core-related invalid derivation. This is more than a peripheral rounding error under the clarified standard, even though the qualitative rate is repairable.\par
Counterevidence and surviving claims limit the judgment: in this singleton model, ERM excess risk is identically zero, so the counterexample does not itself refute Eq.(14)’s qualitative ERM rate or demonstrate learning failure. The earlier generic noiseless interpolation discussion is a separate argument and is not refuted. The broader algebraic program and the qualitative geometry-to-complexity motivation survive these checks. Their survival and ease of correcting constants do not alone justify Minor for the false theorem actually used.\par
