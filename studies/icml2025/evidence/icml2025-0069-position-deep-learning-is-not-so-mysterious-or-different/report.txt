# Problem 1 | Inverse and spectral convention

## Claim, location and operative assumptions

Section 6, pp.8-9, describes X as n by d, explicitly enters d>n, writes w*=(X^T X)^(-1)X^T y for the minimum-norm interpolant, and calls parameter covariance the inverse Hessian. Appendix F, p.20, specifies alpha=10 for its effective dimension. The matrix in that inverse is singular in the stated regime.

\[\operatorname{rank}(X^\top X)\le n<d,\qquad \dim\ker(X^\top X)\ge d-n.\] This is a structural failure for every such X, including almost every draw under the stated feature model X_ij=y_i+epsilon_ij with Gaussian noise. A particular support-point matrix illustrates the algebra; the conclusion does not rely on the probability of that exact point.

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad y=\binom10,\quad \operatorname{spec}(X^\top X)=\{0,1,3\}.\]

## Complete check and precise algebraic repair

\[w_{\min}=X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}y=X^+y=(2/3,-1/3,1/3)^\top.\] Here Xw_min=y. Every interpolant is w_min+t(-1,-1,1), with squared norm 2/3+3t^2, minimized uniquely at t=0. Thus the minimum-norm proposition is true when the inverse is replaced by the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. References to growing eigenvalues must concern the nonzero spectrum, since the nullspace persists.

The covariance convention also matters. Define N_eff(A,alpha)=sum_i lambda_i(A)/(lambda_i(A)+alpha), and H=X^T X. At alpha=10, N_eff(H^+,10)=42/341. For C_lambda=(H+lambda I)^(-1), N_eff(C_0.1,10)=59/96 and its limit as lambda decreases to zero is 1+42/341. A null mode contributes zero in the first convention and one in the latter limit. Alpha in N_eff does not itself regularize H before inversion.

## Counterevidence, dependency and remaining scope

Maddox et al. (2020), Appendix B.1, Eq.(12), explicitly uses the pseudoinverse and rank-based identity. Its historical random_features_nb.ipynb uses least-squares and singular values, providing a coherent interpretation. This is evidence against treating the printed inverse as a demonstrated failure of the underlying minimum-norm result. The supported judgment is a bounded equation/convention defect, not a disproved generalization guarantee.

The 2020 notebook has different metric/scaling details and is not authenticated as the generator of the 2025 Figure 1(g). No experiment was run. A complete plot audit needs the actual script, covariance definition, normalization, numerical tolerance and plotted data. This separate provenance question remains Needs Evidence and does not raise the supported severity.

# Problem 2 | A false single-scale inequality

## Source claim and exact scope

Appendix C, p.19, displays Rademacher complexity bounded by c gamma sqrt(fat_gamma(H)/n), immediately before a generalization-bound expression; Table 1, p.20, repeats the latter comparison. As a bound on the real-valued class and empirical Rademacher complexity defined on the same page, the displayed inequality is false.

\[\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_S(H)=\mathbb E_\sigma\sup_{h\in H}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sigma_i h(x_i)\ \not\le\ c\gamma\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{fat}_\gamma(H)}{n}}\quad\text{in general}.\]

## Counterexample with every condition specified

Take any nonempty input domain, n=2 fixed sample inputs, H={h0,h1} with h0(x)=0 and h1(x)=1/2 for all x, and gamma=1/2. Both functions are bounded in [0,1], the class is finite and measurable, and the signs are independent uniform {-1,+1}. The sample inputs can also be drawn independently; their values do not change the calculation.

Using the standard margin definition in Alon et al. (1997), Definition 2.2, shattering even one point requires two available values separated by at least 2 gamma=1. The class has maximum gap 1/2. Hence fat_gamma(H)=0.

Signs (sigma1,sigma2) | Supremum over H

\((-1,-1)\) | \(0\)

\((-1,+1)\) | \(0\)

\((+1,-1)\) | \(0\)

\((+1,+1)\) | \(1/2\)

\[\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_S(H)=\frac{0+0+0+1/2}{4}=\frac18>0=c\,\frac12\sqrt{\frac02}\quad\text{for every finite }c.\]

## Alternative readings and effect on the paper

Changing the multiplicative constant or log convention cannot repair a zero right-hand side. A margin-loss bound or a scale-dependent entropy bound is a different statement and needs its own loss, accuracy/residual, scale and confidence conditions. The cited primary source explicitly makes scale relative to accuracy relevant; it does not justify discarding all within-scale variation.

Major is supported by the affected core dependency. Section 3.3 explicitly introduces the comparison of generalization frameworks and directs the reader to Appendix C and Table 1. The false raw-class inequality is the quantitative link offered for the fat-shattering certificate in that comparison; the counterexample invalidates this technical component, and no finite multiplicative constant repairs it. The direct refutation concerns the displayed complexity inequality, not every entry of Table 1 or all possible margin-loss bounds. A replacement requires a precisely stated and verified loss/scale/accuracy theorem. The independent Hoeffding/weighted-union proof in Appendix D and the minimum-norm argument survive. No empirical bound computation is declared false. No general replacement theorem has been verified; the correction status remains open.

Evidence: primary p.19 visual check; exact reviewer enumeration in independent_fat_check.py and independent_fat_check.json; Alon et al. (1997), pp.619-620. The numerical enumeration is a check of an analytic counterexample, not an empirical reproduction.

# Problem 3 | Weighted L2 does not enforce zeros

## Claim, objective and matched parameters

Section 2, pp.3-4, introduces f(x)=sum_{j=0}^J w_j x^j and penalty sum_j gamma^j w_j^2, gamma>1. It then asserts that for data on a straight line the preferred fit has all w_j=0 for j>=2 because this incurs the lowest penalty. The exact lowest-penalty assertion fails for finite weighted L2 regularization.

Set J=150 and gamma=2, the order and weighting used in Appendix F for Figure 1(a-c). Choose three distinct noiseless samples (x,y)=(-1/2,-1/2),(0,0),(1/2,1/2), all on y=x. Use the stated Gaussian data-fit term with any fixed sigma>0. The basis is the stated monomial basis from degree 0 through J=150. Both candidates set the intercept and all unused coefficients to zero, so penalizing or exempting the intercept does not change the comparison. This is a counterexample to the hypothetical straight-line assertion, not a reproduction of the plotted nonlinear data.

\[f_0(x)=x,\quad f_1(x)=\frac{64}{65}x+\frac{4}{65}x^3,\qquad w_j=0\text{ otherwise}.\]

## Complete fit and penalty comparison

At x=+/-1/2, x^3=x/4, so f1(x)=(64/65+1/65)x=x; at x=0 it is also exact. Both models therefore have identical predictions and identical Gaussian negative log likelihood at every training sample, for any fixed positive noise variance.

\[\Omega(f_0)=2,\qquad \Omega(f_1)=2\left(\frac{64}{65}\right)^2+8\left(\frac4{65}\right)^2=\frac{128}{65}=2-\frac2{65}<2.\] The competing polynomial is in the same J=150 hypothesis space and has strictly smaller total objective because its data fit is identical. Thus the all-zero higher-order interpolant cannot be the claimed minimizer, even before considering other coefficients or noninterpolating solutions.

## Alternative reading, repair and dependency

The weaker interpretation, that increasing weights discourage higher-order coefficients, is valid. A plotted curve can also look nearly linear without having zero cubic and higher coefficients. Neither interpretation makes the exact zero-coefficient statement true. Finite quadratic penalties favor distributed coefficients when monomials agree on the observed samples; they do not generally induce exact sparsity.

A precise supported correction is: order-dependent L2 penalties make high-order terms more expensive, but need not set them to zero or exactly recover the lowest-degree interpolant. The analytic example and exact arithmetic verify this correction’s scope. No claim is made that the corrected sentence proves better test risk.

Major is supported because the failed optimizer-selection step belongs to the paper’s core running example. The abstract and introduction identify simple models and soft inductive biases as central explanatory contributions. Section 2 derives exact straight-line preference from the finite weighted penalty; Sections 4 and 7 reuse this polynomial construction when explaining benign overfitting and the bias-variance discussion. The lower-objective, equal-fit competitor disproves the claimed selection step under its own assumptions. The broad existence of soft preferences and the independent countable-hypothesis theorem survive, but their survival does not repair this exact claim. No change in empirical scores is required for this judgment, and no such change is asserted. Figure 1 and Figure 6 performance were not independently reproduced. Evidence and executable reviewer-only replay are in `verification/0069/agent_math/independent_checks.py` and independent_results.json.
