Abstract: The full proceedings paper and appendices were checked, including the original figures, metric definitions, tabulated arithmetic, and available repository documentation. Two local presentation errors are confirmed: Figure 1 negates the conclusion of disjunctive syllogism, and appendix H misstates the minimum of a reported accuracy range. Both are Minor and neither refutes the main benchmark findings. The familiarity analysis has an unresolved statistical-unit problem because each city-level score is reused in 600 prompt pairs. The published significance cannot be independently certified without the analysis code and grouped observations. No author code or model inference was executed; adjudication is pending.

Author: AI-assisted technical review

Title: Technical Verification of RULEBREAKERS

# Paper, version, and scope

The reviewed paper is Jason Chan, Robert Gaizauskas, and Zhixue Zhao, RULEBREAKERS: Challenging LLMs at the Crossroads between Formal Logic and Human-like Reasoning, ICML 2025, identifier I25-43712 (0343) [paper]. All 30 PDF pages, including appendices A–N, were read. Page references are one-based PDF pages.

All eight figures were visually inspected, together with the relevant template and numerical tables. The public author repository at commit `0c782b4e8e14ca822b8c9636e4c4fa7f4d5e8f64`, dated 10 December 2025, contains a README and a CSV dataset, with no experimental implementation source in that tree. The README was read; the CSV was excluded from the acquired packet by its source-selection scope. The absence of code in this snapshot is not evidence about earlier or private code. Tokenization, actual model outputs, test implementation, and per-instance labels were consequently not reproduced.

# Design and independent checks

The benchmark pairs a knowledge-inconsistent scenario with a minimally changed counterpart. Its specified target behavior rejects a conclusion in a rulebreaker and accepts it in a non-rulebreaker. The main templates have forms \(P\to\neg Q,Q\vdash\neg P\) and \(P\lor Q,\neg Q\vdash P\). These are valid classical forms. In the rulebreaker interpretation, background knowledge relates the predicates and makes the stated premises jointly inconsistent. The authors explicitly distinguish their intended knowledge-informed response from classical entailment, including explosion (appendix I, page 25). Thus classical validity alone is not a counterexample to their operational labels. It also does not establish that every generated item has the stated human response; no representative human evaluation on this full benchmark is supplied.

The construction counts check: \[2(183)(6)(5)+2(91)(2)(5)=10{,}980+1{,}820=12{,}800.\] Adding one non-rulebreaker per rulebreaker gives 25,600 instances. If the reported 20-city exclusion removes precisely the stated geographical instances, there remain 11,600 instance pairs, or 232,000 individual prompts after ten phrasings. These are deductions from the described design, not a count of the unacquired CSV.

Equation reference: bounds. Let \(R,N\) denote correctness indicators on the two members of a pair. The paired score is \(\tau=\Pr(R=N=1)\); hence \[
 \max(0,\tau_R+\tau_N-1)\leq\tau\leq\min(\tau_R,\tau_N).\] All 28 condition/model triplets in Table 8 and the six complete triplets in Table 10 satisfy these bounds, allowing two-decimal rounding. A constant positive or negative answer gives paired accuracy zero; independent fair binary guesses give \(1/4\), as stated. More generally, independent guesses with positive-answer probability \(q\) give \(q(1-q)\leq1/4\). These properties substantiate the metric’s intended protection against a constant answer preference, not against all possible confounding.

Table 8’s reported Gemma gain is \(31.27-7.14=24.13\) percentage points. Table 10 shows CoT increasing GPT-3.5 paired accuracy from 50.80 to 65.36, a 14.56-point gain, whereas the two evaluated symbolic pipelines reduce it. Accordingly, the evidence should remain specific to those methods and configurations; it does not establish that every reasoning enhancement lowers paired accuracy. The paper does acknowledge heterogeneous responses and several counterexamples to its familiarity and attention hypotheses.

# Confirmed local findings

Problem 1 (The symbolic conclusion in Figure 1 has the wrong polarity).  The upper example on PDF page 1 shows “A or B” and “Not B” on the left, with “Not A” on the right.

Under the propositional interpretation used in the caption and surrounding text, set \(A\) true and \(B\) false. Both premises are true and the displayed conclusion \(\neg A\) is false. In fact, the only truth assignment satisfying the premises has \(A\) true, so the correct symbolic conclusion is \(A\). This calculation was independently enumerated. The lower natural-language panel’s conclusion, “Anne is in Stockholm”, also corresponds to \(A\), not \(\neg A\).

The body text on page 1 and the formal rule in Table 1/page 4 give the correct disjunctive syllogism. This counterevidence strongly favors an isolated diagram-label mistake over a mistaken experimental rule. The caption presents the top panel as rule application; if it were intended as a deliberately erroneous model output, that distinction would need to be stated. The supported assessment is Minor, confined to Figure 1. Changing the green conclusion bubble to “A” aligns it with the valid rule and the surrounding explanation. No dataset or model-result error follows from this label error.

Problem 2 (The reported minimum accuracy in appendix H disagrees with Table 8).  On page 23, the alternative-phrasing-plus-prefixed-premises result is described as ranging from 31.27 (Gemma) to 69.12 (Llama-3-8B).

The paired-accuracy column for that condition in Table 8, page 24, is \[(28.72,44.66,69.12,56.19,40.09,31.27,31.92)\%.\] Its minimum is 28.72% for Phi-3-mini, not 31.27%. The correct range is therefore 28.72–69.12%, assuming the table is authoritative. The prose refers to all models and gives no restriction excluding Phi-3-mini. The error is Minor: it misidentifies the lowest-scoring model under this condition, while preserving the broader observation that the prompt change does not yield uniformly high performance. The raw observations were unavailable, so this is an internal prose–table inconsistency, not proof of which original measurement is correct.

# Unresolved inferential checks

## The city, rather than each duplicated score, is an essential dependence unit

Section 6, Table 3, page 8, defines familiarity by one first-country-token probability per city, then compares familiarity between correctly and incorrectly answered prompt pairs and reports Welch-test significance. Footnote 19 states that each city occurs in 600 pairs. Thus, if \(f_c\) is its familiarity and \(z_{cj}\) is correctness of pair \(j\), the reported means have the form \[\overline f_{+}=\frac{\sum_c\sum_j z_{cj} f_c}{\sum_c\sum_j z_{cj}},\qquad
 \overline f_{-}=\frac{\sum_c\sum_j (1-z_{cj}) f_c}{\sum_c\sum_j(1-z_{cj})}.\] These are valid descriptive, prompt-weighted means. They are not two samples of independent city-familiarity observations: identical \(f_c\) values recur, and a city can contribute to both groups. Unequal-variance Welch formulas alone do not address that dependence.

An independent synthetic calculation isolates the problem without claiming to recreate the authors’ data. Four city scores \((0.7,0.8,0.9,1.0)\) in one group and four \((0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9)\) in the other give Welch \(t=1.0954\) and two-sided \(p=0.3153\). Repeating every value 600 times leaves the mean difference at 0.1 but makes a naive observation-level Welch calculation give \(t=30.9774\) and \(p\simeq3.12\times10^{-192}\). No new independent information was added. This demonstrates the failure of treating repeats as independent, not that Table 3’s actual association is absent.

The paper does not provide test sample sizes, degrees of freedom, or a city-cluster procedure, and the inspected public tree supplies no analysis implementation. An unreported city-level or cluster-aware calculation could resolve the concern. Consequently this remains an unresolved evidence gap, not a confirmed claim that the authors executed the naive test or that their corrected \(p\) values would be nonsignificant. A suitable reanalysis would preserve all variants of a city together, state the target population and weighting, and use an appropriate city-level model or cluster resampling. The same audit is needed before interpreting the accuracy confidence intervals as generalization uncertainty; their construction is not specified here.

## Other limits on interpretation

The confidence means in equations (5)–(6) condition on the model giving a positive answer, selecting different subsets. They are not an unconditional within-pair discrimination statistic. The first-token familiarity score is a proxy for full country knowledge, not the probability of the full country name. Without token mappings and probabilities, neither proxy’s calibration was verified. Attention ratios and neuron activation counts are observational, model-aggregated summaries; the available analysis does not isolate a causal mechanism. Appendix N sums positive activations over token positions, so differing prompt lengths can affect the counts. No claim that this explains the observed curves was established.

Appendix A reports an informal annotation and cites small prior human studies (Table 5); it does not provide a human baseline for this dataset’s full range of verbs and phrasings. This restricts generalizations about actual human behavior but does not by itself falsify the defined task. The prompt variants, generated-conclusion task, and explicit knowledge reminders in appendices H–I are useful counterevidence against attributing all behavior merely to one wording. The unparsed response categories are disclosed rather than silently labeled as correct. Parser behavior, output-length effects, labels for every generated example, symbolic-solver execution, and the cited studies themselves remain unverified at the raw-data level.

# Conclusion

The two established defects warrant a Minor assessment and bounded corrections. The main score definitions and checked numerical relationships survive. The familiarity significance requires clarification or grouped reanalysis, but the evidence available here does not support a Major scientific allegation or a claim that historical experiments were corrupted. Later corrections and author adoption are unknown. This initial review is pending adjudication and is not independent acceptance.
