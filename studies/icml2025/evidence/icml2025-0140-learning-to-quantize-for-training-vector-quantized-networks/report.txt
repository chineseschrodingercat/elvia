Abstract: The inspected finite-difference hypergradient derivation is inconsistent with the specified straight-through and stop-gradient rules. A scalar quantizer with two strictly separated codes gives an unrolled automatic-differentiation gradient of \(0.4\), whereas the paper’s finite-difference expression gives \(0.576\) for every sufficiently small perturbation. The discrepancy does not involve a changing nearest-neighbor index or truncation error. It supports a Major finding about the central derivation and its interpretation as a bilevel hypergradient. The finite perturbation update may remain a useful task-aware heuristic; its empirical benefits were not reproduced or disproved.

# Necessary distinction between three derivatives

Let \(\operatorname{sg}(u)\) have numerical value \(u\) but zero assigned derivatives, as explicitly defined on p. 4. With a locally fixed selected code \(q\), the released implementation supplies the task loss with \[q_{\rm ST}=z_e+\operatorname{sg}(q-z_e).\] It also computes commitment and codebook losses with opposite stopped arguments, matching Equations (4)–(7). Thus numerical values, assigned backward derivatives, and derivatives of a freshly re-evaluated gradient field need not coincide.

For an ordinary twice continuously differentiable scalar objective, the chain rule and the central difference in Equations (12)–(16), p. 5, have the familiar justification. Here that justification must be established for the actual stop-gradient computation. In particular, a finite perturbation re-evaluates the numerical contents of stopped expressions, whereas differentiating their existing computational graph does not. This distinction is separate from the explicitly acknowledged one-step approximation to a fully solved inner problem.

# Problem 1: The finite difference does not approximate the stated unrolled hypergradient

Supported impact: Major. Equations (12)–(14) identify the one-step upper gradient with a direct term and two mixed-derivative products. Equations (15)–(16) approximate those products by differences of \(\nabla_\psi\mathcal L\) under perturbations of \(\phi\) and \(\theta\). Section 4.3 and Figure 2 then use these terms to explain how task feedback reaches the quantizer. The suspect step is applying this finite-difference identity to a gradient field containing the specified stop-gradient operations.

## Admissible parameters and unchanged quantization branch

Take one scalar training input and target \(x=y=0\), encoder \(F_\phi(x)=\phi\), and decoder \(G_\theta(z)=\theta z\). Let the two codes be \[C_\psi=(\psi,\psi+10).\] This is an affine hyper-net \(H_\psi(e)=e+\psi\) acting on embedding values \(0,10\). The other hyper-net or embedding coordinates can be held fixed when examining this parameter component. Affine reparameterization is within the method’s stated framework and its linear ablations. No claim is made that this small network is a benchmark architecture.

Use squared reconstruction loss, squared Euclidean commitment and codebook losses, \(\alpha=\beta=1\), and \[\phi=\theta=\psi=1,\qquad \xi=\tfrac1{10}.\] These positive loss weights and learning rate are not excluded by the derivation. At the initial point the code \(q=\psi=1\) is uniquely nearest; after the inner step the encoder value will be \(4/5\), still uniquely nearest to that code. All perturbations below remain in the same cell: the other code is \(11\), and the assignment boundary is at \(6\). There is no argmin tie or boundary crossing.

Equation reference: loss, field. In this branch the actual forward graph is \[
 \mathcal L=
  \bigl[\theta\{\phi+\operatorname{sg}(\psi-\phi)\}\bigr]^2
 +\alpha\{\phi-\operatorname{sg}(\psi)\}^2
 +\beta\{\psi-\operatorname{sg}(\phi)\}^2 .\] The assigned first-order gradients, evaluated numerically at general points of this branch, are \[
 g_\phi=2\theta^2\psi+2\alpha(\phi-\psi),\qquad
 g_\theta=2\theta\psi^2,\qquad
 g_\psi=2\beta(\psi-\phi).\] Equation [field] (#field) describes their values after reevaluation. It is not permission to erase stop-gradient nodes when taking higher derivatives.

## Literal unrolled differentiation

At the initial point, \(g_\phi=g_\theta=2\), so \(\phi'=\theta'=4/5\). When the graph [loss] (#loss) is differentiated again, both \[D_\psi^{\rm AD}g_\phi=0,\qquad D_\psi^{\rm AD}g_\theta=0.\] All paths from the reconstruction or commitment term to \(\psi\) are stopped; the codebook term has no backward path to \(\phi,\theta\). A fully explicit way to verify this is to replace the three stopped expressions in the local graph by frozen symbols \(u,v,w\): \[\widetilde{\mathcal L}
   =[\theta(\phi+u)]^2+\alpha(\phi-v)^2+\beta(\psi-w)^2.\] Then \(\partial_\psi\partial_\phi\widetilde{\mathcal L}
=\partial_\psi\partial_\theta\widetilde{\mathcal L}=0\) identically. Consequently the inner updated parameters have zero assigned \(\psi\)-derivative. At the outer point, the direct codebook gradient is \[g_\psi^{\rm out}=2(1-\tfrac45)=\tfrac25,\] and the complete literal unrolled gradient is also \(\boxed{2/5=0.4}\).

## The paper’s central difference

Equation reference: fd. At the outer point, the two directions prescribed below Equation (16) are \[v_\phi=g_\phi(\phi',\theta',\psi)
       =2(\tfrac45)^2+2(\tfrac45-1)=\tfrac{22}{25},
 \qquad
 v_\theta=g_\theta(\phi',\theta',\psi)=\tfrac85.\] Recomputing the codebook gradient at \(\phi^\pm=1\pm\epsilon v_\phi\) gives \[\frac{g_\psi(\phi^+,\theta,\psi)
           -g_\psi(\phi^-,\theta,\psi)}{2\epsilon}
       =-2v_\phi=-\frac{44}{25}.\] The corresponding \(\theta\)-difference is zero. Therefore the finite-difference hypergradient in Equation (16) is exactly \[
 \frac25-\frac1{10}\left(-\frac{44}{25}\right)
   =\boxed{\frac{72}{125}=0.576}.\] This holds for every sufficiently small nonzero \(\epsilon\), not just a poorly chosen perturbation size. The difference \(22/125=0.176\) from the stated graph derivative does not tend to zero. The linearity of \(g_\psi\) makes the finite difference exact for its own, different target.

Reviewer-written exact rational calculations checked \(\epsilon=10^{-2},10^{-4},10^{-6}\) and the frozen-symbol mixed derivatives. An independent scalar differentiation implementation propagated values, gradients, and Hessians with zero derivatives at stop-gradient nodes. It gave the same \(0.4\) unrolled result. With effective weights \(\alpha=\beta=0.1\), matching the quantization weighting style in the released FFHQ configuration, the two results remain different: \(0.04\) versus \(0.0648\). These checks are in and its JSON/stdout outputs.

## Alternative interpretations and counterevidence

One could instead differentiate the freshly recomputed numerical gradient field [field] (#field) to describe how actual inner updates change with \(\psi\). That also does not justify the transpose used in Equation (16). At the stated point with unit weights, \[\frac{d(g_\phi,g_\theta)}{d\psi}=(0,4),\qquad
 \frac{d g_\psi}{d(\phi,\theta)}=(-2,0).\] These are not transposes of one another. The stopped backward field is not the gradient of a common smooth scalar potential across reevaluations, so symmetry of mixed partials cannot be imported.

For further separation of the objects, the ordinary numerical loss after the one-step inner update has \[\phi'(\psi)=\tfrac45,\quad \theta'(\psi)=1-\tfrac15\psi^2,\quad
 V(\psi)=\{(1-\tfrac15\psi^2)\psi\}^2+
                2(\tfrac45-\psi)^2 .\] It satisfies \(V'(1)=36/25=1.44\), also not [fd] (#fd). This is not an assertion that STE must equal an ordinary forward derivative; STE is deliberately a surrogate. It shows why a precise choice of differentiated object is essential.

The finite perturbation update does contain task information through \(v_\phi\); it can therefore be a task-aware heuristic with nonzero indirect-update plots. The finding does not say that this update has no useful feedback, nor that the method cannot improve reconstruction. A different explicit smooth surrogate, or a separately defined higher-order estimator, may support a valid derivation. Neither was specified in the inspected argument to restore the asserted identity. No complete corrective objective or training algorithm is certified here.

# Source correspondence and bounded consequences

The relevant released operations are in `models/models_vq.py`, lines 100–105: discrete nearest-code selection, the two oppositely detached squared losses, and the STE. Lines 115–120 add an optional entropy term; its default coefficient is zero, as used in the example. The hyper-net permits linear and MLP forms at lines 32–56. These facts corroborate the mathematical graph examined above.

The full training driver was read statically. It selects a Betty-based method using `hyper_gradient_type`, whose default is `darts` (`arguments.py`, lines 34–36), and connects generator and codebook problems at `training_vqgan.py`, lines 421–426. The externally provided Betty implementation was not staged or pinned by the unversioned requirements list. Accordingly this report evaluates the paper’s explicit Equation (16), not an unobserved library runtime or historical job.

Other current-release details require provenance clarification: the codebook optimizer is SGD at lines 389–392 of the training driver, whereas the paper’s experiment descriptions discuss Adam; the shared reconstruction routine in `utils.py`, line 91, computes absolute error, whereas the VQVAE experiment description specifies MSE. The staged release principally supplies an FFHQ MQGAN configuration. These observations do not establish what ran in the VQVAE studies or that published results are invalid. They remain Needs Evidence for reproduction and version correspondence, not additional graded Problems.

The failed derivative equivalence directly affects the central optimization construction, the claimed best-response interpretation in Section 4.3, and the meaning attached to indirect gradients in Section 5.5. This core dependence supports Major under the assigned standard, without requiring score degradation or a whole-paper refutation. The hyper-net representation, constant inference cost after materializing its codebook, and the possibility of empirical usefulness survive. Reported benchmark comparisons, their uncertainty, training histories, and cited baseline comparability were not independently reproduced.
