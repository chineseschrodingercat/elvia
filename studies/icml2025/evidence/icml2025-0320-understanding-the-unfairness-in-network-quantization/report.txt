Abstract: The principal quantization bound double-counts the scale, and its subgroup-Hessian argument is invalid at a minimum of the aggregate risk. Exact admissible examples also defeat the claimed general ordering of subgroup harm and the QAT bound with only a learning-rate remainder. These defects are Major for the theoretical explanation; the reported empirical associations and augmentation outcomes are assessed separately and were not reproduced.

# Definitions and dependence of the conclusions

For a symmetric range \([-w_{\max},w_{\max}]\), the paper uses \[s=\frac{w_{\max}}{2^{b-1}-1},\quad 0<s<1,\qquad
 q(w)=s\,\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\mathrm{round}(w/s),-2^{b-1},2^{b-1}-1\right).\] There is no clipping within this range. The empirical minimizer \(w^*\) minimizes the aggregate risk, not necessarily each subgroup risk \(L_a\). The paper defines \(G_a=L_a(q(w^*))-L_a(w^*)\) and \(\varphi=\max_{a,a'}|G_a-G_{a'}|\). A subgroup’s \(G_a\) can be negative. This loss-based metric is distinct from accuracy disparity.

The central theoretical explanation uses Eq. (4) to bound each \(G_a\) by a gradient norm and Hessian trace, then argues that subgroup sizes order these quantities and hence order quantization harm. The QAT comparison relies on an additional Taylor-expansion bound. The three concerns below affect these links individually; the empirical observations need not be false for the theoretical claims to fail.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The quantization scale and subgroup-curvature assumptions invalidate the core PTQ bound).  Equation (4), p. 4, asserts \(\|\Delta w\|\le\frac12\sqrt n\,s_{\max}^2\). Appendix A.1, p. 12, explicitly derives \(|\Delta w_i|\le s_i(s_i/2)\). These are the printed powers, confirmed in the page images. Theorem 4.1 consequently uses \(s_{\max}^2\) in its linear term and \(s_{\max}^4\) in its quadratic term.

Rounding \(w_i/s_i\) has error at most \(1/2\) in integer units. Multiplication by \(s_i\) therefore gives \(|\Delta w_i|\le s_i/2\), with only one scale factor. The correct norm bound is \(\frac12\sqrt{\sum_i s_i^2}\le\frac12\sqrt n\,s_{\max}\). The proof double-counts dequantization. This is not an extraction ambiguity: Appendix A.1 displays both factors and the resulting fourth powers.

#### A fixed admissible counterexample.

Take three parameters, \(w^*=(1,-1,1/3)\), and identical subgroup risks \[L_a(w)=\tfrac12(w_3-1/3)^2.\] This is a nonnegative, infinitely differentiable, convex risk, realizable by a linear prediction \(f_w(x)=3w_3\), class label \(1\), and squared loss \((f_w(x)-1)^2/18\). The first two coordinates are unused model weights setting the symmetric range; nonunique minimizers are allowed. Any positive group sizes give this same aggregate risk and \(w^*\) is a global minimizer. Its gradient is zero and Hessian trace is one.

For every even \(b\ge4\), put \(m=2^{b-1}-1\) and \(s=1/m\). Since \(m\equiv1\pmod3\), ordinary nearest rounding gives \(q(w_3^*)-w_3^*=-s/3\), without a tie or clipping. The other coordinates are exactly represented. Hence \[\|\Delta w^*\|=s/3,\qquad G_a=s^2/18,
 \qquad\hbox{Theorem 4.1's explicit bound}=3s^4/8.\] At \(b=4\), the actual norm is \(1/21\simeq0.04762\), versus the claimed \(\sqrt3/98\simeq0.01767\). The actual loss increase is \(1/882\), versus \(3/19208\). The quadratic Taylor remainder is identically zero. More generally, the actual \(s^2\) term cannot be absorbed into an \(O(s^3)\) remainder as even \(b\to\infty\) for this fixed problem. The executed rational check verifies the same mismatch at \(b=4,6,8,10,12\).

#### Fixing the powers alone is insufficient.

Appendix A.2, p. 13, additionally asserts that every subgroup Hessian is positive semidefinite because \(w^*\) is a local optimum. Only the aggregate risk has that optimality guarantee. For an explicit counterexample, take \(w^*=(1,-1,0,1/3)\), \(z=w_3\), \(u=w_4-1/3\), two equally sized groups, and \[L_A=(z^2-1)^2+u^2,\qquad L_B=3z^2+u^2.\] Both risks are nonnegative and smooth. Their average is \(z^4/2+z^2/2+u^2+1/2\), globally minimized at \(z=u=0\). Such risks can be realized with a group-dependent model and a common squared output loss. At the minimum, \(g_A=0\) and the active Hessian of \(L_A\) is \(\mathrm{diag}(-4,2)\), with trace \(-2\), although the aggregate Hessian is positive semidefinite. Quantization changes only \(u\) by \(-s/3\), giving \(G_A=s^2/9>0\) with zero remainder along this path. Both the printed trace bound \(-s^4\) and the power-corrected trace bound \(-s^2\) are negative. Thus the trace substitution fails independently of the scale error.

The same unstated sign condition appears when Appendix A.5 drops a subgroup trace in Lemma 4.4. For three parameters with only \(z=w_3\) active, equal-sized risks \((z^2-5)^2\) and \(11z^2\) have subgroup traces \(-20,22\), while the aggregate Hessian has maximum eigenvalue \(1\) at its global minimum. Lemma 4.4 bounds the second trace by \(n|D|\lambda_{\max}/|D_B|=6\), although it equals \(22\).

#### Repair and impact.

A valid local bound follows if each subgroup risk has a Hessian with Lipschitz constant \(M_a\) on the relevant segment. With \(r=\frac12\sqrt n\,s_{\max}\), Taylor’s theorem and norm inequalities give \[G_a\le r\|g_a\|+\tfrac12r^2\|H_a\|_{\mathrm{op}}+M_ar^3/6.\] The operator norm may be replaced by the trace if the subgroup Hessian is explicitly assumed positive semidefinite. Twice differentiability alone, as stated in Theorem 4.1, does not ensure a cubic Taylor remainder; the counterexamples above are smoother and do not depend on that additional deficiency. This establishes a bounded repair of the local inequality, not the paper’s subsequent fairness ordering. The defect is Major because the incorrect estimate and curvature step underlie the principal PTQ guarantee and are reused in QAT. A short correction to one exponent does not repair all these dependencies.

Problem 2 (Subgroup-size bounds do not establish the claimed ordering of fairness damage).  The discussion following Theorem 4.1, pp. 4–6, and Takeaways 1–2 infer greater quantization harm from larger gradient norms and Hessian traces, and infer these quantities’ inverse ordering by subgroup size. Valid upper bounds alone do not establish those orderings.

The weighted stationarity identity \(\sum_a |D_a|g_a=0\) and the triangle-inequality proof of Lemma 4.2 are valid. When exactly two groups exhaust the dataset, the gradient-norm ratio in Corollary 4.3 is also valid. The surrounding prose explicitly acknowledges this two-group condition; its omission from the corollary’s standalone wording should not obscure that valid special case. It does not extend to the five-, seven- or ten-group settings by selecting an arbitrary pair.

For a fully convex three-group example, let sizes be \((1,2,3)\) and let \(z=w_3-c\) with unused range-setting weights \((w_1,w_2)=(1,-1)\). Set \[L_A=\tfrac12z^2,\qquad L_B=(z-1)^2,\qquad
 L_C=\tfrac32(z+4/9)^2.\] At \(z=0\), the gradients are \((0,-2,4/3)\), so their size-weighted sum is zero. The aggregate curvature is \(7/3>0\), making this a global minimum. Subgroup Hessian traces are \((1,2,3)\): they increase with group size. The smallest group has gradient norm zero, less than the larger second group’s norm two. All risks are nonnegative quadratics. Thus neither generic inverse ordering follows from the stated ERM assumptions, even with positive semidefinite subgroup Hessians.

#### Loss direction and bit-width.

Even when the smaller group has both a larger gradient norm and larger curvature, its loss need not deteriorate more. With group sizes \((1,2)\), take \[L_A=\tfrac32(z+2/3)^2,\qquad L_B=\tfrac12(z-1)^2.\] At \(z=0\) the gradients are \(2,-1\), the Hessian traces are \(3,1\), and the aggregate risk is minimized there. For \(c=1/3\) and \(b=4\), quantization gives \(\delta=-1/21\), and \[G_A=2\delta+\tfrac32\delta^2=-9/98<0,
 \qquad G_B=-\delta+\tfrac12\delta^2=43/882>0.\] The supposedly more vulnerable group improves. The signed inner product \(\delta^{\top}g_a\) and directional curvature cannot be recovered from separate magnitudes. Nor is worsening \(\varphi\) with lower bit-width universal: for the same two risk shapes with \(c=1/7\), four-bit quantization is exact and \(\varphi_4=0\). Eight-bit quantization gives \(\delta=-1/889\) and \(\varphi_8=2666/790321>0\). These use the paper’s actual range and scale, not arbitrary perturbations.

#### Scope of the concern.

These examples refute the purported general implications about \(G_a\) and \(\varphi\); they do not estimate accuracy changes in the reported image datasets. Figures 1–2 and the artificial-imbalance experiments are empirical evidence for associations in those runs. They may survive after the theoretical claims are narrowed. This is Major because the missing implications are central to the advertised explanation of unfairness, independently of the incorrect numerical bound. A repair needs additional directional/distributional conditions or an explicitly empirical scope. The valid two-group stationarity relation alone is insufficient.

Problem 3 (The QAT theorem drops quantization remainders that do not vanish with the learning rate).  Theorem 5.1, Eq. (13), pp. 6–7, gives explicit gradient/trace terms plus only \(O(\eta^2)\). Appendix A.6 expands in both quantization errors and the learning rate, but does not retain the independent quantization remainder. This fails even for a convex, infinitely differentiable risk and identical batches/groups.

Take the same range-setting weights and \(w_t=(1,-1,c)\) with \(c=1/3\), all subgroup risks \(L_a=(w_3-c)^4\), and \(b=4\). These are nonnegative convex risks, realizable with a linear score and a fourth-power loss. The aggregate minimizer is \(w_t\); every group gradient and Hessian there is zero. Let \(r=s/3=1/21\). Initial quantization gives \(q(w_t)_3=c-r\). Under the paper’s Eq. (11), the next unquantized QAT coordinate is \[c-r+4\eta r^3.\] Appendix A.6 then requantizes it. For \(0<\eta<27/(8s^2)\), its displacement from \(c-r\) is less than half a quantization step, so it rounds back to \(c-r\). The unquantized comparator starts at the minimizer and remains at \(c\). Consequently, \[G_{t+1}(a)=r^4=1/194481>0\] for arbitrarily small positive \(\eta\), while every explicit term of Eq. (13) is zero. For fixed bit-width this positive constant is not \(O(\eta^2)\). The exact independently executed update/requantization checks confirm it for four decreasing learning rates; the analytic interval proves the limiting discrepancy. Correcting the powers of \(s\) does not change the zero gradient/trace terms.

#### Other proof limitations.

Equation (21d), p. 16, replaces batch gradients and Hessians by subgroup quantities using an approximation, although the preceding training setup selects a batch from the full dataset. Even sampling within a group does not give a deterministic norm identity: two sample gradients \(+1,-1\) have zero mean gradient but mean single-sample gradient norm one. No fluctuation bound is included. Moreover, the stated individual error bounds yield \(\frac12\|\Delta w_t+\Delta w_{t+1}\|^2\le\frac12ns_{\max}^4\) under the paper’s own erroneous scale convention, not the \(\frac18ns_{\max}^4\) substituted in Eq. (21e). These are additional derivation gaps, not separate empirical-failure claims.

If Eq. (13) is intended merely as a heuristic second-order approximation, the quartic example identifies a discarded term rather than an unexpected training failure; it still defeats the theorem as an upper bound with the displayed remainder. A corrected result must state a joint quantization/learning-rate regime or retain all mixed remainders and batch-control terms. No such general correction is verified here. This is Major because the invalid bound supplies the theoretical QAT/PTQ comparison. Even a valid larger upper bound for QAT would not itself prove larger realized subgroup disparities or larger \(\varphi\).

# Experimental checks, counterevidence and limits

All nine figures were inspected as rendered source pages; plot trends and labels were checked, without pretending to recover underlying arrays. Figure 1 shows the stated deterioration of the small Others group; Figure 2 shows the reported inverse association; Figure 3 and Tables 2–3 show improvements under augmentation in the prominent low-bit settings. Table 2’s ResNet50 UTK-Face four-bit \(\varphi\) values change from \(18.8\) to \(2.6/1.7\) under PTQ and from \(25.4\) to \(2.7/2.0\) under QAT for GT/RE. These are internally reported empirical outcomes, not invalidated measurements by the counterexamples.

The improvements are not universal even within the tables: Table 5’s VGG19 FER2013 sixteen-bit PTQ values increase from \(0.6\) to \(1.4/1.7\) under GT/RE. This qualifies the scope of the mitigation claim. Augmentation also changes the full-precision reference model substantially, so comparing excess-loss gaps against each new reference is not by itself an isolated measurement of quantization’s causal effect.

The appendix reports ten repeated runs and average results; it should not be described as reporting no replication. Raw runs, seeds, loss values, an error-bar definition and a Hessian-trace estimation procedure were unavailable for checking. The settings do not specify enough of the loss computation and quantization/training configuration to reconstruct the tabulated loss-based \(\varphi\) from the accuracy plots. No actual fairness metric was recomputed from model predictions.

Several local documentation inconsistencies remain: Appendix C.2 says ResNet50 while B.2 and figure captions identify ResNet18 for the imbalanced datasets; Table 4 lists 23,782 imbalanced MNIST training images while C.2 states 23,250. The listed FER2013 class counts sum to 28,706, versus the stated 28,708. The UTK-Face class counts do sum correctly to 18,964. Captions for Figures 5–9 repeatedly refer to gender classification and four groups despite the described object/digit/ethnicity/expression tasks; Figure 6(c) uses object-class labels on its MNIST bar chart. These may be copied labels or reporting mistakes, and no measured consequence is inferred without the underlying runs. They are not additional Major findings.

# Overall impact and calculation record

The Major assessment concerns demonstrated failures in the central theoretical chain: the scale/curvature bound, the inference from magnitudes and group sizes to actual harm, and the QAT remainder. Empirical associations and the practical usefulness of some augmentation settings may remain valid. No fabricated-data allegation, full empirical refutation, verified author correction or author adoption is asserted.

The accompanying `independent_check.py` and `independent_check_output.json` record every explicit example above, the exact quantizer, rational outputs, symbolic Hessians, parameter values and runtime. The proofs specify why the hypotheses hold and why finite calculations support, rather than replace, the universal counterarguments. The norm repair is established only under its newly stated regularity/curvature assumptions; no repaired universal fairness-ordering theorem is claimed.
