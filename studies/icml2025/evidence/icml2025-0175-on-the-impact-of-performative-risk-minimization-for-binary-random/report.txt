Abstract: The finite-sample high-feedback derivation assigns a binomial tie to the opposite branch from the estimator explicitly defined in its proof. For an admissible two-sample example, this changes the expected risk from \(9/128\) to \(15/128\) and reverses both reported impact signs. This is a Major error in the exact finite-sample analysis under the stated tie rule; a consistent alternative tie rule restores the printed formulas. Three separate Minor concerns concern a main-text formula already correct in the appendix, a released uncertainty-band scale, and use of a true unknown parameter in a released exploratory policy. The full-information framework and the qualitative possibility of performative side effects are not refuted. Scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Model and checked baseline

Equation reference: eq:risk. The observation \(z\) takes values \(\{-1/2,1/2\}\), with mean \(p\in[-1/2,1/2]\). Predictions lie in the same interval. The response is \[p_{t+1}=\alpha\theta_t+(1-|\alpha|)
 \{\lambda p_t+(1-\lambda)\pi\},
 \quad -1<\alpha<1,\quad 0\leq\lambda<1.\] In the one-period slow-deployment experiment with \(p_0=\pi\), the test mean conditional on the prediction is \(p_1=\alpha\theta+(1-|\alpha|)p_0\), so the exact risk is \[
 R=(1-2\alpha)\mathbb E\theta^2
   -2(1-|\alpha|)p_0\mathbb E\theta+\frac14.\] This follows directly from \(z^2=1/4\) and agrees with the paper’s Appendix B.4. Its error–uncertainty decomposition is also valid.

For \(\alpha<1/2\), minimizing the deterministic quadratic gives the clipped formula in Proposition 4.1. For \(\alpha>1/2\), a zero sample mean makes the two endpoints equally optimal for the plug-in problem; which endpoint is selected must be specified when calculating moments and impact. For the two-period rapid case, backward substitution gives \(\theta_1=p_1\) and a quadratic in \(\theta_0\) with coefficient \(1-\gamma\alpha^2>0\), supporting Proposition 4.2. These analytic checks do not depend on the disputed binomial boundary.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The binomial boundary changes the estimator whose moments are computed).  The high-feedback branch of Lemma B.1 and Theorem A.2 uses a different tie rule from the estimator explicitly defined in its proof.

## Source claim, domain, and suspect step

Equation reference: eq:positive, eq:source-moment. Section 4.2.1, page 5, defines the plug-in estimator using the empirical mean of \(m\) independent observations from \(D_0\), with \(p_0=\pi\). Appendix B.4, page 19, case (ii), explicitly defines, for \(\alpha\in[1/2,1)\), \[
 \widehat\theta_+=
 \begin{cases}
  1/2,&\overline p_0\geq0,\\
  -1/2,&\overline p_0<0.
 \end{cases}\] Let \(K=m(\overline p_0+1/2)\sim\mathrm{Bin}(m,1/2+p_0)\). The same page converts the strict event \(K<m/2\) to the inclusive binomial CDF \(F(m/2)=\Pr(K\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor)\), and consequently states \[
 \mathbb E\widehat\theta=\frac12-F(m/2).\] For even \(m\), this assigns \(K=m/2\) to the negative branch. The error propagates through Equation [eq:risk] (#eq:risk) into the \(\alpha\geq1/2\) risk branch of Theorem A.2, page 16, and into the bias and shift identities of Appendix A.3.1, page 15.

## Complete admissible counterexample

Set \[\alpha=\frac34,\qquad p_0=\pi=\frac14,\qquad
 \lambda=\frac15,\qquad m=2,\qquad T=1.\] All parameters are inside their stated domains. Any \(0<\gamma<1\), for example \(1/2\), is admissible and irrelevant for this one-period risk. The observations are independent with \(\Pr(z=1/2)=3/4\). Because \(p_0=\pi\), the response baseline is \(s_1=1/4\), independently of \(\lambda\). The complete sample-space calculation is \[\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 K&0&1&2\\ \hline
 \Pr(K)&1/16&6/16&9/16\\
 \overline p_0&-1/2&0&1/2\\
 \widehat\theta_+&-1/2&1/2&1/2\\
 p_1(\widehat\theta_+)&-5/16&7/16&7/16\\
 \mathbb E[(\widehat\theta_+-z)^2\mid K]&3/16&1/16&1/16
\end{array}\] Every induced mean remains in \([-1/2,1/2]\), so the test distributions are valid. The sample tie selects a legitimate endpoint minimizer; the positive tie rule is not an inadmissible policy. It follows exactly that \[\mathbb E\widehat\theta_+=\frac7{16},\qquad
 \mathbb E\widehat\theta_+^2=\frac14,\qquad
 R_+=\frac9{128}.\] In contrast, \(F(1)=7/16\), so Equation [eq:source-moment] (#eq:source-moment) gives \(1/16\), and Theorem A.2 gives \(15/128\). The bias and shift are, from the paper’s definitions, \[b=(1-\alpha)\mathbb E\theta-(1-|\alpha|)p_0,\qquad
 d=\alpha\mathbb E\theta-|\alpha|p_0.\] Thus the stated estimator has \((b,d)=(3/64,9/64)\), whereas the printed moment yields \((-3/64,-9/64)\). Both signs reverse. The independently written exact enumeration and its full table are saved in `verification/0175/independent_results.json`. The source’s inclusive inequality and CDF conversion were verified visually on page 19.

## Counterevidence and precise repair alternatives

For odd \(m\), \(K=m/2\) is impossible and the formulas agree. An exact \(m=3\) check at the same parameters confirms this. Moreover, the printed formulas are correct for the negative tie estimator \[\widehat\theta_-=
 \begin{cases}1/2,&\overline p_0>0,\\-1/2,&\overline p_0\leq0.\end{cases}\] The released imperfect-information notebook, source cell 1, calculates the printed moment; its cell 2 calculates the corresponding printed risk. This is evidence for a consistent alternative intended convention. It does not make that convention identical to Equation [eq:positive] (#eq:positive).

There are two exact repairs. If the stated positive tie rule is retained, replace Equation [eq:source-moment] (#eq:source-moment) by \[\mathbb E\widehat\theta_+
 =\frac12-\Pr(K<m/2)
 =\frac12-F\bigl(\lceil m/2\rceil-1\bigr).\] Equivalently, add \[q_m=\mathbf1_{\{m\ {\rm even}\}}
 \binom{m}{m/2}(1/2+p_0)^{m/2}(1/2-p_0)^{m/2}\] to the printed first moment. The second moment remains \(1/4\). The risk correction is \(-2(1-\alpha)p_0q_m\), and the bias and shift corrections are \((1-\alpha)q_m\) and \(\alpha q_m\). Alternatively, explicitly define the estimator with the negative tie rule throughout; the printed formulas then apply. Both statements follow analytically by assigning the single tie event to its intended branch. Neither repair is an empirical rerun.

## Core dependence and assessment

The affected objects are the exact moments, risk, and impact measures for the proposed finite-sample estimator, one of the paper’s central contributions. The counterexample demonstrates a false derivation and changes the direction of both impact measures under the explicitly stated estimator. The supported impact is Major for this finite-sample result. That judgment does not require a failure of the full-information theory or of the broad claim that performative optimization can have side effects. An endpoint convention can repair the result, but it must be chosen consistently. The comparison does not establish which convention generated every historical plot.

Problem 2 (Theorem 4.3’s displayed risk omits the squared mean).  The main-text risk formula differs from the correct expression already given in the paper’s appendix.

Equation reference: eq:correct43. Theorem 4.3 on page 5 prints \[\frac14+\frac{(1-|\alpha|)^2}{1-2\alpha}
              \frac{1-4(m+1)p_0}{4m},\qquad \alpha\leq0.\] Theorem A.2, page 16, Appendix B.4, and the released notebook instead use \[
 \frac14+\frac{(1-|\alpha|)^2}{1-2\alpha}
       \left(\frac{1/4-p_0^2}{m}-p_0^2\right)
 =\frac14+\frac{(1-|\alpha|)^2}{1-2\alpha}
              \frac{1-4(m+1)p_0^2}{4m}.\] For \(\alpha=0\), \(p_0=\pi=1/4\), \(m=1\), the printed main expression is zero, while the exact risk is \(2(3/4)(1/4)=3/8\), as given by Equation [eq:correct43] (#eq:correct43). This discrepancy is not merely a stylistic spelling issue: it changes an expected loss. Both pages were visually inspected to exclude extraction errors.

The correct expression follows from \(\widehat\theta=(1-|\alpha|)\overline p_0/(1-2\alpha)\), \(\mathbb E\overline p_0=p_0\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\overline p_0)=(1/4-p_0^2)/m\). Independent exact enumeration agreed with the appendix expression in 27 selected cases, in addition to this analytic derivation. This is Minor: the same source already contains the correct general result and the released code uses it. The correction is to restore \(p_0^2\) in the main display. It is not presented as a failure of the appendix derivation or a new author-adopted correction.

Problem 3 (The released shift band uses the bias standard-deviation factor).  The plotting implementation scales the random mean shift by \(1-\alpha\) instead of \(|\alpha|\).

In the released notebook `c2-figures-2-3-imperfect-info.ipynb`, source cell 4, both the bias and shift band widths are set to \((1-\alpha)\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)}\). But for fixed true parameters the random shift is \(D=\alpha\widehat\theta-|\alpha|p_0\), whereas the random bias is \(B=(1-\alpha)\widehat\theta-(1-|\alpha|)p_0\). Therefore \[\operatorname{sd}(D)=|\alpha|\operatorname{sd}(\widehat\theta),
 \qquad
 \operatorname{sd}(B)=|1-\alpha|\operatorname{sd}(\widehat\theta).\] The released shift width is too large by \(7/3\) for the plotted \(\alpha=0.3\), and by \(7/2\) for \(\alpha=-0.4\), at nondegenerate points. These ratios were checked with exact rational arithmetic. For example, at \(\alpha=-0.4,p_0=0,m=10\), the correct shift standard deviation is \(0.4/(6\sqrt{10})\), while the released width is \(1.4/(6\sqrt{10})\).

Figure 2 and the page 5 discussion interpret the shaded bands as one-standard-deviation ranges; they are not 95% confidence intervals. The published image was inspected and is consistent in appearance with the released scaling, but no original plotting arrays or hash-bound generation receipt was available. Accordingly, the established Minor finding is a release implementation discrepancy. The exact historical figure-generation provenance remains Needs Evidence; no precise digitization or reproduction of the paper’s figure is claimed. Replacing the shift multiplier with \(|\alpha|\) restores the variance identity for fixed parameters. No new simulation is needed to prove that identity, although regenerating and checking the affected figure is remaining work.

Problem 4 (A released exploratory policy uses the true unknown feedback parameter).  The released one-period exploration routine’s action depends on the environment’s true \(\alpha\), despite its unknown-parameter setting.

Section 4.2.2 and Algorithm 1, page 25, describe learning the performative parameters from observations, with only \(\lambda=0\) assumed known. In the released `c1-figure-4-rl-exploration.ipynb`, source cell 4, the action-selection routine receives estimated \(\widehat\alpha,\widehat\pi\) and true \(\alpha,\pi\). For \(\widehat\alpha<1/2\), its unclipped action is \[\theta_{\rm release}
 =\frac{(1-|\alpha|)\widehat\pi}{1-2\widehat\alpha}.\] Cell 6 supplies the true environment value \(\alpha\) to that argument. The optimal action for the selected estimated model instead uses \[\theta_{\rm estimated}
 =\frac{(1-|\widehat\alpha|)\widehat\pi}{1-2\widehat\alpha}.\] For \(\alpha=3/20,\widehat\alpha=1/10,\widehat\pi=1/5\), both actions are interior, but the released expression gives \(17/80\) and the estimated-model expression gives \(9/40\). Holding the observations’ estimates fixed while changing the true unknown parameter changes the released action.

The true parameter is properly needed to simulate the response after the action; this does not justify its use inside the unknown-parameter decision rule. The discrepancy disappears when \(|\widehat\alpha|=|\alpha|\), so convergence to the same final point may still occur. The finite learning trajectory can nevertheless differ. This is a Minor static implementation finding: the release does not implement the declared information restriction. Its use in the historical Figure 4 run has not been established independently, and no claim is made that the reported convergence disappears after correction. Substituting \(|\widehat\alpha|\) in this numerator restores this local information constraint; rerunning the exploratory experiment would be needed to assess the numerical consequence.

# Overall impact and conclusion

The highest supported impact is Major because the finite-sample moment calculation for its explicitly defined estimator is wrong on an allowed event of positive probability, with a demonstrated effect on the paper’s principal impact metrics. The consistent negative-tie convention is substantive counterevidence: it recovers the printed formulas and is compatible with the released risk notebook. The report therefore identifies exactly what must be made consistent and does not reject all possible PRM estimators.

The binary risk identity, full-information one-period quadratic minimization away from the tie ambiguity, and two-period rapid calculation survive inspection. Four finite checks of bounded infinite-horizon paths gave necessary-Euler residuals below \(2\times10^{-17}\); this verifies those substitutions at the selected parameters, not a general sufficiency or boundary-regime theorem. The two code concerns remain separated from unproved historical experiment provenance.
