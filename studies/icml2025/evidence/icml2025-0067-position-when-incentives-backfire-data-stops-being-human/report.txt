# Problem 1 | Outcome timing in the reward example

## Claim and exact location

Section 5, p.4, paragraph headed Overjustification Effect, summarizes Lepper, Greene and Nisbett (1973). Its concluding sentence states that ’removing rewards can lead to a decline in both engagement and performance.’ The suspect step assigns a shared post-removal interpretation to two measurements made at different stages.

Primary-source endpoint | Measurement stage / locator | What the comparison supports

Voluntary drawing time | Later classroom observations; printed p.134 / PDF p.6; 7-14 days after the sessions | Subsequent interest under an unrewarded setting

Rated drawing quality | Experimental-session drawings; printed pp.133,135 / PDF pp.5,7 | Immediate performance under the experimental reward conditions

The quality ratings were 2.18, 2.85 and 2.69 for expected, unexpected and no-award groups, respectively. They were not post-withdrawal quality measurements. Reading the methods and results together therefore does not support attributing a measured later quality decline to reward removal. This is a documentary endpoint/timing check, not a new statistical reanalysis.

## Alternative reading and exact impact

The phrase ’These findings suggest’ can be read as proposing an extrapolation. Under that reading, post-removal performance decline is an untested possibility, not a reported result. The review does not claim that such a later effect is impossible or contradicted by other research. The problem is local evidentiary ambiguity in the illustrative example.

Minor is supported because the demonstrated defect changes the interpretation of this one endpoint, rather than invalidating a claimed estimated effect or a formal mechanism used elsewhere. The paper’s design proposal does not depend on a measured post-removal quality coefficient, and its Section 5 discussion explicitly permits beneficial, context-sensitive incentives. A stronger severity would require evidence of damage to the actual central recommendation or its principal empirical basis, which was not demonstrated.

## Verified correction of the attribution

Replace the closing inference with a distinction between immediate drawing quality and later voluntary engagement. Treat any effect on later performance as an extrapolation requiring separate support. The primary-source comparison verifies this narrow correction only; no intervention, empirical repair or author adoption was performed.

## Counterevidence retained

Section 2 explicitly accepts compensation. Section 5 recognizes informational rewards and calibrated incentives as potentially beneficial. Section 6 permits judicious LLM assistance. Sections 7.2 and 8.2 acknowledge feasibility and trust problems; Section 8.3 discusses bias, privacy and manipulation. Thus neither a universal claim that rewards are harmful nor a proven universal advantage of games is an accurate representation of this paper.

# Conclusion, references and evidence appendix

## Other central evidence

Veselovsky et al. (2025) supports prevalent LLM use in the studied crowdwork task and greater output homogeneity; it also reports better keyword-retention scores for LLM-labeled summaries. These findings concern different dimensions of quality. They do not contradict the reviewed paper’s contextual definition of quality and acknowledgement of useful AI assistance. They do not independently establish the entire proposed causal chain from incentive design through motivational crowding-out to LLM use.
