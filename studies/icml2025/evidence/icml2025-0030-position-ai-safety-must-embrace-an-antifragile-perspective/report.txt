Major — formal inevitability and fast-loop convergence claims require correction

Theorems 3.2–3.3 are false under the stated general MDP assumptions. Valid counterexamples undermine the formal route to universal inevitability, while leaving the broader case for adaptation and controlled stress-testing open. # 3. Problem 1: The cardinality argument does not prove inevitability

Major; high confidence in the counterexamples. Theorem 3.2 (p. 4) asserts zero robustness gap whenever there are two states or a one-step horizon. The two-state branch already fails. Use initial state s0, discount gamma=1/2 and the following stationary MDP:

State / action | Reward | Deterministic next state

s0 / stay | 1 | s0

s0 / exit | 0 | s1

s1 / either action | 4 | s1

Set u_plus(r)=r/(1+r) for nonnegative r and u_minus(r)=2r for negative r. These satisfy Appendix B.1: the positive branch is increasing and concave with derivative one at zero; the negative branch is increasing and convex with derivative two. All admissible probability distortions preserve 0 and 1, so P_dagger=P. An explicit Appendix-B-compatible pair is w_plus(p)=p+p(1-p)(1/2-p), w_minus(p)=p-p(1-p)(1/2-p), with a=b=1/2.

With the paper’s sum from reward index 0 through T=2, the true-optimal policy exits immediately and has value 3. In the perceived model, staying at s0 has values 0.5, 0.75 and 0.875 for one, two and three remaining reward slots, exceeding exit values 0, 0.4 and 0.6. Thus the perceived-optimal policy stays; its actual value is 1+1/2+1/4=1.75. The gap is 1.25, not zero. The alternate two-reward-slot convention gives gap 0.5, so horizon indexing does not rescue the claim. Scaling rewards by L>=1 gives gap 1.25L. This refutes the explicit zero-gap conclusion; it does not describe an observed negative-reward catastrophe.

Theorem 3.3 (p. 4) asserts that in any environment with at least three states and two steps, some perceived model induces catastrophic true suboptimality. Counterexample: three states s0, good and bad, with two actions having identical true consequences everywhere. At s0 both actions yield reward zero and go to bad with probability epsilon=0.01, otherwise good. Good is absorbing with reward zero; bad is absorbing with reward -1000. With gamma=1/2 and T=2, every policy has true value -7.5. Therefore every perceived-optimal policy has zero true policy gap, for every perceived model. Rare severe outcomes exist, but actions cannot alter them.

The cited Lee et al. source does not fill this gap: its Theorem 2 proof (PDF pp. 26–27) invokes zero intermediate rewards, absent from this paper’s general formulation; its Theorem 3 (p. 6) is an existence claim for some transition/reward functions. Its later same-policy value-discrepancy bound has further assumptions. These are not a universal policy-regret theorem.

Impact and repair: state count is neither necessary nor sufficient for positive policy regret. Replace the universal assertions with correctly restricted, proved claims; identify reward-timing, action-effect and policy-ranking assumptions. Distinguish existence of harmful misperception from inevitability in a specified deployment. Stronger eventual-occurrence conclusions need exposure/reachability conditions. The counterexamples validate the need for restriction, not a complete replacement theorem. The argument for continual monitoring can remain as a motivated position.

# 4. Problem 2: An average gap is assigned a cumulative-regret rate

Major; high confidence. Section 4.1, p. 6, defines the fast-loop average gap A_N=(1/N)sum_i Delta_i and then requires A_N=o(N), stating that this means near-optimality is eventually reached. A sequence with Delta_i=1 for every i has A_N=1 and A_N/N tending to zero. It satisfies the printed condition despite never improving.

A concrete witness is an initial state with actions yielding rewards one and zero, both entering a zero-reward absorbing state; always choosing zero gives gap one at every iteration. Repair the average condition to A_N=o(1), or use cumulative sum_i Delta_i=o(N). This repairs convergence of the average gap; convergence of the final iterate needs an additional argument. Definition 4.1 separately uses decreasing/increasing slow-loop average regret, so this finding does not render that entire definition vacuous.

Core dependency: Section 4.1 introduces the two-timescale regret framework and explicitly uses the fast-loop rate as the condition for reaching near-optimality within the current perception. For bounded persistent suboptimality the printed condition is automatically satisfied, so that optimization conclusion does not follow. This is a demonstrated failure of a formal implication attached to the proposed measurement framework, supporting Major independently of the Section 3 theorem failures. Definition 4.1’s trend criterion and the general case for adaptation can survive. Replacing the rate is a narrow repair; its ease does not reduce the severity of the failed implication.

# 5. Conditional corollary and rejected false positive

Visual inspection of original PDF pp. 4 and 16 confirms absolute-value bars in Delta_1 and Appendix C’s proof. Poppler text extraction omitted them. The apparent signed-gap contradiction was therefore rejected, and there is no basis to call the later arXiv text a correction of that nonexistent error.

> Let A=V_M(pi*), B=V_M(pi_dagger), a=V_Mdagger(pi*), b=V_Mdagger(pi_dagger). Delta=A-B=|A-B| >= |B-b|-|b-a|-|a-A| >= c-delta-epsilon.

True and perceived optimality justify the signs and the margin bound. Thus Corollary 3.4 is algebraically valid when all its stated hypotheses hold. The theorem-scope problem is that neither state count nor the existence of some misperception establishes those hypotheses in every environment.
