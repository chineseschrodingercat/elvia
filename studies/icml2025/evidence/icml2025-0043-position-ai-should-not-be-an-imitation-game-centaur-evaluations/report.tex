\section*{3. Problem 1: randomized incentives do not identify the claimed whole-pool derivative}
Source claim and exact location. Section 5.2, p6, proposes random treatments that alter compute use or encourage shorter human work, including early-finish bonuses, and then states that the design allows an unbiased estimate of f\_i(K,L) or its human-time derivative. Sections 4.3.1-4.3.2, p5, use that derivative to define human augmentation, compare systems and motivate policy-relevant quantities.\par
Target and assumptions. Fix the model, interface and K. Let U denote a worker drawn from the declared human pool. The economically relevant population response to setting work time to l is f(l)=E\_U[Y\_U(l)], keeping that pool fixed. A descriptive regression E[Y|L=l] is a different object when realized time depends on worker type. Random assignment of a bonus Z does not make every realized L a randomized intervention.\par
Counterexample. The pool contains types C and N in equal proportions. Assign Z in \{0,1\} independently of U, with equal probabilities. Type C responds to the bonus by reducing time from 2 to 1; type N always uses time 2. Work time l ranges over [1,2]. Consider two possible worlds:\par
\[\begin{gathered}
\Pr(U=C)=\Pr(U=N)=\tfrac12,\qquad Z\perp U,\\
L_C(0)=2,\quad L_C(1)=1,\quad L_N(0)=L_N(1)=2,\\
\begin{array}{c|cc}&Y_C(l)&Y_N(l)\\\hline A&l&l\\B&l&2\end{array}
\end{gathered}\]\par
Observed event (Z,L,Y) | Probability in A | Probability in B\par
\((0,2,2)\) | 1/2 | 1/2\par
\((1,1,1)\) | 1/4 | 1/4\par
\((1,2,2)\) | 1/4 | 1/4\par
Every operative design feature is satisfied. The human pool is prespecified and can be heterogeneous under Section 2.1. Assignment is genuinely randomized. All output response functions are smooth, nondecreasing and concave on the relevant interval. Exclusion holds: conditional on U and l, Z has no direct effect on Y. Time responses are monotone. The first stage is nonzero. No direct bonus effect, selection into treatment or noisy finite-sample accident is needed.\par
\[\Delta\mathbb E[L]=\Delta\mathbb E[Y]=-\tfrac12,\quad \frac{\Delta\mathbb E[Y]}{\Delta\mathbb E[L]}=1;\qquad f_A(l)=l,\ f_A'(l)=1,\quad f_B(l)=\tfrac12l+1,\ f_B'(l)=\tfrac12.\] Both worlds therefore generate exactly the same observable joint distribution for every sample size, but the target derivatives differ. Any statistic computed from those data has the same expectation in both worlds, so it cannot be unbiased for both targets. This is a nonidentification result, stronger than a complaint about sample size or a particular regression implementation.\par
The error can change an actual comparison: a reference system with f\_R(l)=3l/4 has derivative 3/4. The unobservably different worlds rank above and below that reference respectively. More generally, with a smaller positive fraction of responsive workers, the population derivative in World B can be arbitrarily smaller than the identified responsive-worker slope. The displayed equal-proportion construction alone is sufficient for the finding.\par
Alternative interpretation and counterevidence. Section 5.2 says that causal methods may be used and cites instrumental-variable work. That is a legitimate recommendation. Angrist, Imbens and Rubin’s 1996 abstract explicitly describes identification of the effect for compliers under its restrictions, rather than an unrestricted population response. Here the population Wald ratio correctly captures the slope for C. It does not reveal N’s unobserved response at time 1. The cited methods are not invalid, and randomized time-budget designs can answer useful questions.\par
Impact and severity. The unsupported step is upgrading a randomized encouragement result to an unbiased estimate of the whole-pool production function or derivative without identifying restrictions. The derivative is a central quantitative output proposed by this paper, so the invalid inference supports Major even though the normative case for more centaur evaluations remains intact. The proof does not assert that any published productivity experiment used the proposed design incorrectly.\par
Defensible correction. Name the assigned-policy effect or a precisely defined local responder effect, and state its identifying assumptions. If the desired target is instead a fixed-pool response to a controlled resource budget, randomize that budget with common population, interface and other resource settings, and report the resulting budget estimand. A two-budget mean contrast estimates a finite difference, not automatically an exact point derivative. Derivative identification requires suitable support and regularity or a justified response model. Even an identified IV ratio is not generally an unbiased finite-sample estimator. No complete new estimator or empirical repair is claimed.\par

\section*{4. Problem 2: the cited coding experiment has no AI-alone comparator}
Source claim and location. Section 2.2, p2, attributes the HTTP-server speed comparison in Peng et al. (2023) to a centaur outperforming both a machine-learning model and a human alone. The machine-only comparison is not part of that experiment.\par
Primary evidence. Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon and Demirer, arXiv:2302.06590v1, 13 February 2023, pp2-5, describe 95 recruited developers randomly assigned to Copilot access or no Copilot access (45 and 50 assignments). The task is implementing an HTTP server in JavaScript. The reported speed comparison is between those two human-participant groups; there is no AI-alone arm.\par
Alternative reading. The main sentence could be read loosely as describing an assisted system and an unassisted person. Its explicit machine-only comparator, however, adds a baseline the citation does not measure. The genuine assisted-human productivity result remains evidence that centaur evaluations are feasible and useful.\par
Impact and repair. Minor: this is a bounded attribution error in an illustrative example, without a demonstrated failure of the central normative position or the other cited empirical work. Restrict the statement to assisted versus unassisted developers. A claim of superiority to AI alone would require an appropriate additional comparison; that experiment was not performed in this audit. The same wording persists in the inspected Stanford author copy.\par
\section*{5. Clarifications and preserved counterevidence}
Economic first-order condition, p5. With K fixed and output already expressed in monetary units as the paper assumes, let profit be pi(L)=f\_i(K,L)-wL. At a differentiable interior optimum, pi’(L)=0 yields f\_\{i,L\}=w. The standard thought experiment is valid under those conditions. At L=0 only the one-sided inequality f\_\{i,L\}<=w need hold. No general-equilibrium wage forecast follows from a benchmark gradient alone; this is an applicability clarification, not an additional Major.\par
Definition 4.1 deliberately defines augmentation by marginal human-time contribution. It does not prove that every combined system beats every human-only or AI-only system. Resource units, population and operating range must be fixed when comparing gradients. The 15-versus-30-minute illustration is explicitly a finite-difference approximation; dividing by the time interval gives an average slope under a controlled design. It is not treated as a false exact derivative identity.\par
Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (2024), published pp2294-2295, distinguish two baselines. Against the better of a human or AI alone, pooled synergy is g=-0.23 (95\% CI -0.39 to -0.07). Against humans alone, pooled augmentation is g=0.64 (0.53 to 0.74). Neither is the paper’s marginal-time derivative. Section 6.1 presents a skeptical argument and then emphasizes task dependence; clarify those baselines rather than infer that the paper disproved or entirely misreported the meta-analysis. No raw meta-analysis was rerun.\par
The call-center example is supported by the inspected primary institutional publication summary of Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond’s Generative AI at Work: assistance improved productivity on average in that setting. It does not itself establish superiority to a fully autonomous call-center system or the labor-market consequences of universal deployment.\par
Sections 2-4 propose collaboration-specific evaluation affordances and an economic motivation. Sections 5-6 explicitly discuss costs, variability, human selection, interfaces, synthetic calibration and systems that can underperform standalone agents. Appendix A supplies proposed human/interface/scoring configurations, not new experimental results. These qualifications survive the findings. Long-run claims about technology, bargaining power and universal automation remain a motivating position, not empirically established guarantees from this paper.\par
Figure 2’s caption contains a model-label inconsistency with the plot/prose. It is a presentation-only issue and is excluded from substantive findings under this task’s instructions.\par
