Abstract: The printed level-to-score conversion reverses the declared quality direction. Public source supplies counterevidence to interpreting this as a proven historical experiment failure: its available level-weight helper uses the correct direction. The released correlation loss also differs from the displayed loss. Independent arithmetic checks support several headline counts and relative improvements, but identify a pretraining-count discrepancy. Dataset-level validity and historical benchmark rankings remain unverified. Scientific adjudication is pending.

# Problem 1: level weights reverse the intended ordering

Severity: Minor for the confirmed specification defect; empirical impact Needs Evidence. Equation (1), page 8, orders levels as \((\text{excellent},\text{good},\text{fair},\text{poor},\text{bad})\) and weights their normalized exponentials by \((1,2,3,4,5)\). Section 5.4 then selects the AGAV with the highest score. Appendix B.1 instead defines higher scores as higher quality, with excellent at 4–5 and bad at 0–1. These conventions conflict. For illustrative logits \(z_A=(8,0,0,0,0)\) and \(z_B=(0,0,0,0,8)\), the printed mapping gives \[S_A=1.0033501,\qquad S_B=4.9966499.\] Thus a confidently bad candidate defeats a confidently excellent one. For this level order the corrected weights are \((5,4,3,2,1)\), or any positive affine equivalent. Equivalently \(S_{\rm corrected}=6-S_{\rm printed}\). This reverses every strict ordering and the sign of rank correlation with a nonconstant quality target. The issue is not a small rounding difference. The text also calls \(X\) a probability distribution before exponentiating it; whether \(X\) means logits or probabilities should be made explicit, although either reading retains the direction defect.

Crucial counterevidence is present in the release: `wa5` in `videollama2_qwen2.py` uses \((1,0.75,0.5,0.25,0)\) for the same excellent-to-bad order, giving \(0.9991625\) and \(0.0008375\) in this example. It therefore has the correct direction. The top-level AGAV-Rater inference uses a regression head, not this helper; the code does not establish what every comparison model used historically. The defensible conclusion is a confirmed paper-formula error with a verified local repair. A claim that Tables 1–3 actually used reversed scores would require the missing baseline evaluation paths and raw outputs. No ranking reversal of the published methods is asserted.

# Problem 2: the released loss differs from equation (3)

Severity: Minor; release-to-description discrepancy, not a demonstrated failure of training. Appendix C.2 defines \(L=(1-\rho)/2\). The inspected Qwen2 `plcc_loss` instead standardizes prediction and target with population standard deviations, computes two mean-square losses divided by four, and averages them. Ignoring its \(10^{-8}\) numerical stabilizers and assuming nonconstant vectors, the first term is \((1-\rho)/2\) and the second is \((1-\rho^2)/4\). Hence the returned loss is \[L_{\rm release}=\frac{3-2\rho-\rho^2}{8}.\] At \(\rho=0\) the paper loss is \(0.5\) and the release loss is \(0.375\); their derivatives with respect to \(\rho\) are \(-1/2\) and \(-(1+\rho)/4\). Both favor \(\rho=1\), so the discrepancy alone does not refute the method. It changes optimization gradients and should be documented or corrected against the actual training revision. The released call reduces the tensor globally rather than specifying a dimension-wise reduction, which also needs historical clarification.

Both losses are invariant to a positive affine transformation of a single prediction vector, so correlation supervision alone does not determine calibration on the 0–100 MOS scale. For example, \(s=(10,20,30)\) and \(\hat s=2s+100\) have \(\rho=1\) and zero paper loss but RMSE \(120.2775\). This is a diagnostic of the objective, not a measurement of the model’s actual predictions. The normalization or calibration used for reported RMSE should be stated and tied to training/validation data. The inspected source uses a three-output linear head on the final hidden state; it does not by itself establish a numerical score-to-score causal chain among the three predictions.

# Arithmetic and interpretation checks

The dataset construction counts reconcile: \(386\times8=3088\), \(3088\times3=9264\) scalar MOS values and \(3088+294=3382\) AGAVs. The 9,264 figure is not the number of individual subject ratings. Fifteen subjects rating all 3,088 items in three dimensions would yield 138,960 raw scalar ratings before any exclusions. At most 60 videos per subject per day requires at least 52 rating days, consistent with the approximately two-month schedule stated in B.4.

The pretraining components printed in Section 4.1 do not reconcile with the total: \[25,592+19,000+6,000=50,592\ne50,952.\] The 360-pair difference is a Minor numerical/provenance defect. Either a component or the total should be corrected from the construction manifest; the acquired source does not resolve which. No particular missing training example is inferred.

Against the largest printed comparison SRCC for content consistency, the relative improvements are \(10.8941\%\), \(6.5562\%\) and \(3.2780\%\) for AGAVQA-MOS, TTA and TTM, agreeing with the rounded 11%, 7% and 3% claims. These are relative changes, not percentage-point changes or independently demonstrated statistical significance. Table 10 category counts sum to 230. Weighting its rounded accuracies gives \(0.8044783\); integer successes \((32,13,17,17,25,15,16,9,12,20,9)\) are compatible with those cells and sum to 185, or \(0.8043478\). This supports the table’s rounded 0.80, but is only a compatible reconstruction, not access to raw preference labels. It does not establish the distinct wording that 80% of ten people prefer the selected videos. The aggregation unit and tie rules need to be specified. Latency/throughput values in Table 9 agree approximately with reciprocal times; no hardware measurement was performed.

# Remaining limits and disposition

The 75 selection questions originate in demonstration pages where each source promotes its own VTA method. Manual verification is reported, but the raw judgments are unavailable here; third-party origin alone is not proof of an impartial sampling frame. The paper reports fivefold validation without sufficient information here to verify whether all audio variants of the same video stay in one fold. This is a need for a content-grouped split manifest, not an assertion that leakage occurred. Likewise an observed SRCC of 0.6860 between two quality dimensions (B.5) does not establish statistical independence. It can support discussing distinct, correlated constructs; it cannot prove their independence.

The three confirmed local defects are the reversed printed score mapping, the loss-description mismatch and the pretraining arithmetic discrepancy. No Major empirical failure is established in the available evidence. The score-map repair and loss algebra are verified by independent small calculations; retraining, benchmark regeneration and author adoption were not performed. Scientific adjudication and independent acceptance remain pending, with no human review recorded.
