# 2. Verified definitions and methodological context

The generator injects a K-bit message through 2K learned bit-position/state embeddings. Temporal modulation maps the selected K-by-H vectors into a time-by-H latent. The detector reshapes the shared embedding table into K-by-2H rows, projects keys and values to K-by-H, and uses audio-derived K-by-H queries. Eq. (2)’s attention matrix is K-by-K and its output is K-by-H. The declared products in Eqs. (2)-(3) are dimensionally compatible; no dimensional contradiction was found.

For the main experiments, Appendix A, p. 15, explicitly fixes K=16, permitting at most 65,536 distinct binary identifiers. Increasing the user-pool size N within that capacity need not increase the stored bit length. The p. 7 sentence suggesting that it does increase is an imprecise description, not a demonstrated failure of the fixed-16-bit experiment.

Detection is a binary watermark-presence task. Attribution is nearest-Hamming-distance message retrieval from a user pool. The main attribution metric is conditional on positive detection, while Table 5 separately reports joint detection-and-attribution accuracy. These are different quantities, and their explicit definitions prevent interpreting conditional attribution as success over every input.

The TF loss first selects original-audio masker tiles above 0.8 times the maximum magnitude in each frequency band. Each masker contributes a local threshold; the largest available threshold defines the global threshold at a target tile. If that threshold exceeds the target watermarked magnitude in dB, Eqs. (5)-(6) divide its squared magnitude error by a factor greater than one.

# 3. Needs Evidence: the masking-coefficient sign convention

Section 4.2, pp. 5-6, and the second contribution bullet on p. 2 describe an asymmetric TF masking construction with slower upward-frequency and forward-time decay. Eq. (4), visually checked in the publisher and later arXiv PDFs, takes an absolute value after adding signed offsets. With u=Delta m and v=Delta t, its decay is:

\[D(u,v)=\left|\alpha_f^+\max(0,u)+\alpha_f^-\min(0,u)+\beta_t^+\max(0,v)+\beta_t^-\min(0,v)\right|.\] Conditional failure under a nonnegative-magnitude interpretation: take alpha_f+=1, alpha_f-=2, beta_t+=0.5 and beta_t-=1 in the corresponding per-bin units. These positive choices satisfy both printed inequalities. Use an interior masker and a local neighborhood containing offsets (1,0) and (1,-1). Let its original magnitude be 1, giving a center level of 0 dB. At (1,0), D=1 and the threshold is -1 dB; at (1,-1), D=|1-1|=0 and the threshold returns to 0 dB. The target moves farther backward in time at fixed frequency offset, but its modeled masking becomes stronger.

Offset from masker | Printed Eq. (4) decay | Threshold for center magnitude 1

(0,0) | 0 dB | 0 dB

(1,0) | 1 dB | -1 dB

(1,-1) | 0 dB | 0 dB

Under that coefficient interpretation, the phenomenon is algebraic rather than a rounding issue. Along (u,v)=(1,-s), 0<=s<=1, the decay is |1-s|, which decreases as backward distance increases. A single outer absolute value enforces a nonnegative total but does not prevent cancellation between contributions of opposite signs.

Downstream consequence in the loss: let the original target magnitude be 0.1 and its watermarked magnitude be 0.8. Choose all other eligible maskers to have magnitude at most 0.1, so their threshold cannot exceed -20 dB and cannot dominate the chosen masker. The chosen center is selected because 1 exceeds 0.8 times its band maximum. A target that is itself selected as a weak masker does not change the dominance argument. At offset (1,-1), the printed global threshold is 0 dB, above 20 log10(0.8)=-1.93820026 dB, so the tile is masked. Eq. (5) gives lambda=2 and its Eq. (6) contribution is (0.8-0.1)^2/2=0.245.

For a directional-decay construction without cancellation, the same positive slopes give a decay of 1+1=2 dB. Its threshold is -2 dB, below the target level, so the tile is unmasked, lambda=1, and the loss is 0.49. Under the nonnegative-coefficient reading, this changes a branch of the objective and halves the penalty in this explicit TF-domain example. It is a counterexample to directional-decay behavior under that reading; it is not an experiment showing that a trained audio model audibly fails.

Decisive counterevidence: use signed coefficients alpha_f+=-1, alpha_f-=2, beta_t+=-0.5 and beta_t-=1. These also satisfy the printed numerical inequalities. Every term inside the absolute value is now nonpositive: a positive offset multiplies a negative plus coefficient, and a negative offset multiplies a positive minus coefficient. Therefore the outer absolute value yields independent additive decay magnitudes. The upward magnitude is 1 versus downward 2, and forward magnitude is 0.5 versus backward 1, matching the intended asymmetry. At (1,-1), D=2 and there is no cancellation.

Assessment: the positive-coefficient example is a fully specified conditional failure of one natural reading, but the signed-coefficient reading is a coherent favorable interpretation. The source does not fix the sign convention or numerical slope values, so the actual intended/implemented operator cannot be inferred. The ambiguity potentially concerns a central loss contribution, yet the available evidence does not establish that the authors used the failing convention. No unconditional Major or empirical falsification is recorded.

# 4. Concrete clarification and conditional repair

A reviewable clarification is to define four nonnegative decay magnitudes explicitly and write the intended directional rule with [z]_+=max(0,z):

\[\begin{aligned}D_+(u,v)&=\alpha_f^+[u]_++\alpha_f^-[-u]_+\\&\quad+\beta_t^+[v]_++\beta_t^-[-v]_+,\\ E_{\rm mask}&=20\log_{10}S_O(m_c,t_c)-D_+(u,v).\end{aligned}\] Every directional term is nonnegative. Holding the other coordinate fixed, moving farther along any one direction cannot decrease D_plus, and therefore cannot increase a single masker’s threshold. For the same magnitudes (1,2,0.5,1), this expression is algebraically equivalent to the rescuing signed-coefficient convention above. If the implementation already uses that convention, this is a notation clarification; if it uses four nonnegative coefficients in the printed expression, it is a change to the operator. The missing implementation determines which case applies.

The verified result is directional monotonicity of this explicit kernel and its equivalence to the favorable signed convention. It is not a validated physiological masking law. The cited primary study, Necciari et al. (2016), cautions that simply superposing spectral and temporal masking functions need not match measured TF masking. Required follow-up is to provide the actual coefficient signs/values and loss implementation, compare its map against both readings, and rerun the affected ablation only if the operator changes. No author adoption is asserted.

# 5. Empirical evidence, counterevidence and remaining scope

The displayed primary detection advantage is not overturned by this audit. Table 1 reports 99.19% average detection for XAttnMark and 97.1% for AudioSeal over the selected edits. Its 93% attribution figure is conditional and averaged across the stated user-pool settings. Repeated embeddings and edits of the same base audio should not be treated as new independent audio content when interpreting generalization.

Generative editing must be distinguished from robust attribution. Tables 2 and 9 evaluate detection under two editors and selected prompts/strengths. The p. 8 discussion and Appendix B explicitly acknowledge that robust attribution after these edits remains unresolved. The reported detection results support that narrower task; they do not establish recoverable creator identity after arbitrary generative editing or a deployment-level low-FPR guarantee.

Several reporting inconsistencies remain Needs Evidence rather than additional proven core failures. Table 7’s displayed average columns can often be explained by an omitted N=10 condition, which is visible elsewhere; treating every average as an error would ignore this alternative. However, attribution values across Tables 5, 7 and 13 differ substantially even where they describe MusicCaps and the same user count. Checkpoints, sample lists, pooling and averaging must be reconciled before those tables can be treated as one reproducible experiment.

Appendix C.14 uses McNemar and Wilcoxon tests on 100 messages per clip across 100 base clips. The raw paired results and the intended inferential population are needed to evaluate dependence. Clip-level clustering matters for generalization across audio; a conditional fixed-clip/message interpretation is a different target. This audit does not assert that the observed mean advantage vanishes or that the tests necessarily used an invalid unit.

The speed-change limitations are explicit and substantial: the main table’s attribution for XAttnMark is only about 3%, while TimbreWM is higher in that row. Statements that all baselines completely fail should be narrowed. The post-hoc speed-reversion search is a separate experiment with substantial added search time. This is a scope correction, not a newly established failure of average robustness.

Quality is competitive rather than uniformly best. Table 4 and the small listening study support different trade-offs across metrics. In Table 3’s spectrogram attack, XAttnMark has better detection and PESQ but lower SI-SNR and ViSQOL than AudioSeal; the prose’s blanket claim of improvement in every configuration/quality measure needs qualification. These table-visible exceptions do not establish a central empirical failure.

The MusicCaps subset is drawn from AudioSet and both appear in training. The paper calls its evaluation subset held out, but no sample-ID manifest was available to verify cross-dataset exclusion. Dataset overlap is therefore an unresolved provenance question; leakage is not alleged without identifying overlapping training/test examples.
