Abstract: Theorem 5’s convergence of the dynamic graph tangent kernel to an independently specified RKHS kernel is false under the stated assumptions. Proposition 6’s sufficient-loss-reduction bound is also false, even when the tangent and canonical kernels agree exactly. These are Major defects in the principal theoretical justification for GraNT. A related coefficient-only gradient-ranking argument requires additional kernel geometry. Static inspection additionally demonstrates Major errors in the pinned release’s core discrepancy-based selection and comparison metrics. These defects invalidate the corresponding released selection/evaluation routes; historical score and timing effects remain unresolved without bound run/prediction provenance. The review does not infer that all reported time savings disappear.

# Problem 1: Convergent training does not identify an arbitrary RKHS kernel

Assessment: Major. Theorem 5 and Eq. (20), p. 6, state that for convex loss and a given training set the dynamic GNTK satisfies \[\lim_{t\to\infty}K_{\theta_t}(G_i,\cdot)=K(G_i,\cdot).\] Section 3 defines an RKHS from a symmetric positive definite graph kernel. It does not impose an equality between that fixed kernel and the parameter-induced tangent metric.

## An admissible GCN with successful convergence

Let the graph domain contain a single one-node graph with \(X=[1]\), \(A=[0]\), and convolutional orders \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2=1\). This order is explicitly permitted in Section 4.1. A two-layer, width-one GCN with sum pooling is \[f_{a,b}(G)=b\,\operatorname{ReLU}(a).\] Choose the RKHS kernel \(K(G,G)=1\), which is strictly positive definite on this singleton domain. All scalar functions on the domain, including every network output and \(f^\star(G)=1\), belong to this RKHS. Use \(\ell(f,1)=\tfrac12(f-1)^2\), \(a_0=b_0=1/2\), and \(0<\eta\leq1/4\).

Gradient descent preserves \(a_t=b_t=c_t>0\), with \[c_{t+1}=c_t+\eta c_t(1-c_t^2),\qquad
 1-c_{t+1}=(1-c_t)[1-\eta c_t(1+c_t)].\] For \(0<c_t<1\), the sequence increases and stays below 1. Its limit is therefore 1. The loss and residual tend to zero, and the trajectory never reaches a ReLU kink. Nevertheless, \[K_{\theta_t}(G,G)
 =(\partial_a f)^2+(\partial_b f)^2
 =a_t^2+b_t^2=2c_t^2\longrightarrow2\ne1=K(G,G).\] This is a counterexample with genuine successful training, not a failure to optimize a nonconvex parameter objective. It also works for arbitrarily small positive learning rates in the interval above. The corresponding continuous flow \(\dot c=\eta c(1-c^2)\) has the same limiting contradiction.

## Invalid proof step and surviving statement

Appendix B, Eq. (33), p. 17, equates parameter-gradient evolution with functional gradient descent in the independently chosen RKHS metric. Membership \(f_\theta\in\mathcal H\) does not justify that equality. For differentiable parameter gradient flow, the chain rule gives exactly \[\frac{d}{dt}f_{\theta_t}(G)
 =-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_i
 K_{\theta_t}(G,G_i)\,\partial_f\ell(f_{\theta_t}(G_i),y_i).\] A finite discrete step has a Taylor remainder; a time derivative does not need the additional remainder used in Eq. (16). Neither form identifies an arbitrary fixed canonical kernel.

Equations (36)–(37) further infer convergence of individual kernel sections from a residual-weighted sum as the residual vanishes. Vanishing coefficients or cancellation cannot establish that conclusion. Defining the canonical kernel retrospectively to equal an existing limiting tangent kernel changes the theorem’s setup; it does not prove convergence to the originally specified RKHS kernel.

The false theorem supplies the paper’s central bridge between finite-width GCN learning and canonical-kernel nonparametric teaching. Its failure is Major. Ordinary tangent-kernel dynamics and practical subset training remain meaningful; no replacement universal kernel-convergence theorem is verified here.

# Problem 2: Kernel upper bounds cannot provide the claimed minimum decrease

Equation reference: eq:claimed. Assessment: Major. Proposition 6 and Eq. (21), p. 6, assume a convex \(\tau\)-smooth loss, kernel upper bound \(\gamma>0\), and \(\eta\leq1/(2\tau\gamma)\), then assert \[\dot{\mathcal L}\leq-\frac{\eta\gamma}{2}
 \left(\frac1N\sum_i\partial_f\ell(f(G_i),y_i)\right)^2.
 \]

## Counterexample with identical tangent and canonical kernels

Use two one-node, edgeless graphs with features \(x_a=(1/2,0)\) and \(x_b=(0,1)\). A one-layer \(\kappa=1\) GCN is \(f_\theta(G)=x_G^\top\theta\). Choose its actual tangent kernel as the canonical kernel: \[K(G,G')=x_G^\top x_{G'},\qquad
 K_{\{G_a,G_b\}}=\begin{pmatrix}1/4&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\] The kernel is strictly positive definite, with tight global upper bound \(\gamma=1\). Train on the one observation \((G_a,1)\), starting at \(\theta=0\), with squared loss, \(\tau=1\) and \(\eta=1/4\). The smoothness and step-size conditions hold; the tangent/canonical kernels agree throughout.

At time zero the actual flow gives \[\dot{\mathcal L}(0)=-\eta K(G_a,G_a)=-1/16,
 \qquad\hbox{whereas Eq.~\text{(eq:claimed)} requires }\dot{\mathcal L}(0)\leq-1/8.\] The inequality is false. Under a discrete-step reading, \(\theta_1=(1/8,0)\) and \(f_{\theta_1}(G_a)=1/16\), so \[\mathcal L_1-\mathcal L_0
 =\tfrac12(15/16)^2-\tfrac12=-31/512>-1/8,\] also contradicting the asserted reduction. This issue is independent of Problem 1.

Appendix B, Eq. (44), p. 19, upper-bounds a positive quadratic form. Equation (46) substitutes that bound into a negatively weighted term, which reverses the valid inequality direction. Even the scalar case above suffices, without any ambiguity about entrywise versus positive-semidefinite matrix bounds.

For canonical-kernel gradient flow the valid identity is \[\dot{\mathcal L}=-\frac{\eta}{N^2}d^\top Kd,\qquad
 d_i=\partial_f\ell(f(G_i),y_i).\] If additionally \(K\succeq\mu I\), this implies \(\dot{\mathcal L}\leq-\eta\mu\|d\|_2^2/N^2\). This changed-assumption flow statement is verified algebraically; it is not a repair of the published finite-step theorem or a proof of improved teaching speed.

## The related coefficient-only greedy argument

Section 4.3, p. 6, claims that gradient size can be increased by maximizing projection coefficients without computing kernel-section norms. In fact, \[\|d_i K(G_i,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal H}
 =|d_i|\sqrt{K(G_i,G_i)}.\] Consider the same linear graph family with \(x_1=(0.1,0)\), \(x_2=(0,1)\), targets \((2,1)\) and \(\theta_0=0\). The target is realizable by \(\theta^\star=(20,1)\). The largest-residual rule chooses graph 1, although its sample-gradient norm is \(0.2\), versus 1 for graph 2. For one selected example and \(\eta=0.1\), full empirical squared loss changes from 1.25 to 1.248001 if graph 1 is selected, or to 1.2025 if graph 2 is selected.

Thus coefficient ordering alone does not provide the claimed gradient-size justification. Equal kernel diagonals would repair the single-example norm ordering; multiple-example correlations and full-loss descent still require analysis. This does not assert that the heuristic must always choose an optimal step or cannot help empirically. Together with the false sufficient-reduction proposition, it undermines the core analytic justification, while leaving empirical time savings to separate evidence.

# Problem 3: The released sampler loses graph identity after shuffling

Assessment: Major, scoped to the pinned implementation of core GraNT selection. Algorithm 1 (p. 7) selects the graphs or batches with the largest stated discrepancies. In , the relevant training loaders use ; lines 265–267 pass that loader to . The sampler scores traversal order, then turns selected positions into ranges (batch variants) or (single variants). At these offsets index the underlying, unshuffled dataset. No original dataset IDs are retained.

Take dataset discrepancies \((1,2,30,40)\), batch size 2, ratio \(1/2\), and admissible shuffled order \((3,0,2,1)\), using zero-based IDs. The observed batch means are 20.5 and 16. GraNT(B) should retain IDs \((3,0)\) but returns \((0,1)\). Selecting one graph from each scored batch, GraNT(S) should retain \((3,2)\) but returns \((0,2)\). The supplied selection operation therefore need not return the graphs it scored highest. Largest-discrepancy teaching is the defining selection operation in Algorithm 1 and Eq. (23), not an incidental utility. Selecting unrelated dataset IDs directly invalidates that operation in the released route and supports Major independently of a measured change in accuracy or speed.

Identity traversal and accidental overlaps are valid special cases; the examples are not claims about an observed random seed. Making only the returned subset loader unshuffled does not repair the lost mapping. Carrying original IDs through scoring or using a separate unshuffled scoring loader would fix the index correspondence locally. Actual training outcomes and historical correspondence are unverified, so no measured loss of speedup is asserted.

# Problem 4: Released metrics have incompatible aggregation

Assessment: Major, scoped to the pinned core evaluation/comparison route; historical corrected values remain Needs Evidence.

## MAE is divided by the batch size again

returns the mean absolute error of a batch. The train/validation/test routines in sum those means and divide by the number of graphs (e.g., test lines 494 and 511). For \(N\) examples in equal batches of size \(B\), \[\mathrm{MAE}_{\rm released}
 =\frac1N\sum_b\frac1B\sum_{i\in b}|e_i|
 =\frac{\mathrm{MAE}_{\rm ordinary}}{B}.\] With 512 scalar errors all equal to 1 and \(B=256\), the outputs are \(1/256=0.00390625\) and 1. The supplied AL-3DGraph baseline instead concatenates predictions and computes the ordinary full-set mean (, ). Under these source paths, Table 2’s MAE comparison does not use a common normalization.

The released QM9(B) log contains test MAE 0.005005646; the ZINC(B) log contains 0.004855736, corroborating the reported scale. They do not authenticate every table run. A common factor can preserve ordering among GraNT variants and their same-batch baseline. With a partial final batch, multiplying a published aggregate by 256 is not an exact correction: per-example or per-batch errors are required.

## Average precision is computed within batches

For ogbg-molpcba, evaluates AP per batch and averages it. AP is a ranking statistic and is not batch-additive. Consider batches of \((\text{label},\text{score})\) pairs \[,\qquad[(1,0.9),(0,0.8)].\] Each batch has AP 1, whereas global ranked labels \((1,0,1,0)\) give AP \((1+2/3)/2=5/6\). This finite discrepancy also applies to a task within a multitask evaluation. The released full-training molpcba log records 0.326976432, rounding to Table 1’s 0.3270; this supports result correspondence but supplies no predictions from which to compute a corrected AP.

The molhiv graph-level ROC-AUC branch concatenates predictions correctly; this finding should not be generalized to it. The noncommon MAE normalization and replacement of whole-dataset AP by a different statistic directly invalidate the released route as a faithful implementation of the paper’s central metric comparisons. This material evaluation defect supports Major without requiring a demonstrated ranking reversal. Neither the corrected historical MAE/AP values nor their comparative ordering are established. Reaggregation from bound prediction files is the necessary repair check.

# Overall impact, positive checks, and state

The kernel-convergence and sufficient-decrease statements are false as stated, so the overall assessment is Major. The released selection and evaluation paths independently contain Major defects tied to their core roles, with historical experimental attribution kept separate. The routine RKHS evaluation-functional derivative and tangent-kernel chain rule remain valid. The percentages calculated from Table 1’s displayed times are 36.6248%, 38.1947%, 30.9680%, and 47.3025%, consistent with the four headline rounded reductions. Their underlying timings were not reproduced.

The supplied cumulative timer includes selection overhead; there is no basis here for alleging that all teacher costs are omitted. Its epoch timestamp is sampled before the current epoch’s work, a narrower plotting limitation whose material effect was not quantified. Released ZINC validation losses round to the table’s loss entries while test losses differ; exact table/run provenance remains Needs Evidence rather than a definitive attribution.
