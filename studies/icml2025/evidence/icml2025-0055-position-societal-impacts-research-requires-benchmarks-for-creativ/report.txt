# 3. Problem 2 - Professional creativity is misattributed as Big-C (Minor)

Section 2.1, p2, attributes creative work by professional artists and writers to Big-C in Kaufman and Beghetto (2009). The cited Four-C model instead distinguishes professional expertise (Pro-c) from eminent contributions (Big-C); see its abstract and pp1-2 [E3]. Profession alone is not the criterion for Big-C.

The strongest alternative is that the author intends a practical everyday-versus-professional scope distinction. That is a permissible choice, but it is not the taxonomy attributed to this reference. Correct the terminology and explicitly state whether both professional-level and eminent work are excluded. This is a source-verified textual repair. It does not itself change the operational little-c inclusion rule or establish different sampled themes; the supported impact is a bounded attribution/scope error. The same wording persists in the inspected arXiv v2 passage.

# 4. Problem 3 - The InstructGPT percentage is miscomputed (Minor)

Table 1, p5, assigns 57.5% to InstructGPT generation plus brainstorming. The cited official NeurIPS 2022 supplement, Table 1, physical PDF p1 / printed p16, gives 45.6% and 11.2% [E4]. Their sum is 56.8%, a difference of 0.7 percentage points. Rounding components to one decimal cannot explain 57.5%. The focal paper’s prose approximation of about 57% is consistent with 56.8%.

The verified repair is 56.8% for this stated aggregation, unless a different source population is explicitly documented. This change does not alter the displayed 28%-62% range or demonstrate a failure of the prevalence motivation. The upstream categories and sampling frame also differ across studies: broad generation or assisting categories are not identical to the focal definition of creative composition. Table 1 should be read as heterogeneous contextual evidence, not a pooled or population-wide prevalence estimate.

# 5. Problem 4 - Released counts do not fully match the described pipeline (Minor; historical effect Needs Evidence)

The two author datasets linked in Appendix A.2 were pinned to LMSYS commit c44782f10f8a329558745fa410686f32d45477b9 and WildChat commit d470c0f576cefc0f29a0b9589f5266e9b205e112 [E5]. Their public Parquet metadata and complete cluster/creative-flag columns were read through verified byte ranges. All 50 clusters per dataset were counted. Cached-byte crosschecks agreed; no author processing code or model was run.

Using the manuscript’s rule, a cluster is selected when its count of creative flags divided by its row count exceeds 0.30. The exact integer check is 10 times the creative-flag count > 3 times the row count. No cluster is exactly at 30%, so changing > to >= cannot resolve the WildChat discrepancy. The released selected pool totals 289,730 rows, consistent with the approximate 289k description, but it contains 42 selected clusters rather than 43.

The established issue is a publication/release correspondence discrepancy. Figure 3’s LMSYS count differs by 1,000 rows; Appendix A.2’s WildChat selected-cluster count differs by one. The manuscript’s reported design implies 43*100=4,300 sampled prompt slots, while the pinned labels would imply 4,200 if the same 100-per-cluster rule were applied. Neither number is a verified realized annotation count: exact historical sampled IDs, revisions and coding records were not acquired.

Different historical labels or a manual selection override could explain the difference. Consequently no lost theme, fabricated annotation, or changed societal conclusion is inferred. Minor is supported for the observable correspondence defect; its effect on the historical qualitative analysis remains Needs Evidence. Reconcile the data revision and selected cluster/sample manifest before replacing historical totals. The counting procedure is independently checked, but the authors’ release and analysis were not modified.

# 6. Checks that preserve the argument and delimit the evidence

Sampling and qualitative scope. The two-million figure describes the source-corpus frame. Main Section 2.2 and Appendix A disclose filtering, 50 clusters per dataset, a keyword-based cluster threshold and 100 sampled prompts per selected cluster. Appendix A.3 combines manually identified tasks with Claude summaries. This supports qualitative task discovery, not proof that millions of prompts were manually coded or that a model independently validated the human coding. Section 2.2 limits scope to the included datasets, and p5 footnote 4 explicitly warns that power users distort raw counts.

Selection can miss rare or lexically atypical creative uses, and whole selected clusters can contain noncreative prompts. Those limitations constrain representativeness and completeness; they do not negate the existence of observed task types. Appendix A.4 expressly reports residual sexual content after moderation. No hidden claim of perfect removal was inferred.

Benchmark and risk scope. The narrow HELM Lite comparison is supported by its dated official description of reference-based generation and missing open-ended instruction following [E7]. BIG-Bench Lite must be distinguished from the full suite. Sections 4-6 generally frame downstream harms as possible or uncertain and propose research directions; no new causal experiment is asserted. This review does not demand such experiments to justify a research-policy position. The adequacy of every proposed metric, every external risk study and every possible benchmark was not established.

Version comparison. The retrieved arXiv record’s latest version is v2, dated 24 May 2025 [E6]. Its first page lists Shen and Carlos Guestrin, whereas the PMLR record lists Shen; the PMLR source remains the reviewed artifact. The inspected p2 attribution and pp4,15,16,21 passages retain the relevant content. Selected-page token comparison is only a locator aid because layout reflow changes reading order.
