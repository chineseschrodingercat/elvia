Abstract: The spherical-cap privacy calculation, sampling construction, moment formulas, and released calibration routines were checked. A dimension-independent privacy bound for coordinatewise Laplace noise disproves the claim that the Laplace comparator uses a nearly minimal noise scale under the paper’s unit \(\ell_2\) sensitivity assumption. At an explicitly reported privacy setting and dimension, an admissible comparator has one quarter of the stated Laplace mean squared error. This is a Major issue in the optimal-baseline justification, without refuting the new mechanism’s privacy guarantee or its superiority to the analytic Gaussian comparator. A separate Minor endpoint inconsistency affects the printed one-dimensional privacy checker. Scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Definitions and valid parts of the calculation

Let \(T\) have global \(\ell_2\) sensitivity at most one. The new mechanism releases \(T(X)+Y\), with density proportional to \(\exp(-\|Y\|_2/\sigma)\). For a unit displacement \(e_1\), put \(\tau=\epsilon\sigma\) and \[V=\{y:\|y-e_1\|_2-\|y\|_2\geq\tau\}.\] Under \(d\geq2\) and \(0<\tau<1\), its intersection with a radius-\(r\) sphere about zero is described by \[y_1\leq \frac{1-\tau^2}{2}-\tau r.\] The cap height is therefore \(\min\{r(1-\tau)+(1-\tau^2)/2,2r\}\), as in Lemma 3.6. On a radius-\(R\) sphere about \(e_1\), the cutoff becomes \((1+\tau^2)/2-\tau R\), and the nonzero cap has height \(R(1-\tau)-(1-\tau^2)/2\) for \(R\geq(1+\tau)/2\). These formulas follow by squaring the respective nonnegative distance inequalities, with the stated radius restrictions.

The normalized first cutoff is \(-\tau+(1-\tau^2)/(2r)\), decreasing in \(r\); the normalized second cutoff relative to \(e_1\) is \(-\tau-(1-\tau^2)/(2R)\), increasing in \(R\). This directly confirms the cap-fraction monotonicities needed for Lemmas 3.13 and 3.21. The radial law is \(\mathrm{Gamma}(d,\sigma)\), so the corresponding gamma-mass weighted left sums have the stated directions when the grids meet the lemmas’ increasing-radius assumptions. These analytic observations do not certify floating-point rounding or every parameter choice of the implementation.

For the sampler, a uniform ball radius has distribution function \(u^d\), and an independent \(\mathrm{Gamma}(d+1,\sigma)\) scale yields the stated radial density. In particular, the squared-error identities \(d(d+1)\sigma^2\) for the \(\ell_2\) mechanism and \(2db^2\) for independent \(\mathrm{Laplace}(0,b)\) coordinates are consistent. Here \(b\) always denotes the coordinate Laplace scale, avoiding the rescaled \(\sigma\) notation surrounding Corollary 4.2.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The Laplace comparator is not nearly optimally calibrated under unit \(\ell_2\) sensitivity).  Section 4.2, PDF p. 8, says the comparison uses the smallest possible scales, sets the Laplace coordinate scale to \(b_{\rm paper}=\sqrt d/(\epsilon+\delta)\), and footnote 2 asserts that the actual minimum differs only negligibly. Appendix A.4, Lemma A.2, pp. 15–16, is offered as the supporting argument. This optimality justification does not hold for the paper’s \(\ell_2\) sensitivity class.

## The suspect dependence

Lemma A.2 considers a displacement \(e_1\), with \(\ell_1\) and \(\ell_2\) norm both one. For this pair, its exact privacy deficit is \[1-\exp\!\left(\frac{\epsilon-1/b}{2}\right)
 \quad\text{when }\epsilon\leq1/b.\] Lemma A.2 remains valid for its stated axis pair. It gives a necessary lower bound near \(1/\epsilon\) on \(b\), not near \(\sqrt d/\epsilon\). Applying a unit-\(\ell_1\) worst-case interpretation after replacing sensitivity by \(\sqrt d\) enlarges the neighboring-displacement class: that larger class includes concentration of the whole \(\ell_1\) displacement in one coordinate, which need not have unit \(\ell_2\) norm. The following valid calibration for the original class provides a direct disproof; it does not rely on this interpretation of the appendix.

## A uniform guarantee satisfying every original sensitivity condition

Let \(Z_i\) be independent \(\mathrm{Laplace}(0,b)\) variables. For any ordered neighboring pair let \(v=T(X')-T(X)\), so \(\|v\|_2\leq1\). Its privacy loss under output \(T(X)+Z\) is \[L=\sum_{i=1}^d L_i,\qquad
 L_i=\frac{|Z_i-v_i|-|Z_i|}{b},\qquad
 a_i=\frac{|v_i|}{b}.\] Independence of the \(Z_i\) makes the \(L_i\) independent. The triangle inequality gives \(-a_i\leq L_i\leq a_i\). Direct integration of the Laplace density yields \[\mathbb E L_i=a_i+e^{-a_i}-1\leq a_i^2/2,\qquad
 \sum_i a_i^2=\|v\|_2^2/b^2\leq b^{-2}.\] The inequality uses \(e^{-a}\leq1-a+a^2/2\) for \(a\geq0\). The elementary bounded-variable exponential-moment inequality gives \[\mathbb E e^{t(L-\mathbb E L)}
 \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{t^2}{2}\sum_i a_i^2\right).\] For completeness, each centered summand lies in an interval of width \(2a_i\); convexity bounds its exponential moment by that of the two endpoints, whose log moment is bounded by \(t^2(2a_i)^2/8\). Multiplication over independent coordinates gives the display.

Equation reference: eq:bound. Chernoff minimization, followed by the two bounds above, proves that for \(\epsilon>1/(2b^2)\), \[
 \Pr(L>\epsilon)
 \leq \exp\!\left[-\frac{b^2}{2}
       \left(\epsilon-\frac{1}{2b^2}\right)^2\right].\] The case \(v=0\) has zero privacy loss and is immediate. Equation [eq:bound] (#eq:bound) holds for every direction and every allowed displacement, including the reverse ordered pair. Splitting any event into \(L\leq\epsilon\) and \(L>\epsilon\) proves \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-DP whenever its right side is at most \(\delta\). Thus the calculation verifies the entire adjacency condition, rather than only a convenient shift.

Take \[d=100,\qquad \epsilon=1,\qquad \delta=10^{-5},\qquad b=5.\] This dimension and privacy pair are used in Section 4.2. All noise coordinates are independent and use the same scale; no extra restriction is imposed on \(T\). Equation [eq:bound] (#eq:bound) evaluates to \[\exp[-25(1-1/50)^2/2]
 =6.1135679664\times10^{-6}<10^{-5}.\] This is a valid Laplace comparator. Nevertheless, \[\begin{aligned}
 b_{\rm paper}&=\frac{10}{1.00001}=9.999900001,\\
 \mathbb E\|Z_{\rm paper}\|_2^2&=2(100)b_{\rm paper}^2
       =19999.600006,\\
 \mathbb E\|Z\|_2^2&=2(100)(5)^2=5000.\end{aligned}\] The ratio is \(0.250005000025\). Hence the optimum is no greater than \(5\), at most approximately half the stated scale, and the achievable mean squared error is approximately one quarter of the stated comparator error. The numerical evaluation is preserved in `independent_check.py` and `independent_check_results.json`; the guarantee itself is analytic.

## Counterevidence, impact, and a supported correction

The paper’s Laplace calibration remains a valid conservative calibration, and the formula in its released `laplace.py` agrees with the printed value. Under a unit \(\ell_1\) neighboring-displacement class, the axis argument is relevant to near-pure-DP optimality. Neither fact rescues the asserted near optimality for all unit-\(\ell_2\) statistics.

This is assessed Major for the invalid optimal-comparator justification, which directly supports the central error comparison in Section 4.2 and the minimum-scale characterization of Figure 5. The example does not establish that Laplace beats the \(\ell_2\) mechanism, does not refute the reported \(\ell_2\) versus analytic-Gaussian advantage, and does not refute the peak advantage quoted at \(d=7\). Figure 1 deliberately truncates the Laplace curve after \(d=8\), so the present \(d=100\) calculation is not represented as a correction to an actually plotted Laplace point at that dimension.

A precise supported replacement is: the evaluated Laplace baseline uses the conservative scale \(\sqrt d/(\epsilon+\delta)\); equation [eq:bound] (#eq:bound) supplies another sufficient calibration under unit \(\ell_2\) sensitivity. It is not claimed to be the exact optimum. Recomputing comparisons against an optimized approximate-DP Laplace baseline requires additional privacy calibration and regenerated numerical comparisons; the present bound alone does not determine that optimum.

Problem 2 (The closed high-loss event is mishandled at the one-dimensional pure-DP boundary).  Lemma 3.2 on p. 3 and Corollary 3.14 on p. 5 assert zero probability of the closed high-loss event when \(\sigma\geq1/\epsilon\). At \(d=1\) and \(\sigma=1/\epsilon\), the negative half-line has positive probability. The two printed algorithms handle this equality differently.

## Exact endpoint check

Set \(d=1\), \(\epsilon=1\), \(\sigma=1\), \(T(X)=0\), and \(T(X')=1\). This is an allowed unit-sensitivity pair. The definition on p. 3 gives \[V=\{y:|y-1|-|y|\geq1\}=(-\infty,0].\] Consequently, \[\Pr_{M(0)}(V)=\tfrac12,\qquad
 \Pr_{M(1)}(V)=\tfrac{1}{2e},\qquad
 \Pr_{M(0)}(V)-e\Pr_{M(1)}(V)=0.\] The mechanism is therefore pure \(1\)-DP, as also follows from Lemma 2.4. The measure-zero assertion in the proof of Lemma 3.2 is valid for the negative axis in \(d\geq2\), but not for a half-line in \(d=1\).

Printed Algorithm 1, p. 5, uses \(\sigma>1/\epsilon\) for its shortcut and therefore returns \(1/2\) at this input. Algorithm 2, p. 6, uses \(\sigma\geq1/\epsilon\) and returns zero. Algorithm 3 consequently compares \(1/2\) to \(\delta\) and rejects this valid mechanism for \(0<\delta<1/2\). At \(\delta=0\), its preliminary finite-radius truncation cannot achieve zero tail probability, so this executable false-negative claim is restricted to positive \(\delta\). This is a false negative of the sufficient checker, not an unsafe privacy acceptance.

The staged `l2.py` uses \(\sigma\geq1/\epsilon\) in both term routines, giving a combined value zero. Its individual probability-bound interpretation still needs the closed-versus-strict-event qualification, but its combined result at this endpoint is correct. Thus the printed pseudocode behavior is not attributed to the available implementation or to historical experiment runs.

The issue is Minor: it is a localized boundary inconsistency, with no demonstrated failure of the interior spherical-cap guarantee. One exact correction is to return a positive DP verdict in the overall checker whenever \(\sigma\geq1/\epsilon\), using the pure-DP theorem, and retain the actual endpoint probabilities in any separately stated closed-event probability lemmas. Another consistent convention uses a strict high-loss event throughout; changing only one term is insufficient. These endpoint corrections are analytically verified only in their stated scope.
