Abstract: A three-author instance already contained in the paper disproves the claimed equivalence between the continuous LP and integer desk-rejection problem, including its solution-extraction rule. The separate worst-case NP-hardness proof also contains an invalid reduction, although its conclusion admits a valid replacement proof. Two illustrative arithmetic errors are local. The ideal-feasibility impossibility examples and the distinction between average and individual fairness survive. Scientific adjudication remains pending.\par
\section*{Identity, scope and definitions}
Yuefan Cao, Xiaoyu Li, Yingyu Liang, Zhizhou Sha, Zhenmei Shi, Zhao Song and Jiahao Zhang, Dissecting Submission Limit in Desk-Rejections: A Mathematical Analysis of Fairness in AI Conference Policies, ICML 2025, PMLR 267, I25-43632 (0263), is reviewed in its 23-page proceedings version [paper]. Physical and printed page numbers agree. The independent check read the main argument and experimental description (pp. 1–9), proofs (pp. 13–20), case studies and policy-link appendix (pp. 21–23). Bibliography entries were not independently audited against their original works.\par
There are \(n\) authors, \(m\) papers and a positive integer cap \(x\). The binary incidence matrix \(W\) has \(W_{ij}=1\) when author \(i\) coauthors paper \(j\). Write \(d_i=\sum_jW_{ij}>0\), \(D=\operatorname{Diag}(d_i)\), and let \(r_j=1\) mean retaining paper \(j\). Feasibility is \(Wr\le x\mathbf1\), \(r\in\{0,1\}^m\). Individual rejection cost is \(c_i=1-(Wr)_i/d_i\). Average and worst-case costs are \(\zeta_{\rm avg}=n^{-1}\sum_i c_i\) and \(\zeta_{\rm worst}=\max_i c_i\). Every witness below has positive degrees.\par
\section*{The exact LP guarantee}
Problem 1 (Fractional extreme points invalidate the core guarantee: Major).  Theorem 5.16, p. 7, and Theorem B.9, p. 20, claim that maximizing \(\mathbf1^\top D^{-1}Wr\) over \(0\le r\le1\), \(Wr\le x\mathbf1\) is equivalent to the binary problem. The proof infers that an LP extreme point must have binary coordinates. Algorithm 1 then retains only papers with \(r_j=1\).\par
\subsection*{A fully admissible counterexample}
Use the paper’s own Lemma A.6 (p. 15): three papers have author sets \(\{a_1,a_2\}\), \(\{a_1,a_3\}\), \(\{a_2,a_3\}\), with \(x=1\). Thus \[W=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix},
\quad D=2I,\quad \mathbf1^\top D^{-1}W=(1,1,1).\] Every paper has authors and every author has two submissions. The relaxed constraints are \[r_1+r_2\le1,\quad r_1+r_3\le1,\quad r_2+r_3\le1.\] Adding them gives \(r_1+r_2+r_3\le3/2\). The vector \(r=(1/2,1/2,1/2)\) attains this bound. Equality in the sum forces equality in all three constraints, whose unique solution is that vector. It is the unique LP optimum, not an avoidable fractional choice among tied integral optima.\par
Any two retained papers share an author, so any binary feasible solution retains at most one paper. Keeping one is feasible, hence the integer objective optimum is \(1\), strictly below \(3/2\). Since \(\zeta_{\rm avg}=1-\mathbf1^\top D^{-1}Wr/3\), the LP value is \(1/2\) while the best actual retained set has cost \(2/3\). Algorithm 1, lines 19–24, extracts no paper from its unique LP optimum; its returned cost is \(1\). Parent exact enumeration independently confirms all four binary feasible vectors (`check.json`).\par
\subsection*{Generality, alternatives and impact}
This is not peculiar to cap one. Lemma A.7 supplies \(n=m=x+2\) and \(W=J-I\). Then \(D=(x+1)I\), all objective coefficients are one, and the unique LP optimum is \(r_j=x/(x+1)\). Its average cost is \(1/(x+1)\). Any binary feasible solution retains at most \(x\) papers: retaining \(x+1\) leaves an omitted author present on all of them, violating the cap. Retaining \(x\) is feasible, giving integer average cost \(2/(x+2)\). At cap four these values are \(1/5\) and \(1/3\); the extraction rule still retains nothing.\par
The linear-programming theorem ensures existence of an optimal extreme point for this bounded feasible polytope; it does not make that point a vertex of the unconstrained cube. Intersecting the cube with incidence inequalities creates new fractional vertices. Reading the contradictory binary-vector description below Definition 5.15 as an extra binary constraint would instead produce an integer program and remove the asserted LP efficiency guarantee. Independent random rounding does not restore a deterministic cap or exact optimality.\par
This falsifies a central theorem, the general exactness of Algorithm 1 and its claimed LP-based complexity guarantee (Remarks 5.17–5.18), supporting Major. The relaxation remains a lower bound on achievable average rejection cost. Integer optimization or rounding with a proved guarantee could replace the invalid step, but neither was established or executed here. The repository was unavailable in the staged acquisition, so the actual PuLP variable categories and extraction used for Table 3 remain unknown. This counterexample does not establish that every reported experimental number is wrong.\par
\section*{The published hardness proof}
Problem 2 (The reduction solves a different problem: Major).  Theorem 5.11, p. 6, is a core distinction motivating the switch from worst-case to average fairness. Its proof, Theorem B.7 on p. 19, sets \(x=m\) and adds \(\|r\|_1\le K\), although that cardinality constraint is absent from the target problem.\par
With \(x=m\), the actual target always permits \(r=\mathbf1\), giving cost zero. The displayed construction without its extra constraint therefore cannot encode a nontrivial Set Cover decision. Calling the additionally constrained optimization easier does not provide an oracle reduction from the stated target.\par
A second error occurs in Lemma B.6 (pp. 18–19): retaining at least one paper for every author is claimed equivalent to \[\| \mathbf1-D^{-1}Wr\|_\infty\le1-\frac1{\min_i d_i}.\] Take \(W=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(r=(1,0)^\top\). Both authors retain one paper, but the worst cost is \(1/2\), greater than the printed threshold \(0\). The reverse argument correctly reaches a bound using \(\max_i d_i\) before incorrectly replacing it by \(\min_i d_i\). These are substantive failures in the published derivation of a central complexity result, supporting Major even though the theorem can be repaired.\par
\subsection*{A complete replacement reduction}
Consider a Set Cover instance with \(m\ge1\) sets and \(1\le K\le m\). Empty-universe cases, elements appearing in no set, and \(K>m\) can be decided or normalized directly; nontrivial instances have positive element degrees. Create one author per universe element and one paper per set, with the corresponding incidence. Add one further author who coauthors every paper. Set the common cap to \(x=K\) and the decision threshold to \(\tau=1-1/m\).\par
The added author’s constraint is exactly \(\sum_j r_j\le K\). Its degree is \(m\), while every other degree is in \([1,m]\). If a cover of at most \(K\) sets exists, retain those papers. Every author retains at least one, all counts are at most \(K\), and \(c_i\le1-1/d_i\le\tau\). Conversely, a binary feasible set with \(\zeta_{\rm worst}\le\tau\) satisfies \((Wr)_i\ge d_i/m>0\) for every original author. Integer counts are at least one, so it covers every element; the added author enforces at most \(K\) selected sets. The construction has polynomial size and proves NP-hardness by comparing the target optimum with \(\tau\).\par
This analytically checked replacement preserves the worst-case hardness conclusion; it does not validate the published proof or repair the false average-fairness theorem.\par
\section*{Illustrative arithmetic and evidentiary limits}
Problem 3 (Two local numerical statements disagree with their definitions: Minor).  Example C.2 (p. 21) sets \(n=5\) but averages four entries using \(1/4\). Example 6.1 (p. 8) reverses one inequality for the roulette method’s expected worst cost.\par
In C.2, retaining \(p_3,p_4\) gives all five author costs \((1/2,1,0,0,0)\), so \(\zeta_{\rm avg}=3/10\), not \(3/8\), and \(\zeta_{\rm worst}=1\). Retaining one paper from each pair gives five costs equal to \(1/2\), so both costs are \(1/2\). Parent enumeration confirms both optima. The qualitative conflict between average and worst-case fairness survives; the paper explicitly acknowledges that average optimization can eliminate an individual’s entire portfolio.\par
In Example 6.1, the roulette expected worst cost is \[\frac{25}{26}\frac1{26}+\frac1{26}
=\frac{51}{676}>\frac1{26},\] not the printed \(\le1/26\). Expected average cost is \(1/26\), above the ideal \(1/52\). This correction agrees with the prose that roulette is suboptimal and creates no further core contradiction.\par
Algorithm 2 increments counts only for retained papers. For cap one and ordered author sets \(\{A\},\{A,B\},\{B\}\), it retains the first and third papers. A rule counting all original submissions would reject the third. The rule descriptions are not interchangeable. Which rule a specific historical conference actually enforced was not independently checked; policy correspondence remains Needs Evidence, not a demonstrated historical misdescription.\par
Table 3 concerns simulated caps on public ICLR 2021–2023 submissions; the authors explicitly distinguish these from actual conference desk-rejection outcomes. Improvement factors were read, but datasets, solver runs, raw retained sets and timing were not reproduced. Unavailable code prevents binding the theoretical algorithm to those results. No real-conference experiment is invented as a prerequisite for the normative argument’s mathematical validity.\par
\section*{Surviving results and conclusion}
The small-author feasibility arguments and explicit impossible ideal-retention instances were checked. Theorem 4.3’s negative statement is existential for \(n\ge3\), not failure of every instance or a theorem about each paper having three authors. Binary matrix formulations and \(\zeta_{\rm avg}\le\zeta_{\rm worst}\) survive. Optimizing that lower bound alone cannot guarantee individual protection; the authors already discuss this limitation.\par
