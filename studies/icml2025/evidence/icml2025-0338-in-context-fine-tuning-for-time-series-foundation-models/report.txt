Title: Scope and quantitative checks for in-context time-series fine-tuning

# Identity, scope, and method

This report reviews Matthew Faw, Rajat Sen, Yichen Zhou and Abhimanyu Das, In-Context Fine-Tuning for Time-Series Foundation Models, ICML 2025, PMLR 267. The frozen 20-page proceedings PDF was read through Sections 1–7 and Appendix A.1–A.9, focusing on model construction, temporal information restrictions, metrics, aggregates and timing. Original source figures on pages 4, 6, 8, 11 and 19 were viewed. Individual trajectories in Figure 8 and every entry of the wide WQL/baseline tables were not independently verified. The source manifest records no located release of this specific method; the upstream TimesFM implementation was not treated as its substitute. No author code, training, forecast or raw dataset was run.

The model embeds length-32 input patches, predicts length-128 output patches, inserts a learned separator between examples, and permits causal attention to preceding examples. Section 3’s inclusion of the target horizon in training notation does not itself expose that horizon to an earlier prediction: the specified causal computation of output \(o_j^{(i)}\) uses the target’s tokens only through \(j\). Its validity also requires masking padded and unavailable values in the actual loader. The paper explicitly defines zero-shot as no gradient update on the unseen task; using observed target-domain histories is consistent with that definition. It is not a claim of having no target-domain information.

Appendix A.9 restricts random context selection to the past history of other series. Under synchronized forecast cutoffs, this is an admissible information set. The absent release prevents checking exact timestamps, asynchronous series, all padding cases and split implementation. No test leakage is demonstrated. Overlapping training windows alone do not prove test leakage. Likewise, the paper’s caveat that unaligned examples are outside Moirai-MV’s intended use is explicit; this auxiliary comparison cannot establish a general superiority over correctly aligned multivariate forecasting.

# Independent numerical checks

For \(L>S\), positive seasonal-naive scale, and forecast horizon \(H\), the stated MASE is the usual ratio \[\frac{H^{-1}\sum_{t=L+1}^{L+H}|\widehat y_t-y_t|}
 {(L-S)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{L-S}|y_{t+S}-y_t|}.\] An independent check with history \((1,2,4,7)\), future \((11,16)\), forecast \((10,14)\) and \(S=1\) gives \(1.5/2=0.75\). The formula requires an explicit convention for zero denominators and histories shorter than the seasonal period; no evidence here establishes that an evaluated series violates those conditions. At quantile \(1/2\), twice the stated pinball loss is absolute error, so the WQL formula reduces to normalized absolute error as expected.

The script `check_aggregates.py` parses all 27 Table 6 rows and removes the four named in-pretraining datasets, leaving 23. It computes the geometric mean of the printed dataset MASE values divided by each dataset’s seasonal-naive value:

TimesFM ICF | 0.779313 | \(0.777\pm0.003\)

The first four columns broadly reconcile at printed precision (Table 2 uses 0.811 for LH). The ICF reconstruction differs by 0.00231. This does not establish erroneous aggregation: the paper says it averages seed-wise geometric means, whereas the calculation above takes a geometric mean of rounded per-dataset means. These operations do not commute, and the geometric mean is concave. Raw paired seed results are needed to resolve the exact difference.

The advertised improvements follow from the aggregate entries: \(100(1-0.777/0.834)=6.8345\%\) and \(100(1-0.777/0.818)=5.0122\%\). The prose’s 2.4% LH improvement does not follow exactly from Table 2, which yields 2.7578%; this small arithmetic discrepancy does not change the ordering and is not treated as a substantive methodological failure. Independently averaging the four ICF horizons in Table 9 yields 0.4045, 0.37825, 0.378 and 0.30725, consistent with the rounded Table 1 rows.

# Unresolved uncertainty and controlled-comparison details

Section 6.2, page 7, describes ten runs and standard-error bars. Appendix A.6, page 14, explicitly describes five evaluations, each averaged over ten random seeds, and labels the Table 6 intervals one standard deviation of the five evaluations. Its final paragraph averages five geometric means. Figure 10 separately calls its bars one standard deviation of evaluations averaged over ten seeds. Thus the appendix does supply a nested arrangement; the remaining uncertainty is which replication level and SD/SE convention apply to the main-figure and table comparisons. Different experiment sets could reconcile the wording, and no particular interval is shown to be wrong. Standard deviation and standard error are not interchangeable: for independent replicate units their ratio is \(\sqrt n\), or 2.236 for \(n=5\) and 3.162 for \(n=10\). Those factors apply at the chosen replication level and cannot be used to convert a nested design without its aggregation details.

Consequently the small ICF–FT difference, 0.001 in Table 6, supports a descriptive statement of similar point estimates, not a verified statistical equivalence claim. Table 1 also bolds entries said to lie within one standard error without printing those errors. This review does not infer that any specific interval or bold entry is wrong. The needed evidence is the experimental unit, seed grouping, SD/SE definition, paired outputs and the precise aggregation operation. This is recorded as Needs Evidence, not a demonstrated false forecasting result.

Section 6.4.2 describes at most five length-512 examples against a length-2048 single-series baseline. Five complete examples contain 2560 observed values, 25% more than 2048, before separators; actual short examples may use less. Thus this is a useful cross-series versus single-series comparison, but is not strictly token- or information-budget matched. Moreover, Table 2 repeats ICF’s 0.777 aggregate while the main experiment and Figure 10 use 50 examples. Equal rounded outcomes are possible; they do not prove the five-example run was omitted. A distinct five-example result and length-matched ablation would resolve this issue. No controlled causal attribution to the separator alone is certified.

# Timing interpretation and disposition

Table 8 compares one sequential pass over the benchmark including fine-tuning. Its 418 minutes versus 25 minutes gives a ratio of 16.72, consistent with the stated approximately 16-fold advantage. Inference alone instead takes 1500 versus 28 seconds, a factor of 53.57 favoring the already fine-tuned model. If the same fine-tuned models are reused for \(K\) equally sized forecast batches without retraining, the reported costs imply \[T_{\rm ICF}=25K,\quad T_{\rm FT}=418+\frac{28}{60}K
 \quad\hbox{minutes},\] with crossover at \(K\simeq17.04\). This conditional calculation is not a new benchmark and does not contradict the paper’s explicitly one-pass timing. The 334-minute row already achieves the same rounded 0.776 score; the reported speed ratio is tied to the selected training budget.

The viewed qualitative forecasts demonstrate the intended ambiguity resolution on selected examples, not population-wide guarantees. The main arithmetic supports a positive average effect and similar ICF/FT point estimates in the reported setup. No substantive defect is established in the inspected architecture or metric definitions. Uncertainty, context budgets and implementation provenance remain open.
