# What was checked

Location | Check and conclusion

Abstract and §1, pp.1–2 | Read the six-claim chain. Incremental capability growth is compatible with eventual high capability. The argument proposes a conditional route to permanent loss of influence, not a universal inevitability theorem.

§2, pp.2–3 | Checked distinction between economic share, absolute purchasing power and effective control. §2.3.2 explicitly allows prosperity with reduced relative influence; absolute deprivation requires additional mechanisms.

§§3–4, pp.3–6 | Read cultural and state pathways as capability/incentive scenarios. Beneficial services and possible efficiency gains are acknowledged. Historical analogies do not establish their future magnitude.

# Selected primary-premise checks

Economy: Korinek–Stiglitz [S3], §14.6, printed pp.383–386, discusses autonomous AI, scarce-resource competition and failure to retain enforceable ownership rights. The proceedings p.8 describes this as a possibility. The cited analysis also considers preservation of rights or income as countermeasures. It supports a conditional mechanism, not an empirical probability or an unconditional automation-to-poverty implication.

Culture: Brinkmann et al. [S4] is a conceptual Perspective. In the checked arXiv v1, Table 1 (physical p.9) marks projections as tentative, and pp.15–17 discuss both human-independent cultural dynamics and possible augmentation or separate niches. This is consistent with the proceedings p.3 possibility language. It does not demonstrate the later extreme scenarios of widespread cultural irrelevance.

States: the institutional abstract of Zuiderwijk et al. [S5] describes a 26-article review dominated by exploratory and conceptual evidence, with a call for empirical impact evaluation. It supports administrative benefits as an adoption incentive; it does not establish universally superior automated governance. The full review was not accessible in this check, so its tables and included studies were not audited.

# Evidence boundaries and unresolved questions

Scope clarification: a lower labor-income share need not reduce absolute income. For example, output rising from 100 to 200 while the share falls from 0.6 to 0.4 raises labor income from 60 to 80. This clarifies p.2 terminology rather than refuting the full argument, because p.3 expressly distinguishes relative loss of influence from absolute deprivation. Prices, ownership, distribution and transfers also matter.

Needs Evidence for stronger predictions: the paper does not quantify when delegation defeats effective oversight, when adverse cross-system feedback dominates corrective institutions, or when reduced influence becomes global and irreversible. The effectiveness of proposed restrictions, representation mechanisms and AI delegates also remains unestablished. These open issues do not by themselves constitute a demonstrated error in a conditional position paper; no severity was assigned to them.

No empirical reproduction, calibrated civilization-scale model, exhaustive reference audit, or complete validation of historical examples was performed. The two placeholder author/title entries on p.9 were traced to real, topically relevant arXiv papers used in the p.8 AI-delegation discussion; their metadata mismatch was excluded as bibliographic-only under the review contract.
