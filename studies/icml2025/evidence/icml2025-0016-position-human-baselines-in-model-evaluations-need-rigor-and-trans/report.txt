# 4. Problem 1: imputed classifications become observed-practice claims

## Source claim and location

Section 4.2, p.5, describes approximately 29% of baselines as having reported recruitment quality control. Section 4.3, p.6, describes approximately 88% and 76% as displaying differences in UI and instructions. Tables 3 and 6, pp.41-42, disclose defaults; Appendix B, pp.45,47-48, defines the coding. The concern is the main-text interpretation, not a hidden imputation procedure. [Wei-PMLR (2025)]

## Complete check

The independent checker reads the XLSX ZIP/XML directly and counts raw, non-formula cells. It does not execute the author notebook or any workbook formula. The following categories reproduce from the pinned raw workbook:

Recruitment QC, D34:DN34 | 13 | 0 | 82 | 20 | 0

Instruction equivalence, D57:DN57 | 23 | 5 | 21 | 66 | 0

All ranges are on Final_Data, and each row totals 115. [Wei-data (2025/06); independent_results.json]

Recruitment QC: the explicit-Yes proportion is 13/115 = 11.30%. The stated default-Yes rule adds 20 unknowns, yielding 33/115 = 28.70%, which explains the main-text 29%. That value describes the classification after imputation; it cannot simultaneously be the proportion that explicitly reported QC.

Instructions: 21/115 = 18.26% are explicitly No, and 66/115 = 57.39% are unknown. Defaulting unknowns to No produces (21+66)/115 = 75.65%, explaining 76%. Five Partial records are separate and are rounded upward under the authors’ rule. This calculation does not establish that 76% were observed to use different instructions.

UI: 37 records are explicitly No, 62 are unknown, and two are N/A. The published Table 6 has 14 Yes and 101 No. Its No total numerically equals 37+62+2; thus it also includes the two N/A records. The explicit-No share is 37/115 = 32.17%, whereas the table’s residual-No share is 101/115 = 87.83%. The formula path that allocated N/A was not inspected. Retaining 115 as the denominator matches the published table; removing N/A would create a different analysis. [Wei-PMLR (2025), Table 6; Wei-data (2025/06)]

## Alternative reading and counterevidence

The authors openly disclose imputation and deliberately choose different defaults for different variables. For example, tool-access unknowns default to Yes. Appendix B frames these defaults as assumptions about prevailing practice; this is evidence against an allegation of concealed or uniformly pessimistic coding. The discrepancy concerns the semantic move from an assumed label to a reported or observed practice.

If the intended outcome is an assumption-dependent assessment of likely rigor, the imputed summaries are interpretable with that qualification. They do not directly measure actual reporting rates or demonstrated procedural differences. The many unknown records independently support concern about transparency.

## Impact and precise repair

Severity: Major. The dependency is explicit: the abstract presents the systematic 115-baseline review as a contribution; sections 4.2 and 4.3 use these rates to describe observed recruitment and evaluation practices; Tables 3 and 6 and the coding defaults generate the cited summaries. Treating unknown records as observations changes the estimand and materially overstates what the released annotations establish about these practices. This is a defect in the core audit’s quantitative evidence, even though the textual correction is bounded. The raw categories establish documented evidence and missingness, not the true practices in unknown cases. The disclosed defaults and the continued support for transparency remain valid counterevidence against stronger accusations.

A verified descriptive repair is to report the explicit counts and missing counts separately, while labeling the 28.70%, 75.65%, and 87.83% values as summaries after the specified coding defaults. A corrected estimate of actual practice cannot be obtained by relabeling unknowns; it requires source re-examination or author information. This repair is arithmetically checked against the frozen workbook, not adopted by the authors.

# 5. Problem 2: precision is used to infer power without a design

## Source claim and suspect step

Section 4.1, p.4, first correctly says that power analysis depends on significance level and a prespecified relevant effect. It then invokes a sample near 1,000 for a survey margin of error and, explicitly conditional on that standard, characterizes the baselines as vastly underpowered using their median sample size of eight. The numerical precision reference does not establish test-specific power across heterogeneous outcomes and effects. The wording must preserve that conditional standard; it is inaccurate to attribute an unqualified universal power theorem to the source. [Wei-PMLR (2025), p.4]

The released data reproduce the descriptive median of 8 and mean of 89.8272 (rounded to 90), using 81 numeric entries in Final_Data!D16:DN16. These are not calculations over 115 fully observed participant counts. The descriptive numbers themselves are not the issue. [Wei-data (2025/06); independent_results.json]

## Fully specified analytic counterexample

Consider eight independent, uniformly sampled humans from a large target population. Each answers one fixed binary task under identical conditions. Let Y_i be independent Bernoulli(p) outcomes and K their sum. Treat the AI reference probability p0 = 0.5 as known; no additional estimated-AI uncertainty is present. Test H0: p=0.5 against a two-sided alternative at nominal alpha=0.05.

The conventional nonrandomized equal-tail exact test rejects at K=0 or K=8:

Code:
Null rejection probability = 2 * (0.5)^8 = 0.0078125.
Adding K=1 and K=7 gives (1+8+8+1)/256 = 0.0703125 > 0.05.
At p=0.99, power = (0.01)^8 + (0.99)^8 = 0.9227446944279202.

Every stipulated sampling assumption holds, and the design is a valid human-baseline comparison. Thus n=8 can provide greater than 92% power for a sufficiently large effect even with a conservative exact test. A normal-approximation survey half-width of 1.96*sqrt(0.25/1000) = 0.03099 describes a different target: precision for a proportion near one-half. [Independent analytic calculation; independent_checks.py]

This exact example shows that small sample size alone does not force low power. It uses a known AI probability, independent Bernoulli outcomes, and a very large prespecified alternative. It does not meet a demand for roughly three-percentage-point survey precision and therefore does not refute the source’s conditional precision standard. It is not a counterexample to a theorem asserted in the paper, nor a power estimate for any of the audited studies.

## Alternative reading, impact, and repair

The explicit qualification to the stated standard supports reading the sentence as a warning about survey precision. The earlier effect-specific power-analysis sentence, the sampling-bias discussion on p.5, and the context-dependent framework on p.9 reinforce that reading. The precise supported concern is conflation of precision and statistical power, plus an unestablished transfer of that survey target to heterogeneous baseline studies; the example does not prove those studies adequately powered.

Severity: Minor within this demonstrated scope. The issue bears on the audit’s sample-size diagnostic, but the source’s express precision condition and correct effect-specific guidance prevent the example from establishing a materially false power conclusion under matching assumptions. The descriptive median remains verified. Retain the sample-size evidence, distinguish precision from power, and state the estimand, effect, variance, dependence, sampling design, and test needed for a power assessment. No actual-study power reanalysis was performed.

# 6. Problem 3: the tool-equivalence rule conflates access and fairness

## Source claim and location

Appendix B.3, Q3.5, p.48, asks whether humans and AI have the same technical tools but instructs coders to count Internet-trained AI without Internet access as equivalent to humans with Internet access. Appendix D, p.53, argues that such an asymmetric comparison can be fair because the model has encountered Internet information. Table 6, p.42, and section 4.3, p.6, use the resulting tool-equivalence variable. [Wei-PMLR (2025)]

## Complete check and original assumptions

Prior training does not supply a live retrieval tool. Let model weights Theta be fixed. After training, draw B uniformly from {0,1}, independently of Theta, and publish B at a known URL. Both participants receive the same task identifying the URL but not B. The human is permitted live retrieval; the model has no retrieval or other channel containing B. Model randomization R is independent of B.

For any binary prediction Y=g(Theta,prompt,R), independence gives P(Y=B)=1/2. An idealized human who successfully retrieves and correctly copies the page obtains B with probability 1. The example deliberately assumes successful lookup; it does not assert measured human performance.

All conditions of the codebook exception hold: the human has Internet access, the AI does not, and the AI was Internet-trained. The rule nevertheless labels unequal operational access as equal. The failure of equivalence is already present in the allowed tools; the bit example additionally shows why prior exposure cannot resolve every information-access difference.

## Counterevidence, impact, and repair

The authors’ fairness argument can be reasonable for a specified research question. Fairness of an asymmetric design, however, is distinct from literal equality of permitted tools. The report does not claim that every such comparison is scientifically inappropriate.

Severity: Minor within the demonstrated scope. This is a construct-labeling defect in one audit variable: its operational rule permits different live tools while the question asks about the same tools. The rule can instead be understood as a proposed fairness proxy; the report does not disprove that normative choice. No affected observed row or materially changed aggregate rate has been established, so the analytic example does not establish a false corpus-level result. The released row59 has 34 Yes, 12 No, and 69 Unknown/Unreported; default Yes yields 103/115 Yes. The number of explicit-Yes records using the exception and any corrected percentage remain undetermined.

Repair: record operational tool access separately from the rationale for a fair asymmetric comparison. The example must be unequal in the tool-access field; the fairness rationale may remain in a separate field. Recode affected source articles and recompute dependent statistics before publishing a new percentage. The conceptual distinction is verified analytically; corpus-level repair remains unverified.
