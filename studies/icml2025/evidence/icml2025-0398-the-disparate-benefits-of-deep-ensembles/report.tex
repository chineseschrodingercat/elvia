Abstract: The paper, its complete appendices and the principal supplied analysis programs were inspected. Some signed fairness differences are interpreted as nonnegative violations, published numerical summaries disagree, and the current controlled experiment changes test inputs between ensemble members. The supplied face post-processing comparison also applies thresholds to the wrong member. An appendix likelihood identity fails even for one observation. These findings require corrected definitions, a reconciled experimental record and reruns of the affected analyses. They do not disprove the narrower possibility that ensembling improves average performance while worsening a group disparity. Scientific adjudication is pending.\par

\# Supported findings\par
Problem 1 (A signed difference is not a nonnegative fairness violation).  Equations (5)–(6), p. 4, define SPD and EOD as signed rate differences. The accompanying claim that designating \(A=1\) as advantaged makes every metric lie in \([0,1]\) is not satisfied by the results. Figure 9(f), p. 19, displays \(1-\mathrm{EOD}>1\), with the curve rising farther above one as members are added. Table 1 treats the corresponding negative change as a decrease in fairness violation.\par
For FF target race, group gender, Table 1 gives \(\Delta\mathrm{EOD}=-0.008\), while the before-HPP entries in Table 10, p. 27, give positive disparity magnitudes \(0.019\) for members and \(0.027\) for the ensemble. Signed gaps \(-0.019\) and \(-0.027\) reproduce both the plotted direction and the reported signed change; the absolute violation instead increases by \(0.008\). The source `metrics.py`, lines 132–148, uses signed EOD/SPD by default. The face analysis, lines 131–142, plots one minus those signed quantities. Hence this is supported by definitions, visible axes, table entries and implementation, rather than an assumption that all negative changes must be wrong.\par
This Major finding affects which tasks are called fairer and the interpretation of statistically significant disparity changes. A group advantaged for one target or rate need not be advantaged for every metric, model and distribution. Fix the group identities, report signed direction separately, and compute absolute SPD/EOD per run before averaging and testing; \(|\mathbb E d|\) need not equal \(\mathbb E|d|\). AOD already uses absolute differences and is not affected by this particular defect. Positive-gap FF age examples and several AOD results can still support the narrower disparate-benefits claim.\par
Problem 2 (The reported main summaries and supplied analysis are not mutually reproducible).  Table 1 identifies its changes as the difference between the ten-member ensembles in Table 3 and the members in Table 4. For FF age/gender, those latter tables give accuracy \(0.812\) and \(0.794\), a difference \(0.018\), whereas Table 1 gives \(0.022\). The analogous SPD, EOD and AOD differences are \(0.017\), \(0.012\) and \(0.013\), versus \(0.022\), \(0.017\) and \(0.017\) in Table 1.\par
Differences of \(0.004\)–\(0.005\) exceed the \(0.0015\) maximum discrepancy attributable to rounding three quantities to three decimals. Table 5, p. 26, instead reports the pre-HPP ensemble accuracy as \(0.816\), consistent with Table 1’s accuracy change and inconsistent with Table 3’s stated ten-member quantity. The FF accuracy trajectory in Figures 1 and 9 also lies near the UTK trajectory rather than the FF tabulated values; the race panels make this especially clear. The face notebook explicitly plots `utk\_test\_fm\_accs` in its FF figure at lines 196–199. These are material dataset/aggregation inconsistencies, not evidence that the overall performance gain vanishes.\par
There are two further decisive release defects. Cell 8 averages the complete run-by-ensemble-size arrays when printing an ensemble summary, instead of selecting size ten. Cell 7 computes a fairness change by subtracting a raw member violation from a stored \(1-F\) ensemble quantity (lines 264–275); the medical notebook repeats this at lines 174–181. If both violations equal \(0.1\), this expression yields \(0.8\) although the correct change is zero. That defect cannot itself generate Table 1’s small numbers, so the historical program must not simply be presumed identical. This is a Major reproducibility and statistical-evidence problem. Supply the actual versioned analysis, run-level paired values and dataset identities; regenerate all affected plots, summaries and significance calls after making the sign convention and ensemble size consistent. The five-run design permits paired inference, but neither the displayed standard deviations nor the flawed released expression independently verify the published \(p\)-values.\par
Problem 3 (The controlled experiment averages different inputs across members).  The controlled-experiment notebook samples a new same-label partner inside `ConcatenateFMNIST.\_\_getitem\_\_` whenever `same\_image=False` (lines 59–69). Its test dataset includes this stochastic branch (189–190). Evaluation then loops over networks outside a newly iterated data loader (214–225), without caching the paired images or resetting a common sample mapping. Probabilities are stacked and averaged by test index as if every member had received the same input.\par
For group \(A=0\), both copies of the image are fixed. For \(A=1\), each member can receive a different second image. The latter average therefore combines model variation with extra test views, and its computed diversity is not exclusively variation across predictors at fixed \(x\), as Equation (8) requires. Even identical models can give apparent diversity: one fixed function outputting true-label probabilities \(0.9\) and \(0.1\) on two sampled views produces Jensen gap \(\log(5/3)>0\) when those views are mixed, although its fixed-input ensemble diversity is zero. This establishes the confounding mechanism without running the supplied program or claiming a particular numerical bias for the published trained networks.\par
This is Major for the controlled confirmation in Section 6 and Figure 4. Materialize and hash a fixed evaluation dataset shared by every member; distinguish test-view averaging from model ensembling; recompute group diversity and rates for the first controlled experiment. Identify whether its historical evaluation included this resampling, and establish the separate interpolation-sweep implementation before attributing this specific defect to Table 2. Even with that repair, supplying a second independent class-conditional image and interpolating it with noise change information content and task difficulty as well as the diversity statistic. A causal claim that diversity alone explains the disparity needs a corresponding control. The acquired notebook contains the first controlled experiment; a complete implementation of the interpolation sweep in Figure 5 was not established.\par
Problem 4 (The face post-processing baseline uses another member’s scores).  In the face HPP notebook, cell 13 fits a post-processor to validation predictions for member `mem` (line 273), but evaluates it on `ff\_test\_probits[m][0]` (line 300). Cell 18 repeats the error for UTK, fitting member `mem` at line 481 and evaluating member zero at line 504. Thus different members’ learned thresholds are applied to the first member’s score scale.\par
This directly compromises the comparison used to argue that ensembles benefit more than individual members from HPP. Consider two members with negative/positive scores \((0.1,0.4)\) and \((0.6,0.9)\) and their correct thresholds \(0.25\) and \(0.75\). Both have accuracy one; applying both thresholds to the first member yields accuracies one and one half. The example establishes a possible artificial baseline degradation, not the empirical size or sign of the error on FF/UTK. A version mismatch remains a plausible explanation for the release. This Major finding requires the member index to be preserved from fitting through evaluation, identity checks on prediction files, and regenerated matched HPP comparisons using the actual experimental source. The separate medical HPP implementation was not audited, so this specific error is not asserted there. The inspected face code does correctly multiply the AOD tolerance by two when using an equalized-odds \(L^1\) constraint, consistent with Equation (7).\par
Problem 5 (The Bayesian likelihood-ratio derivation interchanges log and expectation).  Appendix C defines \(p(D')=\mathbb E_w\prod_{k=1}^K p(y_k\mid x_k,w)\) and \(\bar p(D')=\prod_{k=1}^K\mathbb E_w p(y_k\mid x_k,w)\). Equation (15), p. 16, then identifies \(\log(\bar p(D')/p(D'))\) with \(K\overline{\mathrm{DIV}}\). This is false even for a finite posterior with two equally weighted parameter values and \(K=1\).\par
Take likelihoods \(0.9\) and \(0.1\). Both marginal likelihoods equal \(0.5\), so the log ratio is zero, whereas \(\overline{\mathrm{DIV}}=\log(0.5)-\tfrac12\log(0.9)-\tfrac12\log(0.1)=\log(5/3)\). No Monte Carlo approximation is involved. For two observations with those same likelihood columns, the log ratio is \(\log(0.25/0.41)<0\), while \(2\overline{\mathrm{DIV}}>0\). The missing term is the joint-likelihood Jensen gap: \[K\overline{\mathrm{DIV}}=\log\frac{\bar p(D')}{p(D')}+\log\mathbb E_w L(w)-\mathbb E_w\log L(w),\qquad L(w)=\prod_k p(y_k\mid x_k,w).\] This is Minor in overall scope because Equation (8)’s predictive-diversity definition and its exact NLL improvement remain valid without the incorrect Bayesian interpretation. Replace the derivation and its stated interpretation; a typographical repair to the approximation sign is insufficient. The factorization in Equation (11), p. 15, also needs the appropriate label prior in the chain rule rather than multiplying two input conditionals.\par
\# Additional checks and limits\par
Predictive diversity in Equation (8) is a nonnegative Jensen gap and exactly the improvement from mean member NLL to ensemble NLL at fixed inputs. It does not imply any particular accuracy change: true-label probabilities \(0.6\) and \(0.9\) give positive diversity while both members and their ensemble classify correctly. Group-wise associations in Figures 3 and 15–17 therefore provide evidence about the studied distributions, not a distribution-free link to binary decisions.\par
The proposed calibration explanation is also empirical rather than necessary. Perfect scores \(S=Y\in\{0,1\}\) are calibrated but insensitive to every interior decision threshold; accurate but miscalibrated scores \(0.49\) and \(0.51\) are sensitive near \(0.5\). Monotone score transforms can alter calibration and numerical threshold sensitivity while preserving ranking and the attainable threshold classifiers. Figure 30 was inspected, and the paper itself acknowledges similar threshold behavior for CX members and ensembles (p. 38). A calibrated-versus-uncalibrated intervention on matched rankings would better distinguish the proposed mechanism. The change from AUROC to balanced accuracy for CX HPP is explicitly disclosed on p. 18 and is not treated here as a hidden metric substitution.\par
The medical split is made by image indices in the supplied training script (lines 75–90); patient identifiers exist in the loader, but patient-level grouping is not used there. With no raw identifiers or split files, patient overlap and its numerical effect were not verified. Publish an overlap audit or patient-disjoint evaluation to bound that uncertainty. This review does not assert an observed leakage event. Similarly, it did not rerun training, inspect every ancillary notebook, reconstruct the manual hyperparameter search, or establish historical correspondence for any current code defect.\par
