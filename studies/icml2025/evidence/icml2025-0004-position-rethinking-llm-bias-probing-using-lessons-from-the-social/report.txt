Appendix A.2-A.3, pp.19-20 | Association-first screening is conditional on assumptions about alignment. Four correlation/RLHF/domain predictions are explicitly hypotheses; Section 5 acknowledges the need for empirical validation.

A negative association result is not a validated stopping rule or certificate of unbiased deployment. The work supplies neither a calibrated negative predictive value nor a monotonicity theorem. Its proposed RLHF explanation is not causally isolated from model, data, prompt and scoring differences. These limits qualify the framework’s practical use; untested hypotheses alone are not treated as errors.

# 3. Evidence question: what does no observed bias cover?

Target locations: Section 4.4, p.8, and Appendix A.2, p.19. Both contrast strong association bias with an absence of evidence of bias from LLM BTA naturalistic probing. Table 2, p.7, illustrates BTA using relative hiring choices. The inspected author-hosted Morehouse et al. study [2] separates absolute ratings from relative hiring. Its PDF p.5 reports matching aggregate absolute means (81.2), but also reports job-specific relative-hiring bias in 11 of 30 occupations, smaller than association-level bias; the remaining 19 are nonsignificant. These are the source’s reported results, not independently reanalyzed significance claims. Multiplicity control was not established by this review.

An interpretation restricted to aggregate absolute scores, or to most occupations, is defensible. The target does not make that restriction explicit, and its relative-hiring example supports a broader reading. The inspected study is linked under the exact 2024 workshop entry on its author’s publication page [3], so study identity is supported. However, the cited OpenReview artifact Fg6qZ28Jym returned a browser challenge/HTTP 403. The saved author PDF was not verified byte-identical to the historical workshop file. Consequently this is retained as Needs Evidence, not an established version-specific error.

The precise implication is limited: the cited illustration may describe attenuation and measurement-dependent effects rather than a categorical absence of downstream bias. Mixed outcomes would still support the paper’s broader task- and context-dependent argument. Resolve the concern by identifying the cited file and specifying the metric and aggregation level. If it matches the inspected copy, a supported formulation would state that absolute ratings were similar while relative-hiring effects persisted for some occupations. No author-adopted correction is claimed.

The introductory Armstrong comparison also needs a narrow reading. In arXiv:2405.04412v3 (15 November 2024), Section 4.1 and Tables 4-6 report nonsignificant applicant-gender coefficients for the three assessed rating outcomes; these are difference tests, not equivalence tests [4]. Similar scores in that setup do not establish equality or absence of every gender-related disparity. Its GPT-3.5 model and resume task differ from Morehouse’s GPT-4 association task, so their contrast is illustrative rather than a controlled effect of probe level.
