# 2. Executive assessment

The paper’s methodological position is that continual-RL evaluation should test performance beyond the horizon used to select hyperparameters. It supports this position with Catch, Cartpole, a SAC quadruped task, and Jelly Bean World. The defect established here concerns the advertised SAC extension: p. 2 presents SAC as testing the effects of both limited and lifetime tuning, but the reported SAC comparison uses literature defaults as the reference, while the short-horizon search cannot even select those defaults. [E1, pp. 2, 6-7]

Changing only selection duration within the published SAC candidate set cannot reproduce the reference configuration. Consequently the SAC performance gap mixes selection horizon with candidate-space and default-origin differences. This is a demonstrated inferential defect in an explicitly advertised extension of the paper’s main empirical evidence; Major is assigned to that claim. No deterioration of the published scores, execution bug, or failure of the entire paper is alleged.

The actual Figure 6 default baseline and mitigation comparisons remain valid descriptive results within their stated scope. Separate DQN and Jelly Bean World evidence is not invalidated by the SAC comparison problem. Rejected and unresolved concerns are retained without being converted into additional defects.

# 3. Definitions, operative assumptions, and scope

Lifetime tuning selects hyperparameters using performance over the full experimental horizon n. Limited tuning selects them using an initial horizon j and evaluates the selected configuration over n. For a numerical percentage k, the intended budget is j = floor((k/100)n). Section 5 prints floor(kn) while declaring k in [0,100], but Section 8 correctly gives 150,000 and 300,000 steps for 10% and 20% of 1.5 million. The isolated normalization inconsistency is recorded, not counted as a substantive finding. [E1, pp. 5, 7]

A tuning-duration effect means changing the selection horizon while holding the candidate family and other relevant selection/evaluation conditions fixed. This is the controlled interpretation needed to attribute a performance difference to duration. The review does not invent an explicit same-grid promise in Section 7: the actual, transparently described default comparison does not establish the broader duration attribution advertised on p. 2.

Let H denote candidate configurations, J-hat_t(h) the selection score of h over t steps, and J_n(h) its independent full-horizon evaluation score. The relevant selections are h_short in argmax over H of J-hat_j(h) and h_life in argmax over H of J-hat_n(h). The paper instead reports a comparison involving h_default, an externally supplied configuration. This notation formalizes the comparison; it is not a theorem attributed to the authors.

The review comprises document analysis, visual inspection, an exact finite parameter-membership check, and a targeted later-version comparison. No author code was run, no agent was trained, and no empirical result was reproduced. No dedicated public experiment-code release or raw result bundle was identified through the publisher page, PDF, arXiv record, or inspected author publication entry. OpenReview access failures limit supplement and revision-thread coverage. [E2-E7]

# 4. Problem 1: the SAC comparison does not identify the advertised tuning-duration effect

Source claim and location. In the upper-right paragraph of p. 2, immediately before Section 2, the paper describes the SAC study as an extension to another algorithm and continuous actions that tests the impact of k-percent and lifetime tuning, with similar outcomes. Section 7, p. 6, actually specifies comparison to previously reported default values; Figure 6 on p. 7 labels the reference curve Default. Table 3 defines the short-horizon search and Table 4 gives the configurations. [E1]

Suspect step. The default-versus-short-tuned contrast is used as evidence about the effect of restricting tuning duration. The reference configuration is not a full-horizon selection over the reported short-horizon candidate family. This is not an unknown code-provenance inference: the mismatch follows directly from the reported tables.

Complete finite check. Table 3 contains seven learning rates, five batch sizes, three replay-buffer sizes, two beta2 values, and two Adam epsilon values. Its Cartesian product has 7 x 5 x 3 x 2 x 2 = 420 configurations, if all combinations are used. The conclusion requires only coordinate membership and does not assume all 420 were executed. Exact rational arithmetic confirms the following. [E4]

The one-percent-selected configuration is learning rate 0.001, batch size 512, buffer size 10,000, beta2 0.9, and epsilon 0.00000001; every coordinate belongs to the stated search. The default learning rate 0.0003 is absent and its buffer is 100 times the largest available buffer. Thus h_default is not in H, whereas h_short is in H. No increase of tuning duration within H can select h_default. These assertions use printed values, not assumptions about hidden code.

The desired contrast is Delta_duration = J_n(h_life) - J_n(h_short). The reported contrast is Delta_reported = J_n(h_default) - J_n(h_short). Subtraction gives Delta_reported - Delta_duration = J_n(h_default) - J_n(h_life). The reported SAC experiment does not determine this last term. The identity establishes the attribution gap without inventing learning curves or claiming that the observed direction must reverse. [E4]

Alternative reading and counterevidence. Section 7 is explicit about defaults, and Figure 6 labels the curve correctly. As a comparison of transferred defaults against limited-tuning configurations and mitigations, the experiment is legitimate. Historical defaults could themselves have been chosen using long experiments; that does not make them the full-horizon optimum of the same candidate family on the switching quadruped task. If p. 2 is interpreted only as an imprecise roadmap, the appropriate action is to narrow that claim to the default-transfer comparison. The report does not label the Figure 6 legend or descriptive comparison erroneous.

Impact and judgment. The affected dependency is: advertised SAC corroboration across algorithm/action domains -> interpretation of the default gap as an effect of limiting tuning -> claimed empirical support beyond DQN. The second step is unsupported by the actual design, so that specific central extension fails. Major applies to this invalid empirical attribution. A position-paper label, surviving DQN results, or easy textual narrowing does not restore the missing comparison.

Precisely supported repair. Textual narrowing can describe Figure 6 as comparison to published defaults plus mitigation performance under one-percent tuning; this restores fidelity to the experiment but relinquishes the SAC duration-effect demonstration. Restoring that demonstration requires short- and full-horizon selection over a common predeclared candidate family, a consistent selection protocol, and fresh evaluation trials. Including the default would allow its role to be tested. This experiment was not performed here, and no outcome is predicted.

Repair status. The logical adequacy of the narrowing is checked; no manuscript was edited, no adoption was requested, and no empirical repair is verified. An unreported matched experiment could change the assessment, but the review neither assumes it exists nor claims it was never performed.

# 5. Checked evidence that survives; rejected and unresolved candidates

Catch and DQN. Figures 1-3 distinguish default parameters in a changed environment, tuning both algorithms for 500,000 steps, and extending the selected configurations to 10 million steps. The visual trajectories agree with the descriptive account of a later DQN collapse and sustained W0-DQN performance. The SAC defect does not invalidate these separate comparisons. No seed-level reproduction was done. [E1, pp. 3-4]

Cartpole and mitigations. The stationary Cartpole task tests extended learning stability, although Section 2 motivates continual RL using changing optimal behavior. This difference in scope does not falsify the observed long-horizon contrast. Figure 5 describes mixed, environment-dependent mitigation performance and includes failures; the paper does not claim all mitigations work everywhere. [E1, pp. 5-6]

Jelly Bean World. Section 8 explicitly separates five tuning trials from thirty new evaluation trials. The 10%, 20%, and full-horizon budgets are consistent. DQN has 25 learning-rate candidates; W0-DQN and PT-DQN each have 5 x 5 = 25 combinations. Equal candidate count does not prove equal wall-clock cost, but the arithmetic is correct. Figure 7 supports the stated sensitivity of relative performance to tuning window, with acknowledged late recovery differences even under lifetime tuning. [E1, pp. 7-8; E4]

Partial-observability language. Section 3’s suggestion that repeated tuning can remove the need for continual learning is stronger than a general POMDP result. However, no universal theorem is established, and the paper’s own Jelly Bean World discussion acknowledges continued relearning. The operative concern is benchmark-specific designer adaptation. This is recorded as scope clarification rather than a counterexample to an unstated universal theorem.

Unresolved empirical provenance. Earlier experiments do not document every seed-selection and bootstrap detail in the supplied text. That absence does not demonstrate leakage or false intervals. Figure 6 reports standard error over ten runs; Figures 4, 5, and 7 describe bootstrap confidence intervals. These different summaries were not treated as interchangeable. Exact score and interval recalculation remains unperformed.

Normative recommendation. Section 9 treats k-percent tuning as an illustrative alternative, acknowledges its limits as a deployment simulation, and calls for improved methods. No experiment was demanded merely to justify that normative recommendation. The substantive issue concerns empirical support the paper actually advertises.
