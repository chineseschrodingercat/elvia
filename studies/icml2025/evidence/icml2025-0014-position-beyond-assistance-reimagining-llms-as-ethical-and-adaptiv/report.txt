# Summary and definitions

Supported outcome: Minor, limited to two empirical-evidence descriptions. The Fraser study cannot demonstrate mental-health-crisis misclassification because its eligibility excluded mental-health presentations. The cited Woebot trial does not establish a comparative anxiety benefit. Neither finding invalidates the proposed SAFE-i implementation principles or the rationale for human-centered evaluation. No Major issue is demonstrated. Additional metric and transfer concerns remain Scope Clarification or Needs Evidence and do not increase supported severity.

SAFE-i is an implementation proposal spanning data, engineering, and integration. HAAS-e proposes criteria, score families, and future validation methods. R_llm denotes a model response; C_user, E_human, C_culture, and U_history denote concerns, expert evaluations, cultural context, and history. Align is a schematic scoring function: Appendix A permits expert, learned, or similarity-based estimators. Thus the four names do not yet identify one standardized, calibrated instrument. The paper’s explicit human oversight, escalation, proposed trials, and Appendix B limitations constrain any literal reading of its assurances.

# Problem 1: Emergency-triage evidence transferred to an excluded population

Source claim and location. The Introduction, p.2, attributes unsafe triage and misclassification of urgent mental-health crises to Fraser et al. (2023). Section 4, Challenge 3, p.5, later characterizes that study as examining only diagnostic accuracy. The suspect step is transferring an emergency-medicine result to a population not included in the cited study [E1, E2].

Complete source check. Fraser’s Methods, Data Source, restricts eligibility to adult, English-speaking emergency patients with nontrauma, nonmental-health problems and ESI levels 2–5. Its Study Design and Table 2 report 37 triage cases; ChatGPT 3.5 was rated unsafe in 15, approximately 41%. The analysis used previously collected patient symptom data entered into the LLM. It measured diagnostic and triage performance, including unsafe triage. Consequently, the study supports a general triage-risk example, but it cannot provide observed mental-crisis performance in an excluded cohort.

Counterreading, impact, and precise clarification. The authors may intend a warning by analogy. Indeed, their p.4 discussion correctly identifies emergency-department diagnostic and triage evaluation; the statement that this was not direct patient–LLM interaction is defensible. Preserve that general caution, but identify the study’s nonmental-health cohort and describe application to mental-health crises as a hypothesis needing its own evidence. This repairs the evidence attribution only. It does not establish that LLM crisis triage is safe, validate a new model, or overturn the framework proposal. Judgment: Minor local evidence-scope defect.

# Problem 2: Anxiety improvement described as demonstrated intervention efficacy

Source claim and location. Section 3, p.4, describes Woebot as having shown efficacy in alleviating depression and anxiety, citing Fitzpatrick et al. (2017). The relevant distinction is improvement observed over time versus a benefit attributable to the intervention compared with its control [E1, E3].

Complete source check. The source randomized 70 young adults to a two-week Woebot intervention or information-only control. Its Results, Table 2, reports a depression difference (PHQ-9, P=.017) but no significant anxiety difference (GAD-7, P=.581). The Completer Analysis reports anxiety improvement over time in both groups (P=.004). A within-time improvement shared by both arms does not establish an intervention-specific anxiety effect. The Methods describe a decision-tree conversational system with embedded NLP, so the study also cannot directly validate generative-LLM efficacy.

Counterreading, impact, and precise clarification. If the target sentence means only that participants’ anxiety improved, the source supports that narrower descriptive reading. The target also introduces Woebot under conversational AI, so merely including this older system is not independently treated as a model-category defect. Replace the efficacy summary with preliminary comparative evidence for depression, alongside anxiety improvement in both arms without a demonstrated between-arm benefit. Nonsignificance is not proof of no anxiety effect. Judgment: Minor bounded overstatement of this study’s efficacy evidence; no change to the general case for evaluating collaborative tools.

# Measurement checks and unresolved transfer claims

## Appendix A: correct arithmetic and explicitly illustrative inputs

On p.16, CES=(1.00+0.80)/2=0.90, CSI=0.90 is an assumed expert score, PAS=3/5=0.60, and ASA=40/50=0.80. Independent rational arithmetic reproduces these values. Equal weighting is a valid linear combination, consistent with §6.2. Dividing a 1–5 score by five maps it into [0.2,1]; a zero-anchored scale is not required by the stated example. The appendix explicitly marks scenario details as illustrative. Its 50-session example is not a reported completed pilot; cultural context, history, and ratings cannot be reconstructed from the dialogue alone. No arithmetic or fabrication finding is supported.

## ASA identifies adherence; safety and causal benefit require other measurements

\[\operatorname{ASA}=P(A=1\mid R_{\rm llm}),\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{ASA}}=40/50=0.80.\] Here A is completion of a prespecified beneficial action. Consider two hypothetical populations of 50 sessions, each with 40 action completions. Their ASA is identical even if an independently defined adverse-outcome count is zero in one population and ten in the other. The recommended action remains beneficial in both; the formula contains no constraint fixing other adverse outcomes. Hence ASA alone does not identify an overall safety profile.

For a separate causal check, let A(1) and A(0) be beneficial-action outcomes with the LLM response and with a specified comparator. In both hypothetical worlds, 40 of 50 users have A(1)=1. In world X, A(0)=A(1) for everyone, so mean[A(1)-A(0)]=0. In world Y, A(0)=0 for everyone, so the mean effect is 0.80. Both have observed ASA=0.80. All outcomes are binary, cohort size and beneficial-action definition are fixed, and no medical data are simulated or claimed. This proves nonidentification from adherence alone, not failure of HAAS-e as a whole.

Decisive counterevidence: §6.3, p.9, separately proposes randomized comparisons, expert safety reviews, and red teaming; §5.3, p.7, includes escalation and monitoring. These can supply information missing from ASA. The proper outcome is Scope Clarification, with measurement validation pending. No invented composite safety score or clinically validated repair is supplied.

## Standardization and clinical transfer remain unverified

The Align estimators, score anchors, aggregation, rater reliability, population sampling, and clinically meaningful thresholds remain implementation choices. Appendix A explicitly allows alternative estimators, while §7 calls the frameworks a starting point. Different implementations should not be assumed directly comparable until those choices and validation are fixed. This is unresolved operationalization of a proposal, not an algebraic contradiction.

Section 5.2, pp.6–7, extends ToT/CoT to reduced hallucinations and safer emotionally sensitive reasoning. Yao et al.’s inspected experimental inventory is Game of 24, creative writing, and mini crosswords; its Discussion explicitly limits the study to three relatively simple tasks [E4]. Wei et al.’s abstract describes arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning [E5]. These sources motivate candidate techniques but do not establish the asserted clinical transfer. Treat the mental-health benefit as Needs Evidence requiring task-specific testing; this audit does not prove that such techniques fail.

Selected supporting checks: Bedi et al.’s 519-study review supports the 5% real-patient-evaluation statistic, across healthcare rather than specifically mental health or training data [E6]. HAILEY supports improved expressed empathy in a nonclinical N=300 peer-support trial, without validating this paper’s new metrics or clinical outcomes [E7]. Varghese’s 53% moderate-trust and 34.8% future-outlook figures appear in a survey of 466 Indian volunteers; they do not identify global clinical adoption or effectiveness [E8]. The original GE/MIT adoption table was inaccessible. The target’s 48%, 21%, and 27% categories need source/denominator clarification; 21+27=48 alone does not establish an error.
