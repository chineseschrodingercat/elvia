Abstract: The checked publisher paper describes a weakly supervised video-anomaly detector that uses generated descriptions as features and as human-readable explanations. The supported overall assessment is Minor: two XD-Violence averages cannot be obtained from their displayed components, several performance statements disagree with the paper’s own tables, and the literal normal-similarity inference rule has the opposite score orientation from anomaly confidence. Recomputing the displayed averages preserves Ex-VAD’s leading average rank; the score-rule issue does not establish that a reversed rule was deployed. The trainable label pathway, fusion implementation, explanation faithfulness, selection protocol, and full computation boundary remain Needs Evidence. No verified author repository or raw benchmark artifacts are staged. The report uses independent arithmetic, mathematical checks, actual source-page images, and bounded cross-checks; it does not claim experimental reproduction or a demonstrated core-model failure.

# Method and the claims being checked

The anomaly-explanation generation module (AEGM) captions sampled frames using BLIP-2, replaces captions by the highest image–text similarity match within the video’s caption set, and prompts a language model to summarize unusual events. The explanation \(E\) is encoded and fused with temporally adapted visual features. A coarse classifier predicts anomaly confidence. A separate label-augmentation and alignment module (LAAM) compares fused frame features with embeddings of original and expanded class labels.

The stated losses comprise binary cross-entropy on video-level top-\(K\) scores, multiclass cross-entropy on top-\(K\) label similarities, and a nonnegative-cosine penalty between normal and anomalous label embeddings. Under nonzero cosine inputs, positive temperature, and valid probabilities, the cosine, BCE, softmax, and multiclass-cross-entropy algebra is standard in the inspected equations. No formal theorem or exact guarantee of caption correction, causal explanation, or universal detection performance is given.

The evaluation distinguishes coarse frame-level AUC/AP from fine-grained event mAP at IoU thresholds \(0.1,\ldots,0.5\). Fine-grained mAP is explicitly computed only on abnormal test videos, p. 6. Its average therefore should not be interpreted as a comprehensive measure of normal-video false alarms; the separate coarse metrics remain relevant. This disclosed evaluation scope is not itself an error.

# Supported problems

Problem 1 (Reported averages and comparative statements are internally inconsistent).  Section 4.1, p. 6, defines AVG as the average mAP over the five reported IoU thresholds. Table 2’s XD-Violence AVG entries for Ex-VAD and VadCLIP disagree with the arithmetic of those same rows. Section 4.2, p. 7, additionally gives UCF-Crime values and gains inconsistent with Tables 1, 5, and 6.

Complete calculation. For displayed values \(x_1,\ldots,x_5\), the defined average is \(\bar x=\frac15\sum_i x_i\). Exact decimal arithmetic gives: \[\begin{aligned}
 \bar x_{\rm Ex,XD}
 &=\frac{40.14+32.75+28.78+20.15+18.35}{5}
 =28.034,\\
 \bar x_{\rm Vad,XD}
 &=\frac{37.03+30.84+23.38+17.09+14.31}{5}
 =24.530.\end{aligned}\] The printed AVG values are \(28.23\) and \(24.70\), with discrepancies \(0.196\) and \(0.170\) percentage points. If each component and the final AVG were rounded independently to two decimal places, the maximum difference between the printed AVG and the average of printed components would be \(0.005+0.005=0.010\). Both discrepancies exceed this conservative allowance.

For UCF-Crime, the Ex-VAD components average \[(16.51+12.35+9.41+7.82+4.65)/5=10.148,\] which is consistent with the tables’ \(10.15\), but not the prose’s \(9.00\). The displayed AVG cells \(10.15\), \(6.68\), and \(6.48\) give improvements \(3.47\) and \(3.67\) over VadCLIP and STPrompt, whereas the prose gives \(1.32\) and \(1.52\). Computing all three means from their threshold cells gives gains \(3.466\) and \(3.668\), the same discrepancy at the relevant precision.

The parent independently audited all 26 AVG rows in Tables 1, 2, 5, and 6. Only the two Table 2 averages exceed the conservative rounding tolerance. In particular, Table 5’s caption mean \(10.874\) versus printed \(10.88\), and Table 2’s STPrompt mean \(23.448\) versus printed \(23.44\), are compatible with unprinted precision and are not counted as errors. The inputs, complete row audit, and results are preserved in and .

Counterevidence and exact impact. The average of Ex-VAD’s displayed XD cells remains greater than every listed baseline’s recomputed average. Its recomputed margin over VadCLIP is \(28.034-24.530=3.504\) points. Its UCF average likewise remains highest among Table 1’s rows. Thus these checks invalidate the exact reported averages/margins as a mutually consistent set; they do not reverse the displayed average ranking.

A different aggregation, stale threshold row, or mismatched experimental run could account for the disagreement. Such an explanation requires a corrected definition or provenance, rather than treating the inconsistent numbers as the same experiment. The arithmetic of displayed cells is not a replacement measurement of the historical model. Raw predictions, evaluation code, and run identifiers are needed to decide which fields should change.

The superiority language also needs scope. At XD IoU \(0.2\), Ex-VAD’s \(32.75\) is below CLIP-TSA’s \(32.88\), so it is not best at every threshold. Table 3 reports VERA’s XD AP as \(88.2\), above Ex-VAD’s \(86.52\), and p. 7 explicitly calls Ex-VAD second best there. Page 8 reports TCVADS UCF AUC \(88.58\), above Ex-VAD’s \(88.29\), and acknowledges the difference; TCVADS is omitted from Table 3. These are the paper’s own numbers, not independently verified external baseline results. The evidence supports leading reported fine-grained AVG among the listed methods, with several disclosed coarse/threshold exceptions. The supported severity is Minor for this bounded numerical and reporting inconsistency.

Problem 2 (The literal normal-similarity rule reverses anomaly ordering).  Section 3.6, p. 6, describes two coarse-score branches. The first is the direct anomaly classifier. The second is stated as the similarity to the normal class minus \(1\). Taken literally, if \(r\) is normal-class similarity, this is \(a=r-1\). Under the paper’s higher-is-anomalous convention, subtracting a constant preserves normality ordering instead of reversing it.

Complete check and assumptions. Let the normal and anomalous class vectors be \(L_N=(1,0)\) and \(L_A=(-1,0)\). Let the fused vectors of a normal frame and anomalous frame be \(f_N=L_N\) and \(f_A=L_A\). All vectors are nonzero and unit norm, so cosine similarity is well defined. Normal-class similarities are \(r_N=1\) and \(r_A=-1\). The class penalty in Eq. (10) is zero, since \(\max(0,L_N^\top L_A)=0\). These are admitted vectors in the described similarity calculation; they are not asserted to be outputs of the historical trained CLIP pipeline.

The literal rule gives \(a_N=0\), \(a_A=-2\). With anomaly as the positive class and larger scores indicating anomaly, the AUC for this positive/negative pair is \(0\). The decreasing transform \(a'=1-r\) gives \(a'_N=0\), \(a'_A=2\), and AUC \(1\). Repeating each vector over any number of frames leaves this ordering unchanged and permits any valid top-\(K\) selection. More generally, \(a'=-a\), so reversing every pair’s order yields \[\operatorname{AUC}(1-r)=1-\operatorname{AUC}(r-1)\] under the usual half-credit convention for ties. The parent independently evaluated the finite example. Equation (7)’s \(y=1\) anomaly convention and Figure 5’s high anomaly curves support the higher-is-anomalous reading.

Counterreading, repair, and bounded severity. The strongest explanation is a wording reversal: the intended rule may be \(1-\)normal similarity. The direct classifier branch remains available, and the paper explicitly chooses between the two branches. Neither the trained outputs nor the implemented scoring code are supplied. Consequently, this example is a defect in the literal written alternative rule, not proof that a reversed score generated any published AUC.

The correction \(a'=1-r\) restores the intended monotone direction algebraically; it does not guarantee accurate detection when similarity itself is poor. The exact score normalization and branch-selection protocol must still be supplied. This is Minor as a localized inference specification problem, with deployed behavior Needs Evidence. It is not an invalidation of the fine-grained alignment objective, the independent classifier branch, or the explanation pathway.

# Core questions that remain unresolved

## Which trainable variables does the contrastive term affect?

The paper describes frozen image and text encoders on p. 6 and label strings generated and selected by semantic similarity on pp. 5–6. Its contrastive loss is \[L_{\rm CTS}=\sum_j\max\left(0,
 \frac{L_N^\top L_{A_j}}{\|L_N\|_2\|L_{A_j}\|_2}\right).\] If the labels are fixed text-encoder outputs with fixed aggregation, and the trained parameters \(\theta\) belong only to the temporal adapter and classifiers, then \(L_N,L_{A_j}\) are independent of \(\theta\). The chain rule gives \[\nabla_\theta L_{\rm CTS}=0\] for every loss weight \(\lambda\). Under that parameterization, this loss cannot pull the fixed class embeddings apart; BCE and multiclass alignment can still train other components.

That conditional derivation is not proof that the actual loss is inactive. A frozen encoder can backpropagate into learnable input/context vectors, and a trainable label projection or aggregation can also provide a gradient path. The paper mentions context length \(l=20\), discusses prompt learning, and includes a learnable-prompt ablation. The final LAAM’s exact parameter graph is not specified sufficiently to exclude these readings. This is Needs Evidence: a trainable-parameter list, label-construction equation, gradient trace, or verified code is needed. No unconditional zero-gradient defect or Major consequence is asserted.

## Fusion and alignment dimensions

On p. 5, the feature equation is \(F_F=F_V+F_T\), while p. 6 explicitly states concatenation and Figure 2 labels the operation as Concat. For streams in \(\mathbb R^d\), addition yields \(\mathbb R^d\), whereas concatenation yields \(\mathbb R^{2d}\). The two operations are not interchangeable: for \(F_V=(1,0)\) and \(F_T=(0,1)\), their outputs are \((1,1)\) and \((1,0,0,1)\). The subsequent cosine alignment also requires compatible label dimensions.

Both architectures can be valid with suitable mappings. For example, a projection \([I\ I]\) after concatenation would recover the sum, but no claim is made that this is the actual projection. The text also leaves the integration of the selected label embeddings underspecified. The implemented fusion, broadcasting of a video-level text vector across frames, and projection into label space therefore remain Needs Evidence. An inconsistent description is established; an impossible running model is not.

## What is established about explanations?

The generated explanation is computed from captions before the learned detector and is subsequently used as a feature. Appendix Figure 5 supplies six selected qualitative examples, including normal and abnormal videos, with predicted curves and textual descriptions. This demonstrates example generation and semantic usefulness; detection ablations provide further evidence that the text can help the predictor.

It does not independently quantify factual accuracy, coverage of anomalous events, causal correctness about why an event occurred, or faithfulness to the trained detector’s decision. Because the explanation is upstream of the classifier, its existence alone does not determine which features the classifier used. Conversely, generation before classification is not proof of unfaithfulness: a useful semantic bottleneck can genuinely influence a decision. No factual error in a specific shown video is established from the sparse frames alone. The stronger explanation/cause claims remain Needs Evidence, requiring a defined evaluation with representative outputs and evidence tying the text to decisions. Generic possible caption failures do not refute a nonexistent universal caption-correction theorem.

Table 5 openly reports that captions achieve higher fine-grained AVG than explanation summaries, \(10.88\) versus \(10.15\). Page 8 explicitly chooses the latter for a performance/interpretation trade-off. Table 4 reports different coarse AUC results: visual-only \(86.76\), with captions \(86.33\), with the explanatory-text configuration \(87.86\), and with LAAM \(88.29\). The different metrics and disclosed trade-off should not be reframed as a concealed regression. The combined ablations do not separately identify every causal contribution of caption cleaning, summarization, or interpretation quality.

## Selection, evaluation, and compute boundaries

Section 3.6’s selection of the better coarse-score branch and the top-\(k\) analysis do not identify the data split used for selection. A validation-based or precommitted choice is possible. No test-label leakage is demonstrated. The disclosed caption/explanation input contains video content, not a documented ground-truth test-label prompt; whole-video context is consistent with an offline benchmark and does not by itself constitute label leakage.

Table 7 reports \(9.97\)M trainable parameters, \(15.37\) ms per frame, and \(12.04\)G MACs. Trainable counts legitimately exclude frozen weights. Captioning and video summarization may be cached or amortized in an offline pipeline. The paper does not supply a full timing boundary, model variants, frame/token counts, or separate BLIP-2, LLM, encoder, and detector costs. End-to-end/online efficiency interpretations are therefore unresolved, but no contradictory runtime measurement or timing fabrication is established.

Exact prompts, decoding settings, generated label phrases, threshold choices, baseline/run identities, raw predictions, and uncertainty estimates are unavailable. Several baseline bibliography associations do not identify their named VAD methods; those citation mismatches are preserved in the source note but are not promoted to standalone scientific Problems. The appendix’s phrase about unused anomaly categories does not supply a held-out-class protocol; no zero-shot generalization result is inferred from it.

# Overall dependence and conclusion

The supported assessment is Minor: the exact numerical presentation needs reconciliation, and one written alternative score rule needs its direction clarified. The core fine-grained average-ranking claim survives the displayed-cell arithmetic. The broader explanatory and implementation claims retain significant evidentiary limits, but missing evidence is not counted as a demonstrated failure.

The severity follows the dependencies actually established. There is no demonstrated invalid central theorem, nor evidence that the literal score-rule wording was the operative published pipeline. This is not a discount for a short repair or a requirement for whole-paper collapse. A future verified implementation could resolve the frozen-label/fusion questions or reveal material defects; the present record does not decide them in advance. No later corrected version or author-adopted repair was established from the frozen sources.
