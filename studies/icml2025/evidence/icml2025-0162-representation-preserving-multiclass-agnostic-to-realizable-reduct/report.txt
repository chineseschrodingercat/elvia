# Definitions needed for the checks

The framework specifies a tuple \((X,Y,A,L,C,H)\) and requires the learner’s output to belong to \(H\). Appendix B.1 (p. 10) allows partial concepts \(c:X\to A\cup\{\star\}\), defines \(\operatorname{supp}(c)=c^{-1}(A)\), and explicitly sets \(L(\star,y)=0\). A distribution is realizable only if, almost surely for every finite sample, some concept both covers every sampled input by its support and has zero loss on that sample.

The agnostic approximation error is instead \[L_D(C)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E_{S\sim D^n}\min_{c\in C}L_S(c).\] Definition B.2 (p. 11) quantifies over every distribution on \(X\times Y\). Its minimization is not restricted to concepts whose support contains the sample. This distinction is essential.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (Zero-loss abstention invalidates the partial-class reduction).  Theorem B.3 (p. 11) claims that Algorithm 1 converts every realizable unified partial learner into an agnostic unified partial learner with finite sample complexity. Under the printed definitions, a realizable learner can exist while no permitted agnostic learner exists.

## Complete admissible construction

Let \(X=Y=A=\{0,1\}\), equip these finite sets with their full sigma algebras, and take \(\star\notin A\). Set \[L(a,y)=\mathbf1\{a\ne y\}\quad(a\in A),\qquad L(\star,y)=0.\] Let \(C=\{c_0,c_1\}\) and \(H=\{h_0\}\), with

Predictor | \(x=0\) | \(x=1\)

\(c_0\) | \(0\) | \(\star\)

\(c_1\) | \(\star\) | \(0\)

\(h_0\) | \(0\) | \(0\)

All spaces and functions are finite and measurable, both classes are nonempty, and the definitions do not require \(C\subseteq H\). Define the supplied learner to return \(h_0\) on every sample, including the empty sample.

The only \(C\)-realizable distributions are \(\delta_{(0,0)}\) and \(\delta_{(1,0)}\). A point with label 1 cannot be supported and correctly labeled by any concept, already at sample size one. If both zero-labeled input points have positive mass, a size-two sample containing both has positive probability, and no concept supports both. Conversely, each displayed point mass is supported and correctly labeled by one concept. The supplied learner has risk zero on every realizable distribution. Thus a finite realizable sample bound of one is valid for every \(\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)\).

Now take \(D=\delta_{(0,1)}\). Every nonempty sample contains only \((0,1)\). Because \(c_1(0)=\star\), every sample satisfies \(L_S(c_1)=0\). Therefore \[L_D(C)=0,\qquad L_D(h_0)=1.\] Algorithm 1’s candidates all equal \(h_0\), whatever subsets and sample sizes it uses. At \(\epsilon=1/2\) and \(\delta=1/4\), its required success event is \(1\le1/2\), which has probability zero instead of at least \(3/4\). The same contradiction holds for every \(\epsilon<1\), every \(\delta<1\), and every sample size.

The independent finite calculation reproduces the four point-mass losses, verifies the mixed-input support failure, and checks samples of sizes 1, 2, 5, and 20. The argument above, not that finite list alone, proves the all-sample-size conclusion.

## Failing proof step and alternative readings

Before the proof on p. 11 and following Eq. (15) on p. 12, the argument treats the zero-loss portion of an empirical minimizer as realizable and invokes the supplied learner. In this example the empirical minimizer has zero loss only because it is undefined on every observation. Zero loss does not imply the required support condition. Equivalently, if one defines the selected portion using actual support, Eq. (14)’s loss decomposition no longer equals the claimed \(1-\mu^\ast\) under zero-loss abstention.

Changing \(L(\star,y)\) to one, or changing the agnostic comparator to include a support restriction, would alter an explicit definition and remove this particular witness. Neither alteration appears in Definition B.2. No complete repaired theorem under either convention has been verified.

## Impact

This is Major: Theorem B.3 is false as stated, and partial-learning corollaries relying on it cannot inherit its guarantee without reconciliation. The partial extension is advertised as a contribution; its appendix location does not limit its importance. The counterexample does not refute Theorem 4.1 for total predictors, where the support mismatch is absent.

Problem 2 (The lower-bound example does not isolate a representation-preservation cost).  Section 6 (p. 7) states that an extra \(1/\epsilon\) factor relative to compression-based learning can be unavoidable because representation is preserved. Theorem 6.1 and its proof do not establish that conclusion.

## Literal statement

The displayed theorem states \[\frac{M^{\mathrm{RE}}(\epsilon,\delta)}
     {M^{\mathrm{AG}}(\epsilon,\delta)}
\in\Omega(\epsilon^{-2}).\] For the same ordinary learning instance and allowed hypothesis class, any agnostic learner also handles realizable distributions. Hence the optimal sample complexities satisfy \(M^{\mathrm{RE}}\le M^{\mathrm{AG}}\) whenever compared as positive finite quantities. Their ratio is at most one and cannot have the displayed divergent lower bound. The proof’s own claims of constant realizable and order-\(\epsilon^{-2}\) agnostic complexity point in the reverse direction.

## Why reversing the ratio is insufficient

Grant the charitable reading that the ratio should be reversed. In the theorem’s construction take \(n=1\) and \(m=2\): one input point, two labels, and the two constant predictors as \(C=H\). This \(H\) is the entire predictor space. Representation preservation imposes no output restriction at all.

Let \(p=\Pr(Y=1)\). Empirical majority outputs a member of \(H\). If the wrong label has excess risk greater than \(\epsilon\), then \(|p-1/2|>\epsilon/2\). The elementary bounded-sample tail inequality gives a wrong-majority probability at most \(\exp(-N\epsilon^2/2)\). Thus \[N\ge 2\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta)\] suffices while preserving representation. If \(|2p-1|\le\epsilon\), either label already meets the excess-risk target.

This example therefore distinguishes realizable and noisy learning, not restricted and unrestricted representations. In particular, it cannot demonstrate a necessary \(\epsilon^{-3}\) cost or an extra \(\epsilon^{-1}\) penalty relative to an \(\epsilon^{-2}\) comparison. No lower-bound result external to the reviewed paper is needed for this conclusion: the predictor spaces coincide exactly, and the explicit upper bound suffices.

## Impact and correction limits

The reversed ratio is a plausible writing error, but the representation-cost interpretation remains unsupported after that local edit. The defect is Major for the stated lower-bound rationale connected to the central sample-complexity contribution. It does not invalidate the reduction algorithm’s upper bound. A different construction might establish a representation-dependent cost; this review does not show that such a lower bound is impossible. The supported correction is to describe the example as a realizable-versus-agnostic sampling comparison, without claiming that it isolates representation cost. No replacement optimality theorem has been verified.
