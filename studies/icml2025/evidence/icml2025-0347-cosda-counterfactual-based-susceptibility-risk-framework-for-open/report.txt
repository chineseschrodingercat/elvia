Abstract: The proposed representation and prototype heuristics may be useful, but their principal causal and generalization guarantees do not follow. The probability-of-sufficiency risk is replaced by a different quantity; the target-risk bound fails by explicit finite counterexamples; the variance-only generalization bound can be negative when the generalization gap is zero; and mutual-information maximization does not imply the claimed exogeneity. These are Major theoretical findings. Separate, version-bounded source defects affect a distributed training path, benchmark defaults, and regularization accounting. COSDA combines a learned feature intervention, Gaussian representations, target pseudo-labels from known and virtual unknown prototypes, and contrastive alignment. The support decomposition in Theorem 1 is a legitimate partition once its risk is defined consistently. # The causal risk and target bound

Problem 1 (Major: the risk identity and Theorem 2 are invalid).  Proposition 1, equation (8), and Appendix B.1 replace a ratio by a difference. Theorem 2, equation (10), and Appendix B.3 then give an invalid upper bound even if that difference is adopted as a new surrogate risk.

Let \(p=P(Y=y\mid C=c)\), \(q=P(Y=y\mid C'=c')\), \(a=1-p\), and \(b=1-q\). Under the identification assumptions used by the paper, \(\mathrm{PS}=(p-q)/(1-q)\), so \[1-\mathrm{PS}=\frac{a}{b},\qquad\text{whereas equation (8) uses }D=a-b.\] For \(a=1/5,b=4/5\), these are \(1/4\) and \(-3/5\). Both probabilities are realizable by monotone potential outcomes: take independent uniform \(U\), let correctness under \(c\) be \(\mathbf1\{U<1-a\}\) and under \(c'\) be \(\mathbf1\{U<1-b\}\), and randomize the treatment independently of \(U\). Thus this is not a violation of exogeneity or monotonicity.

There is useful counterevidence to an overly broad objection. For \(0<a\le b\le1\), the weaker inequality \[a/b\le \exp(a-b)\] does hold, since \(\log a-\log b\le a-b\). It could justify a limited surrogate interpretation. However, Appendix B.1’s intermediate bound \(a/b<\exp(a-b-2)\) fails in the example above: \(0.25>0.07427\). More importantly, minimizing the difference need not minimize the true risk. The feasible pairs \((a,b)=(.01,.2)\) and \((.4,.9)\) have differences \(-.19,-.5\) but true risks \(.05,4/9\) respectively. The smaller difference chooses the larger true risk. A corrected statement must distinguish the risk from a surrogate and its possible upper bound.

## Subtracting upper bounds does not upper-bound their difference

Equations (36)–(38), pages 15–16, subtract two upper bounds on joint errors. From \(u\le U\) and \(v\le V\) one cannot infer \(u-v\le U-V\). The sign is central because the intervention error is deliberately subtracted.

For a binary counterexample, let the source have two nuisance states with masses \((1/2,1/2)\) and the target have masses \((.9,.1)\), all with the same known label. Let a deterministic classifier be correct on the observed representation and wrong on the intervened representation at both states. The representation can ignore the nuisance state, so its invariance and monotonic correctness are preserved. Then both disagreements vanish, \(e_S(c)=0\), \(e_S(c')=1\), and \(\beta_\infty=1.8\). Equation (10) claims \(-1\le-1.8\), which is false even after replacing its strict inequality by a weak one.

This is not solely a closed-set boundary issue. Add target-only unknown mass \(.2\) on a new support point, with equal errors before and after intervention; give the old target states masses \(.72,.08\). The unknown difference term is zero, \(\beta_\infty=1.44\), and the claimed inequality becomes \(-.8\le-1.44\). This extension uses the paper’s support-restricted density ratio. A convention making the divergence infinite for every open-set problem would instead render the advertised bound uninformative.

## The Gibbs decomposition is binary-specific

Equation (35) also applies \(R=\tfrac12d+e\) to a multiclass problem. When two voters predict distinct wrong classes, the two error indicators sum to 2, while disagreement plus twice joint error is 3.

The failure can reverse the final inequality without any domain shift. Set \(S=T\), fix true label 0, and choose four equiprobable voters with outputs \[(h_1(c),\ldots,h_4(c))=(0,1,1,1),\qquad
 (h_1(c'),\ldots,h_4(c'))=(1,1,2,2).\] Every voter’s correctness is monotone under this intervention. Direct enumeration gives \[(R,d,e)_c=(3/4,3/8,9/16),\qquad
 (R,d,e)_{c'}=(1,1/2,1).\] The true difference is \(-1/4\), but the right side of equation (10), with \(\beta_\infty=1\) and no unsupported mass, is \(-1/2\). Replicating this construction under cyclic class relabeling supplies all three true classes if desired. A new multiclass decomposition and a valid bound for a signed error difference are needed. The advertised source/target loss justification materially depends on both missing steps; the findings do not by themselves refute measured accuracy.

# The generalization bound and causal assumptions

Problem 2 (Major: Corollary 1 is not a valid high-probability bound).  Equation (14), pages 4–5, asserts a uniform bound by \(-\log\sigma_C+\log\sigma_{C'}\). Appendix B.4 does not establish it.

Take Gaussian representations with standard deviations \(\sigma_C=2\), \(\sigma_{C'}=1\), one fixed true label, and a constant wrong classifier. Observed and intervened errors are both 1; correct-class probabilities are both 0. Consequently the difference risk in equation (8) and its empirical difference in equation (13) are both zero for every sample. Nevertheless the alleged bound is \(-\log2<0\). It fails with probability one, independently of sample size. Monotonicity holds with equality. This also avoids relying on the opposite sign conventions of equations (8) and (13), which must themselves be reconciled.

In the proof, upper bounds are again subtracted. Equation (42), page 17, then replaces \(-\mathrm{KL}(\rho\|\gamma_{C'})\) by \(+\mathrm{KL}(\gamma_{C'}\|\rho)\) as an equality. These are not equal: for \(\rho=N(0,1)\) and \(\gamma_{C'}=N(0,4)\), the forward and reverse KL divergences are approximately \(.318147\) and \(.806853\). Nor does taking the prior variance to infinity yield a finite PAC-Bayes bound: the KL diverges. Dropping a parameter-independent divergent term may motivate an optimization heuristic, but cannot preserve a finite probability guarantee. A repaired result needs a proper prior, correct signs, and sample-size/confidence dependence.

Problem 3 (Major: the stated mutual-information equivalence and monotonicity enforcement do not follow).  Proposition 2, equation (16), and Appendix B.5 identify domain-invariant conditional labels with maximized cross-domain mutual information. Equation (15) is also presented as satisfaction of monotonicity. Neither implication is established.

Let \(Z\) be a fair binary label, and pair source and target features from that label as \(C_s=Z\) and \(C_t=1-Z\). Their mutual information is the maximum possible, one bit. In a pooled population with independent fair domain \(E\), let \(C=Z\mathbin{\mathrm{xor}}E\) and \(Y=Z\). Then \(C\perp E\), but \[I(Y;E\mid C)=1\text{ bit},\qquad
 P(Y\mid C,E)\ne P(Y\mid C).\] Thus maximal shared information, even with domain-independent feature marginals and same-label pairing, does not ensure the required conditional invariance. Mutual information is unchanged by invertible domain-specific permutations. This does not show that COSDA’s particular cosine alignment necessarily selects such a permutation; it disproves the claimed equivalence used to justify it.

There is a further definitional issue: conditional on a fixed class and fixed training set, the source centroid \(C_s^k\) is constant, so its literal mutual information with a random target feature is zero. The variables and joint sampling law need to be specified before the displayed expectation of mutual information is meaningful. Equation (44) writes a single event’s log term in place of a full KL/conditional-MI sum, and equation (46)’s claimed “transmissibility” supplies no missing implication.

Likewise, a positive average correctness difference does not exclude individual defiers. A population with \(.6\) responders \((Y_c,Y_{c'})=(1,0)\), \(.1\) defiers \((0,1)\), and \(.3\) never-correct units has \(p-q=.5\), yet violates monotonicity on ten percent of units. With randomized intervention, true sufficiency is \((.6)/(.9)=2/3\), while the formula assuming monotonicity gives \((.6-.1)/(.9)=5/9\). An average objective is not an enforcement mechanism for the pointwise assumption. CFA and VMP can remain useful heuristics; claims that they establish causal identification require additional conditions or weaker wording.

# Static implementation findings and empirical checks

## A conditional target-label leak in the distributed path

In , lines 157–185, the loop runs for the maximum of source and target loader lengths. When the source iterator is exhausted, lines 163–165 reset it to the target loader. Its third output becomes and enters source cross-entropy at line 185 and lines 94–98. The dataset returns the annotation-derived label at , mapping unknown labels to the unknown index.

If the source loader is shorter and the run reaches that branch, target annotations therefore enter the supervised gradient. An independent three-step iterator example, with two source batches and three target batches, confirms the last supervised batch comes from target data. This is a Major protocol defect for an affected distributed experiment. It is conditional on this path and does not prove that any published result is affected. In particular, the README’s adaptation command uses , whose line 142 correctly resets the source loader. The optional true-label contrastive branch defaults off; its existence is not evidence of label leakage in normal non-distributed adaptation. The repair is to reset the source iterator correctly and document the historical command and revision.

## Minor release mismatches requiring reproducibility corrections

The current DomainNet configuration fixes 150 shared and 172 unknown classes at , while Tables 4–5 specify 173/172. Dataset filtering thus retains target class IDs 0–321 rather than all 345 classes, omitting 322–344 and moving the known/unknown boundary. Office-31’s default 10 unknown classes at line 130, combined with the offset in , selects 20–29 rather than the paper’s 20–30; an explicit override to 11 repairs that default. These are confirmed current-configuration differences, not evidence that the historical tables used the wrong split.

Both domains’ loss dictionaries use the same keys and . In the non-distributed loop, source update at line 169 is overwritten by target update at line 207, so the total at line 209 retains only target values, rather than both domains’ regularizers. Distinct keys or explicit summation are needed if Algorithm 1 is intended. Moreover, applies a positive-part entropy threshold to both representations, not equation (14)’s signed log-standard-deviation difference. The implemented cross-entropy surrogate is also not a literal estimator of the disagreement/joint-error expressions. These discrepancies need an explicit algorithm-to-code mapping; replacing the false theorem alone does not document the implemented objective.

## Minor arithmetic and limits of visual evidence

Table 3, page 7, reports an average OS* of 64.6 without \(L_s\), whereas its four entries \(72.9,71.1,51.5,49.8\) average to \(61.325\), or 61.3 at the displayed precision. In Table 8, page 22, the C\(\to\)A OS* improvement over ANNA is \(71.2-48.6=22.6\) percentage points, not the prose’s 20.6. The corresponding UNK and HOS differences, 40.5 and 38.9, agree. These corrections do not reverse those individual improvements.

Figures 1–11, including both Figure 5 sensitivity surfaces and the five released embeddings, were visually inspected. The t-SNE separation is descriptive evidence, not proof of exogeneity. The synthetic graph in Figure 8 is \(Y\to C\to S\); its distance-correlation measurements demonstrate retention of label-associated factors in that generative model, not identification of an intervention effect \(C\to Y\). This is a limit of the proposed causal validation, not an allegation that its plotted measurements are fabricated. The packet does not provide raw run-level uncertainty, a complete historical benchmark record, or synthetic-generation code sufficient to reproduce all these claims. Best-checkpoint selection and correspondence between current training paths and reported tables remain unresolved.

# Disposition and reproducible evidence

Major revision is warranted because the central target-risk, generalization, and causal-identification arguments require replacement. Empirical usefulness can survive these theoretical failures and must be evaluated on its own evidence. The distributed code defect needs correction before that path can support an unsupervised adaptation experiment. Version provenance is essential before attributing it to published results.
