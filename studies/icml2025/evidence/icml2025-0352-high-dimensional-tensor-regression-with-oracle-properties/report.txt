Abstract: The low-rank oracle proof omits cross blocks from an asserted orthogonal decomposition. This invalidates a central proof step and is assessed as Major, without asserting that every possible revised oracle theorem is false. The stated full-ball curvature condition is also impossible for underdetermined scalar-response designs. Static inspection finds that two released proximal routines use convex soft thresholding in place of the claimed SCAD proximal map. Historical experimental consequences remain unresolved.

# Identity, scope and assumptions

Wenbin Wang, Yu Shi and Ziping Zhao, High-Dimensional Tensor Regression With Oracle Properties, ICML 2025, PMLR 267, pp.64302–64342, OpenReview `ue1ptN4kSS`. The reviewed publisher PDF has 41 pages; all locations below use one-based PDF pages, matching printed numbers. Main definitions, Theorems 5–6, the relevant Appendix B.1/B.4 arguments, the algorithm and experimental descriptions were read. Appendix A/B extensions and Appendix C were inspected only to the extent recorded in coverage; the full suite of rates and concentration lemmas is not certified.

The model contracts tensor predictors with a coefficient tensor and minimizes a squared loss plus a separable or singular-value penalty. SCAD and MCP satisfy bounded concavity assumptions, and the theory assumes loss curvature \(\mu\) greater than the penalty concavity \(\zeta\). The low-rank definitions use the compact singular spaces \(U,V\) of the true unfolding. We preserve that definition rather than silently replacing it with the usual tangent space.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The low-rank proof omits two cross blocks).

Section 4.2.2, p.5, defines \(F=\{W:\operatorname{col}(W)\subseteq U,\operatorname{row}(W)\subseteq V\}\) and labels the space with both column and row spaces perpendicular to \(U,V\) as \(F^\perp\). Its projections are written \[P_F(W)=UU^\top WVV^\top,\qquad P_N(W)=(I-UU^\top)W(I-VV^\top).\] Both displayed maps are genuine orthogonal projections, but their ranges do not exhaust the matrix space. The first equality after (23) in Lemma 14, p.25, nevertheless decomposes an arbitrary inner product using only these two projections. Equation (35), p.27, repeats this decomposition in the deterministic error bound; pp.34–35 use it to identify the oracle estimator with the penalized estimator.

Complete algebraic check. Let the true unfolding be \(A^*=\operatorname{diag}(4,0)\), hence \(U=V=e_1\), and set \[W=e_1e_2^\top=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.\] This is a legitimate two-mode tensor/matrix; no high-order restriction excludes it. Then \(P_FW=P_NW=0\) while \(W\ne0\). With the other inner-product factor also \(W\), the unprojected product is \(\langle W,W\rangle_F=1\), whereas the asserted two-term decomposition gives zero. This refutes the universal algebraic identity used by the proof, not merely a bound’s constant. It is independent of distributional, curvature and signal-strength assumptions: those assumptions do not imply vanishing cross components of arbitrary gradients or estimation errors. Indeed, \(A^*+\epsilon W\) remains rank one for every \(\epsilon\), so a low-rank restriction alone does not eliminate this direction.

Correct identity and limits of repair. With \(P=UU^\top\) and \(Q=VV^\top\), the full identity is \[W=PWQ+PW(I-Q)+(I-P)WQ+(I-P)W(I-Q).\] The true complement of \(F\) has projector \(W-PWQ\). Alternatively, the tangent-space projector is \(PW+WQ-PWQ\), whose complement is the paper’s \(P_N\). Substituting that tangent space changes dimension, rank and projected-noise bounds; it does not by itself prove the stated oracle rate. The example does not claim to instantiate every assumption of Theorem 6 as a stochastic counterexample. It is a decisive counterexample to a universal proof identity on which that theorem’s derivation depends.

Impact. Major for the central low-rank oracle argument. The abstract and Theorem 6 rely on this derivation, and Appendix B.5 refers back to it for slice-wise low rank. The sparse case does not use the same matrix decomposition and is not refuted by this calculation. Evidence: `check_output.json`, `projection`; source pp.5,25,27,34–35.

Problem 2 (Full-ball strong convexity cannot cover underdetermined designs).

Section 4.1, p.4, defines the sparse-case neighborhood as the full Frobenius ball \(C=\{B:\|B-A^*\|_F\le r\}\) and Assumption 2 demands, for all \(A,B\in C\), a positive \(\mu\) such that \[L(B)\ge L(A)+\langle\nabla L(A),B-A\rangle+\tfrac\mu2\|B-A\|_F^2.\] The following text asserts that the curvature conditions can be proved with high probability. For scalar responses (\(M=N\), explicitly allowed in Section 3.1), vectorize the coefficient to \(p\) coordinates and write the design as \(Z\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\). If \(p>n\), choose a nonzero \(h\in\ker Z\). For any \(r>0\) choose \(0<t<r/\|h\|\). Taking \(A=A^*\), \(B=A^*+th\) gives \(L(B)=L(A)\) and \(\langle\nabla L(A),th\rangle=0\), while the final term is strictly positive. The assumed inequality is impossible.

This holds for every underdetermined design, including every realization of independent standard Gaussian entries satisfying Assumption 4 with population covariance \(I\). Increasing signal size to satisfy the minimum-signal assumption does not change the kernel. The script also checks \(Z=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and \(h=(-1,-1,1)^\top\), for which \(Zh=0\) and at \(\mu=1/2\) the unsatisfied quadratic term is \(3/4\) before rescaling.

Interpretation and impact. This does not refute Theorem 5 as an implication conditional on Assumption 2: in the stated regime its antecedent is unavailable. It does invalidate the advertised application of that full-ball hypothesis to high-dimensional scalar regression with \(p>n\). This is Major for that core scope claim. A sparsity-restricted cone or a tolerance-bearing curvature inequality could be suitable, but would require changed assumptions, an argument that the error lies in the admissible set, and a new proof. The actual full-ball definition cannot be replaced silently. Overdetermined designs and other restricted formulations remain possible.

Problem 3 (Released routines do not take a SCAD proximal step).

The paper’s Section 5 calls for a proximal step for the nonconvex penalty, and Section 6 says SCAD is used in the experiments. In both inspected `codes/func/niAPG.m` snapshots, `prox_operator` instead replaces each singular value \(z\) by \(\max(z-\lambda\eta,0)\). The earlier version also uses entrywise soft thresholding for its sparsity branch. These are the convex nuclear/\(\ell_1\) shrinkages, not a general SCAD proximal map. The later repository’s `codes/test.m` calls the inspected routine; its separate experiment runners also call distinct solvers, so the discrepancy is not automatically attributable to all published results.

For the paper’s SCAD definition, set \(a=3.7\), \(\lambda=1\), \(\eta=1/2\), and scalar input \(z=5\). On \(u>3.7\), \(p_\lambda(u)=(a+1)/2=2.35\). The proximal objective \(\tfrac12(u-z)^2+\eta p_\lambda(u)\) is 1.175 at \(u=5\) and 1.300 at the released shrinkage \(u=4.5\). The released answer is therefore not a minimizer; even a local move to 5 improves it. Additionally, both files’ local SCAD helper uses the plateau \((a^2-1)\lambda^2/2=6.345\) rather than 2.35, creating a jump at \(a\lambda\). These are exact scalar discrepancies, not a MATLAB execution result.

The current-source inconsistency is Minor because historical provenance and effects on the comparative tables remain Needs Evidence. A correct proximal implementation and continuous SCAD plateau are necessary local repairs, but the other solvers and complete experiment pipeline require separate audit. The nonmonotone selection branch is present in both code versions; this is counterevidence against treating the overwritten selection in the printed Algorithm 1 as proof that every executable omitted the safeguard.

# Overall conclusion and remaining evidence

The supported outcome is Major for a central low-rank proof defect and the impossible full-ball application in underdetermined scalar regression, with a separate Minor release discrepancy. No claim is made that nonconvex tensor regression cannot work or that the experimental scores are fabricated. Figures 1–4 were actually viewed and show the stated comparative patterns; they neither supply missing mathematical assumptions nor establish code provenance. The 100-realization aggregate statistics, cross-validation choices, data processing and all supplementary figures were not reproduced.
