Abstract: Two exact finite-game counterexamples invalidate central theoretical claims. In a symmetric zero-sum game, every pure-strategy LookAhead direction decreases advantage at an interior meta-equilibrium, contrary to Theorem 4.2. In a simplified two-player general-sum game, the optimistic advantage is discontinuous, contrary to Theorem 4.6. These defects undermine the corresponding deterministic-improvement and continuity-based arguments without disproving the reported empirical comparisons. All calculations use independent rational arithmetic. Scientific adjudication is pending.

Author: AI-assisted technical review

Title: Exact counterexamples to two A-PSRO guarantees

# Identity, scope and notation

This is an initial review of Yudong Hu, Haoran Li, Congying Han, Tiande Guo, Mingqiang Li and Bonan Li, A-PSRO: A Unified Strategy Learning Method with Advantage Metric for Normal-form Games, ICML 2025, PMLR 267. For a row strategy \(x\) and payoff matrix \(A\) in a zero-sum game, the paper’s advantage is \(V(x)=\min_j(x^\top A)_j\).

# Problem 1: no improving pure direction in Theorem 4.2

Severity: Major. Theorem 4.2 is false under its stated hypotheses. The theorem, page 4, requires that whenever a current meta-equilibrium \(\theta\) has \(V(\theta)<0\), some pure action \(e_j\) satisfies \(V((1-d)\theta+de_j)>V(\theta)\) for all sufficiently small positive \(d\). Consider the symmetric zero-sum game with row matrix \[A=\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1&-1&-1\\
1&0&-2&2\\
1&2&0&-2\\
1&-2&2&0
\end{pmatrix},\qquad A=-A^\top,\] and column payoff \(-A\). Take both populations to be the singleton \(P_i=P_j=\{\theta\}\), where \[\theta=(3/4,1/12,1/12,1/12),\qquad
 \theta^\top A=(1/4,-3/4,-3/4,-3/4).\] This is a legitimate meta-equilibrium: the restricted game has one action per player and payoff \(\theta^\top A\theta=0\). The underlying strategy is strictly interior to the full simplex, and \(V(\theta)=-3/4<0\). Direct multiplication gives, for every \(0<d\le1\), \[\begin{aligned}
 V((1-d)\theta+de_1)&=-3/4-d/4,\\
 V((1-d)\theta+de_j)&=-3/4-5d/4,\quad j=2,3,4.\end{aligned}\] Thus all pure directions strictly worsen advantage. Reducing the step size, or selecting the best of these directions, cannot produce the claimed improvement. At \(d=1/100\), the four values are \((-301/400,-61/80,-61/80,-61/80)\), all below \(-3/4\).

In contrast, the mixed direction \(m=(0,1/3,1/3,1/3)\) satisfies \(m^\top A=(1,0,0,0)\) and \[V((1-d)\theta+dm)=-3/4+3d/4>V(\theta).\] The independent script confirms the displayed formulas at exact rational steps, including \(10^{-6}\), \(10^{-2}\), \(1/2\) and \(1\). The formulas themselves establish the full interval, not just those four tests.

The proof fails between Appendix A.5 equations (18) and (19), pages 15–16. Existence of an improving mixed direction and a pure component with positive inner product do not imply that the pure component improves a nonsmooth concave objective. Here the three active worst-case payoffs are improved by different components; every individual component harms another active payoff. This is consistent with concavity of \(V\) and does not challenge the valid min-of-linear representation in Theorem 3.4.

The effect is central: the pure-action LookAhead search in Section 4.1 and Algorithm 2 cannot inherit deterministic improvement or a sublinear rate from Theorem 4.2. Random population expansion and diversity may escape this example; the counterexample does not claim the complete stochastic algorithm can never converge. A possible mathematical repair is to allow a genuinely mixed direction with larger \(V\), since concavity then proves improvement along its segment. That changes the oracle and does not supply the paper’s claimed rate or efficiency automatically.

# Problem 2: optimistic advantage need not be continuous

Severity: Major. The Lipschitz assertion in Theorem 4.6 is false. Let the two payoff matrices, with the same row/column ordering, be \[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.\] This game is simplified under Definition 4.3, including its stated quantifier order. Every action of either player is uniquely optimal against one opposing pure action. Thus no action satisfies the paper’s dominated action criterion. No deletion or dominated-action ambiguity is involved.

For row strategy \(x=(p,1-p)\) the column player’s payoffs are \(p\) for column 1 and \(2(1-p)\) for column 2. Therefore column 2 is uniquely best for \(p<2/3\), column 1 for \(p>2/3\), and both are best at \(p=2/3\). Definition 4.5 chooses the best response giving the row player the larger payoff at a tie, so \[V(p)=\begin{cases}
 1-p,&p<2/3,\\
 4/3,&p=2/3,\\
 2p,&p>2/3.
 \end{cases}\] In particular, \[V(2/3)-V(2/3-\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon,
 \qquad 0<\varepsilon<2/3.\] The strategy displacement in Euclidean norm is \(\sqrt2\varepsilon\), so no finite Lipschitz constant exists. The scalar-parameter difference ratios at \(\varepsilon=10^{-2},10^{-4},10^{-6}\) are exactly \(99,9999,999999\). The left and right limiting values differ, so changing only the value assigned at the tie cannot restore continuity.

Appendix A.7, page 16, asserts that the zero-sum continuity proof does not require zero-sumness. It does: in zero-sum games, opponent best responses minimize the reviewed player’s own payoff, making \(V\) a minimum of finitely many continuous linear functions. In general-sum games, equal opponent utility does not imply equal own utility. The example gives equal column utility \(2/3\) at the switch but row utilities \(4/3\) and \(1/3\). Simplification and locally unique best responses on either side do not eliminate the jump.

The claim that nearby strategies have nearby advantage, used in the interpretation following Theorems 4.6–4.7, is therefore unsupported. Continuity on an individual strict-best-response region is a valid restricted statement, but cannot replace the global theorem. Neither this counterexample nor the first one negates all empirical gains. A repaired general-sum theory must explicitly handle best-response boundaries or add assumptions ensuring compatible payoffs there.

# Other inspected claims and limits

For zero-sum games the advantage really is a finite minimum of linear payoffs, hence concave and Lipschitz, and exploitability decomposes as \(E(x,y)=-V_i(x)-V_j(y)\). These properties survive both findings. The phrase “unique maximum” should distinguish a maximum value of zero from uniqueness of its maximizing strategy. Multiple equilibrium strategies are possible. No uniqueness claim is needed for either counterexample.

Theorem 4.8 and Appendix A.9 were read, but their approximation-rate proof was not accepted: convexity of \(-V\) does not establish a smooth quadratic remainder for the generally piecewise-linear \(V\), and the printed proof takes a descent step on \(f=V\) despite seeking its maximum. A projected nonsmooth or suitably regularized ascent analysis, with precise error conditions, would be needed. This is an additional unresolved proof obligation, not a third independent claimed counterexample here.

Figure 4’s plotted rewards and Figure 5’s embedded advantage landscapes were inspected as reported empirical evidence. They do not certify a universal convergence theorem. The t-SNE pictures do not prove continuity of the original function. Reported repeat counts and implementation parameters were read, but raw game matrices, trajectories, seeds and supplementary code were unavailable for reproduction. The multiplayer coalition-response approximation was read as an approximation, not treated as an exact equilibrium oracle.

# Disposition

Both Major findings directly contradict central mathematical assertions under the paper’s hypotheses. Their exact payoff calculations are verified by the initial reviewer. The suggested restrictions/oracle changes are not a verified replacement algorithm. Experimental performance, author adoption and independent acceptance remain unverified; scientific adjudication is pending and no human review is recorded.
