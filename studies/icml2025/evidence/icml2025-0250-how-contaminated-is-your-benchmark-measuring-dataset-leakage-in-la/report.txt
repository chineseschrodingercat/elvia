Abstract: This review checks the mathematical explanation, experimental scope, and released implementation of Kernel Divergence Score. A normalized-embedding counterexample reverses score rankings when the bandwidth changes within the paper’s tested range, disproving the algebraic rationale for bandwidth-independent trends. The released default also estimates two bandwidths, whereas the displayed distance-change decomposition assumes one. A normalization plot is incompatible with the defined full kernel sum. The Pile experiment’s model-specific membership labels require additional evidence. The highest supported impact is Major, confined to the bandwidth-robustness argument; the published empirical correlations are not numerically refuted. Scientific adjudication and independent acceptance remain pending.

# Definitions and the valid common-bandwidth identity

Equation reference: eq:def, eq:score. Let \(Z,Z'\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}\) be the pre- and post-fine-tuning embeddings, with unit-norm rows. Define \[
u_{ij}=\left\lVert Z_i-Z_j\right\rVert^2,\quad
v_{ij}=\left\lVert Z_i'-Z_j'\right\rVert^2,\quad
K^\gamma_{ij}=e^{-\gamma u_{ij}},\quad
E_\gamma=\left(\sum_{i,j}K^\gamma_{ij}\right)^{1/2},\] for finite \(\gamma>0\). The paper’s actual Eq. (5), p. 4, is \[
S_\gamma(Z,Z')
=-\frac{1}{E_\gamma}
  \sum_{i,j}\left|K^\gamma_{ij}
       \log\frac{K^\gamma_{ij}}{K^{\prime\gamma}_{ij}}\right|
=-\frac{\gamma}{E_\gamma}
  \sum_{i,j}e^{-\gamma u_{ij}}\left|v_{ij}-u_{ij}\right|.\] The second equality follows exactly from \(\log(e^{-\gamma u}/e^{-\gamma v})=\gamma(v-u)\). It verifies the common-bandwidth expansions in the paper’s Eqs. (4) and (6).

The absolute-value bars are present in the original PDF. Their omission from the extracted text was checked against the rendered source; it is not a paper defect. The released fixed-bandwidth branch applies the same entrywise absolute value and agrees with [eq:score] (#eq:score). Likewise, the original Appendix C MAPE formula contains absolute values.

Section 2.2 states desired contamination monotonicity and consistency requirements. The paper supports them empirically rather than by a theorem connecting arbitrary embedding configurations to actual LLM training membership. Consequently, the examples below test claims about the defined scoring functional and its implementation. They do not assert that a specified LoRA run produces these embeddings.

# Problem 1: bandwidth changes can reverse the score ordering

Problem 1 (Invalid rationale for bandwidth-independent trends).  The argument following Eq. (6), p. 7, does not establish that changing the bandwidth preserves relative score trends.

## Source claim and suspect inference

After rewriting the score using \(\gamma e^{-u_{ij}\gamma}\), the bandwidth discussion argues that the multiplicative and power factors do not affect trends across contamination rates. It states: This does not influence their relative trends across varying contamination rates. Table 3 reports high correlations on WikiMIA for several bandwidths, and the text presents Eq. (6) as an explanation of that robustness [Retained latex: \cite[p.~7]{paper}].

For an individual positive kernel entry, taking a positive power preserves its order relative to another entry. That observation does not preserve the order of sums with different distance-change weights. Moreover, \(E_\gamma\) also depends on the dataset and on \(\gamma\). Neither effect is a common scaling of all scores.

## Complete counterexample

Take two two-sample, two-dimensional configurations, \(A\) and \(B\). The first point is \(e_1=(1,0)\) before and after tuning in both. The second points are \[\begin{aligned}
z_{A,2}&=(-1/4,\sqrt{15}/4),&
z'_{A,2}&=(-3/4,\sqrt7/4),\\
z_{B,2}&=(3/4,\sqrt7/4),&
z'_{B,2}&=(3/5,4/5).\end{aligned}\] Every row has norm one: the relevant sums of squared coordinates are \(1/16+15/16\), \(9/16+7/16\), and \(9/25+16/25\). Since \(\left\lVert e_1-z\right\rVert^2=2-2z_1\) for a unit vector \(z\), \[(u_A,v_A,\delta_A)=(5/2,7/2,1),\qquad
(u_B,v_B,\delta_B)=(1/2,4/5,3/10),\] where \(\delta=\left|v-u\right|\). Thus all changes are positive, not contractions or cancellation artifacts. The two-point RBF matrices have diagonal one and positive off-diagonal entries; their eigenvalues are \(1\pm e^{-\gamma u}>0\). Both normalizers are positive.

Equation reference: eq:two. The diagonal contributions to [eq:score] (#eq:score) vanish, while the two off-diagonal contributions coincide. Therefore \[
S_\gamma(u,\delta)
=-\frac{2\gamma e^{-\gamma u}\delta}
        {\sqrt{2+2e^{-\gamma u}}}.\] Substitution gives the following values, independently checked to 30-digit working precision:

\(\gamma\) | \(S_\gamma(A)\) | \(S_\gamma(B)\) | Ordering

\(0.1\) | \(-0.082580540560\) | \(-0.028891318381\) | \(A<B\)

\(1\) | \(-0.111595865539\) | \(-0.203022654806\) | \(A>B\)

The same reversal follows without relying on decimal rounding. The ratio of score magnitudes is \[R_\gamma=\frac{\left|S_\gamma(A)\right|}{\left|S_\gamma(B)\right|}
=\frac{10}{3}e^{-2\gamma}
 \sqrt{\frac{1+e^{-\gamma/2}}{1+e^{-5\gamma/2}}}.\] At \(\gamma=0.1\), \(R_\gamma>(10/3)e^{-1/5}>8/3>1\), since \(e^{1/5}<1/(1-1/5)=5/4\). At \(\gamma=1\), \(R_\gamma<(10\sqrt2/3)e^{-2}<10\sqrt2/21<1\), since the positive exponential series gives \(e^2>7\).

The order reverses between two bandwidths explicitly tested in Table 3. All exponentials are ordinary finite positive values; numerical underflow is irrelevant. Replicating each embedding type \(m\) times multiplies both scores by \(m\) and preserves the reversal, so the phenomenon is not tied to \(n=2\). The replication claim concerns the scoring functional at fixed embeddings, not a claim that retraining on duplicated data leaves a model unchanged.

## Alternative readings, impact, and supported correction

If the paragraph is read only as a description of Table 3, the reported empirical correlations can remain correct. The counterexample instead refutes the algebraic explanation that the power transformation itself guarantees the same relative trends. No actual contamination labels or LLM training trajectory are assigned to these synthetic configurations.

Assessment: Major. The invalid inference directly supports a claimed robustness property of the central contamination-ranking statistic. Its dependency is Eq. (6) \(\rightarrow\) bandwidth-independent ranking rationale \(\rightarrow\) the interpretation of design robustness. This assessment does not require invalidating every empirical result, and it does not assert such invalidation.

A defensible correction retains the valid expansion in [eq:score] (#eq:score), removes the invariance inference, and describes Table 3 as empirical robustness within its tested configurations. A general invariance result would need additional joint conditions on pairwise distances, distance changes, and normalization; none is verified here. This correction repairs the logical scope of the explanation, not the empirical method.

# Problem 2: the default source uses separately fitted bandwidths

Problem 2 (The common-bandwidth interpretation does not specify the released default).  The released default compares two kernels fitted with different bandwidths, whereas the paper’s distance-change decomposition uses a common bandwidth.

## Source evidence and exact functional difference

In ``, lines 10–16 estimate the inverse median of off-diagonal Euclidean distances. Lines 85–86 estimate this quantity independently before and after tuning; lines 90–94 construct the kernels and score. The parser in ``, line 195, defaults `` to ``, and the released experiment scripts omit that option. If a numerical `` is supplied, lines 87–88 instead use a common value [Retained latex: \cite{code}].

Equation reference: eq:adaptive. Let \(g\) and \(h\) be the pre- and post-tuning estimates. The default computes \[
S_{g,h}
=-\frac{\sum_{i,j}e^{-g u_{ij}}\left|h v_{ij}-g u_{ij}\right|}
        {\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}e^{-g u_{ij}}}}.\] This follows from the static expression and the documented pointwise operation of the pinned PyTorch 2.1 loss [Retained latex: \cite{torch}]. No author function was run. Equation [eq:adaptive] (#eq:adaptive) measures changes in bandwidth-scaled distances; it reduces to [eq:score] (#eq:score) when \(g=h\).

## A difference that can change rankings

Use the same pre-tuning rows for two configurations, \[Z=((1,0),(0,1),(-1,0)),\] and post-tuning rows \[Z'_A=((1,0),(0,1),(1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)),\qquad
Z'_B=((1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)).\] All rows are unit vectors. The three unordered squared distances are \((2,4,2)\) before tuning, \((2,2-\sqrt2,2-\sqrt2)\) after \(A\), and \((2,2,4)\) after \(B\). The inverse median Euclidean distances are consequently \[g=\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\qquad
h_A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}},\qquad h_B=g.\] The shared pre-kernel normalizer is \[E=\sqrt{3+4e^{-\sqrt2}+2e^{-2\sqrt2}}
  =2.0225425658958995.\] Direct evaluation of the two definitions yields

Configuration | Common bandwidth \(g\) | Separate default bandwidths

\(A\) | \(-0.381510516420\) | \(-0.564793914760\)

\(B\) | \(-0.422643398152\) | \(-0.422643398152\)

Thus \(A>B\) in the common-bandwidth definition but \(A<B\) under the default. This is not just an overall rescaling.

Even the unchanged pair \((1,2)\) in \(A\) receives a nonzero adaptive contribution: its squared distance remains \(2\), but the log ratio is \(2(h_A-g)=1.198912367380\), and its weighted absolute term is \(0.291475659630\). The common-bandwidth contribution is zero.

## Alternative interpretation and bounded repair

The paper mentions a median heuristic without explicitly saying whether it is fitted once or twice. The intended method may therefore be adaptive, with the distinction suppressed in notation. The adaptive statistic is not intrinsically invalid, and the displayed common-bandwidth algebra remains correct under its stated common parameter.

Assessment: Minor, for a supported specification and reproducibility discrepancy. The mapping of published tables to the two variants remains Needs Evidence. The acquired commit does not identify the experiment-generating implementation, so the example does not prove that a published correlation is wrong.

The precise resolution is to state which bandwidth policy each experiment used, identify its code/settings, and use [eq:adaptive] (#eq:adaptive) when separate estimates apply. If a common bandwidth is intended, fit it once and reuse it before evaluating that variant. This review verifies the two formulas and their finite difference; it does not verify a new experimental result. The absolute-value operation should be retained.

# Problem 3: the normalization plot cannot be the defined raw sum

Problem 3 (The quantitative evidence for the normalizer is mislabeled or incompletely specified).  Figure 6’s stated kernel sum is incompatible with the diagonal contribution of the defined RBF kernel.

## Source statement and decisive check

Appendix D, p. 15, motivates the square-root normalizer by describing the sum of kernel entries as linear in subset size. It cites a linear-fit \(R^2=0.9766\) and Figure 6. In the actual rendered graph, the horizontal axis runs from \(350\) to \(700\) samples, while the ordinate labeled Kernel Entry Sum has observed values approximately \(55\)–\(165\). No axis multiplier or rescaling is displayed [Retained latex: \cite[p.~15]{paper}].

Equation reference: eq:diagonal. For any finite RBF bandwidth and finite embeddings, \[
K_{ii}=1,\quad K_{ij}>0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T_n:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K_{ij}
=n+\sum_{i\ne j}K_{ij}\ge n.\] Every plotted value is below the corresponding \(n\). In particular, a value near \(55\) at \(n=350\) cannot be the full sum. This argument needs no assumptions about membership, training, unit normalization, or a particular bandwidth choice.

A scaled sum, a square root, or an off-diagonal-only quantity could explain the graph. Figure 2 explicitly zeroes its diagonal for visualization, so a similar convention is a plausible alternative; Figure 6 does not disclose it. None of these interpretations equals the full raw sum in [eq:diagonal] (#eq:diagonal). The plot alone cannot identify the correct replacement label.

## What the linear fit does and does not establish

Equation reference: eq:expected. For IID embeddings from a fixed distribution and a fixed kernel, \[
\mathbb E[T_n]=n+n(n-1)\mu,\qquad
\mu=\mathbb E[k(Z_1,Z_2)]>0.\] This calculation is not asserted unchanged when the bandwidth is re-estimated from each subset. It shows why a finite linear fit does not by itself establish a scaling law. As an independent illustration, fitting a line to the exactly quadratic values \(n^2\) at \(n=350,400,\ldots,700\) gives \[\widehat T_n=1050n-262500,\qquad R^2=\frac{441}{445}
=0.991011235955,\] which exceeds the reported fit despite being exactly quadratic. The case \(K_{ij}=1\) is realized by equal unit embeddings at any fixed positive bandwidth; the illustration is not a reconstruction of the authors’ plotted data.

## Impact and correction

Assessment: Minor. The displayed quantity cannot support Appendix D’s stated account of the raw sum. This does not establish incorrect score computation or changed fixed-size benchmark correlations.

The paper claims mitigation of size effects, not exact size invariance. Indeed, for fixed embedding maps and common bandwidth, replacing every row by \(m\) copies multiplies the numerator by \(m^2\) and \(E\) by \(m\), so \(S_{\rm replicated}=mS\). The normalizer reduces this replication scaling from quadratic to linear; non-invariance alone is not a separate broken promise. Unequal-size cross-benchmark comparisons still need an appropriate scope or calibration.

A supported repair requires the original plotting calculation: identify diagonal handling, scale transformations, and bandwidth policy; then replot or relabel the actual quantity and explain its relation to \(E\). Do not infer a specific relabeling from the graph. Replace the scaling-law inference with a bounded account of the finite observation. Empirical size robustness of the full score would require additional measurements.

# Problem 4: model-specific membership in the Pile experiment is unresolved

Problem 4 (Needs Evidence: split labels are not sufficient evidence of training membership).  The Pile evaluation requires a documented link between dataset splits and the target model’s actual pretraining exposure.

Section 6, p. 8, treats Pile training samples as seen and validation samples as unseen, then interprets Tables 5–6 as contamination validation without temporal confounding [Retained latex: \cite{paper}]. The released `` selects `` for all six sources. The registry maps that choice to ``. In ``, lines 8–15 load the `` and `` splits of `` and assign labels one and zero directly [Retained latex: \cite{code}].

Let \(L\) denote this split label and \(M\) actual membership in the target model’s pretraining corpus. A mixture fraction of \(L=1\) equals the intended exposure fraction only with an additional relationship, for example \[\Pr(M=1\mid L=1)=1,\qquad
\Pr(M=1\mid L=0)=0.\] A weaker relation could support a weaker interpretation, but it must be specified. IID splitting by itself does not prove the target-model relationship. The inspected paper and release do not establish it.

Random train/validation splitting is a sensible way to reduce temporal cues, and the assigned labels could be correct. The issue is missing provenance, not demonstrated mislabeling. The released commit is also not proven to have generated Tables 5–6. Those tables directly report correlations with the constructed labels; their interpretation as validated pretraining exposure depends on the missing link.

Assessment: Needs Evidence. This item does not raise the supported severity. It concerns the principal empirical response to temporal confounding, so resolving it matters. Supply model-specific training/held-out provenance, or use a model with documented membership for the evaluated splits. If that link cannot be established for existing results, a new evaluation is needed; a prose-only replacement is not a verified empirical repair.

# Overall dependence, checked results, and version status

Problem 1 is the only Major finding: it invalidates the mathematical explanation of bandwidth-independent rankings, while leaving the observed Table 3 correlations possible. Problems 2 and 3 are individually Minor; their scope must not inherit the paper-level assessment. Problem 4 remains an unresolved evidence requirement.

Several checks support the paper within its actual scope. The common-bandwidth RBF expansion is correct. The reported KDS averages in Tables 1, 6, and 10 agree with arithmetic to rounding: Spearman \(0.990333\ldots\), Pearson \(0.982\) in Table 1; Spearman \(0.944166\ldots\), Pearson \(0.947833\ldots\) in Table 6; and average MAPE \(0.1818\) in Table 10. Such evidence should not be relabeled as a failed attempt to estimate absolute contamination probabilities, because the stated target is a relative score.
