# 2. Checked claims, definitions and review limits

The paper studies a system that samples two models, presents their responses anonymously, and receives a preference vote. An attacker may control the prompt, directly query candidate models, train a binary target-model detector, and preferentially vote after identifying the target. The study explicitly uses simulated environments and does not claim an unmitigated live-production attack. Existing identity filters and bot defenses, model-family versus exact-version identification, model-owner advantages and watermark exceptions are acknowledged in the source.

The training-based experiment uses 22 models, eight prompt categories, 200 prompts per category and 50 responses per model per prompt. Its stated binary dataset has 50 positive target responses and 50 uniformly sampled negative responses, split 80/20 for logistic regression. Figure 3 concerns test accuracy for this balanced per-target task, not 22-way model identification or positive predictive value in the full leaderboard. Section 3.1 separately assumes 95% accuracy with symmetric 5% false-positive and false-negative rates for the reranking simulation.

A vote and an interaction are distinct: an interaction includes a query on which the attacker abstains. The simulation recomputes rankings at intervals of 1,000 interactions using a historical testbed described in Appendix C.4 as 1,670,250 votes from 477,322 users. The reported win and tie counts, 1,093,875 and 576,375, sum correctly to that total. No historical ratings or raw vote logs were independently refitted here.

For the defense, Appendix B considers independent observations under a known benign profile, and then a second scenario with known benign and malicious profiles. The latter uses true versus perturbed Bradley–Terry ratings to form a likelihood ratio and invokes Neyman–Pearson optimality. The following verification concerns that actual probability construction. It does not invent a universal model-identification theorem or an optimal adversarial-voting theorem that the paper does not state.

Main paper pp.1–9 and Appendices A–D pp.13–18 were read. Critical formulas and tables were checked against rendered source pages. Relevant public-source searches found no attributable detector, simulator or defense implementation to pin and inspect. That is a reproducibility limit, not evidence of a code defect. No author code was executed or imported, no model/API queries or training were performed, and no live leaderboard was interacted with.

# 3. Problem 1 — Invalid vote probabilities undermine the likelihood-ratio mitigation

Faithful source claim and location. Section 4.2.3, p.7, and Appendix B Scenario 2, p.14, define Pr_B(i) as the probability that a benign user votes for model i, with a corresponding adversarial distribution based on perturbed ratings. Equations (4)–(6) construct the likelihood ratio from these distributions. Eq.(5) gives the usual Bradley–Terry pairwise probability, but Eq.(6) forms each supposed categorical component by multiplying over opponents:

\[p_{ij}(Q)=\frac1{1+\exp(-(Q_i-Q_j)/s)},\qquad
q_i(Q)=\prod_{j\ne i}p_{ij}(Q).\] The product is written here with self-comparisons excluded, the most charitable interpretation of the paper’s product over j. Including a self-comparison merely supplies another factor of 1/2 and does not resolve the problem. The invalid inference replaces the event that model i wins one sampled duel by the event that it wins every hypothetical duel.

Complete counterexample and all operative assumptions. Use three available models, positive scale s = 1, finite benign ratings Q = (log(1), log(2), log(4)), and uniformly select one of the three unordered non-self pairs at each interaction. Conditional on the chosen pair, select its winner by exactly Eq.(5). Successive pairs and votes are independent. There are no ties, a permissible decisive-vote subcase of the stated binary comparison model. Both profiles in the example below are fixed and known, as required by the simple-hypothesis likelihood argument. No assumption about a detector or model-response classifier is needed for this downstream statistical step.

\[(p_{12},p_{13},p_{21},p_{23},p_{31},p_{32})
=\left(\frac13,\frac15,\frac23,\frac13,\frac45,\frac23\right).\] Direct multiplication produces the paper’s alleged probabilities, which fail to sum to one:

\[q_B=\left(\frac1{15},\frac29,\frac8{15}\right),\qquad
\sum_i q_{B,i}=\frac{37}{45}.\] Normalizing these products would give the following vector. The actual probability of a winner, however, is obtained by summing over the mutually exclusive sampled pairs, giving a different vector:

\[\widetilde q_B=\left(\frac3{37},\frac{10}{37},\frac{24}{37}\right),\qquad
p_B=\left(\frac8{45},\frac13,\frac{22}{45}\right).\] For example, model 1 wins only when pair {1,2} or {1,3} is selected, so its probability is (1/3)(1/3) + (1/3)(1/5) = 8/45. The three actual probabilities sum to one. With equal ratings the product would give 1/4 for each model, summing to 3/4; that simpler example shows missing mass but is insufficient alone because normalization happens to fix the symmetric case. The unequal-rating calculation establishes that this is not merely an omitted normalizer.

A stronger exact check: the likelihood-ratio ordering reverses. Let the malicious user’s perturbed strengths be (1,4,4), or ratings Q_M = (log(1), log(4), log(4)), under the same independent uniform-pair sampling. This is an admissible finite perturbation of the known ratings. Its true and normalized-product winner laws are

\[p_M=\left(\frac2{15},\frac{13}{30},\frac{13}{30}\right),\qquad
\widetilde q_M=\left(\frac1{21},\frac{10}{21},\frac{10}{21}\right).\] Consider two positive-probability four-vote winner sequences, A = (1,1,1,2) and B = (3,3,3,3). The alternative-to-benign likelihood ratios using the correct categorical laws are

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{true}}(A)=
\left(\frac34\right)^3\frac{13}{10}
=\frac{351}{640}=0.5484375,\] \[\Lambda_{\mathrm{true}}(B)=
\left(\frac{39}{44}\right)^4
\approx0.617230989.\] The normalized-product construction instead gives

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{product}}(A)=
\left(\frac{37}{63}\right)^3\frac{37}{21}
\approx0.356915947,\] \[\Lambda_{\mathrm{product}}(B)=
\left(\frac{185}{252}\right)^4
\approx0.290458942.\] Thus the correct likelihood ranks B above A as evidence for the alternative, whereas the paper’s construction ranks A above B. For sequences of the same length, an omitted normalizing constant can be absorbed into the rejection threshold; it cannot repair this strict reversal. Equivalently, the true A/B ratio is 29282/32955 < 1, while the product-based ratio is 768/625 > 1.

Alternative interpretations and counterevidence. Under additional independence assumptions, the product could describe winning separate hypothetical matches against every opponent. Those events are not the categorical outcomes of the single duel actually presented, and they need not exhaust the sample space. A separately normalized artificial distribution is mathematically definable, but it is not the stated Bradley–Terry voting law. A practical detector based on a different, empirically calibrated score could still work. The standard Neyman–Pearson lemma itself remains correct for properly specified simple hypotheses.

Core dependency and impact. Pairwise ratings feed the purported full vote distributions; those distributions feed the likelihood ratio and the proposed optimal detector for ranking-aware attackers. That construction supports the Scenario 2 mitigation discussed with Figure 4b–c and the attack-cost analysis. The exact invalid inference is therefore linked to a scientific contribution, even though it appears in the publisher’s appendix. In the earlier preprint it appears in the main mitigation section, pp.9–10, with Eq.(6) on p.10; the publisher version relocates rather than repairs this step.

Judgment: Major for the substantial invalid statistical derivation. No evidence establishes which formula generated Figure 4, so the review does not declare its plotted detection rates false. The attack, authentication and rate-limit mechanisms do not depend on Eq.(6) and are not refuted.

A precise local repair. If only winner identities are retained, use the pair-sampling probabilities pi_ij to marginalize correctly. If actual pairs and their binary outcomes are retained, use the conditional Bernoulli likelihood:

\[P_H(\mathrm{winner}=i)=\sum_{j\ne i}\pi_{ij}p_{ij}(Q_H),\] \[L_H(y\mid\{i_t,j_t\}_{t=1}^{n})
=\prod_{t=1}^{n}p_{i_tj_t}(Q_H)^{y_t}
\bigl(1-p_{i_tj_t}(Q_H)\bigr)^{1-y_t}.\] Here pi_ij is the probability of the unordered pair {i,j}, the unordered-pair probabilities sum to one, and y_t = 1 denotes a vote for i_t. This correction assumes fixed known ratings and independent decisive votes conditional on pairs. A hypothesis-dependent pair-selection law must also enter the joint likelihood; retained ties require their own probability model. The corrected construction is analytically verified under these assumptions. It does not by itself repair the historical simulation, calibrate sequential testing, or reproduce Figure 4.

# 5. Problem 3 — The one-position vote bound overstates the reported results

Source claim and location. Section 3.2, p.5, states that moving a model up or down by one position requires fewer than 1,000 votes in the simulated setting just discussed. Table 4(a), p.6, contradicts this blanket numerical statement in two directly checkable cases:

Model and one-position move | Published adversarial votes | Conflict with strict bound

gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18: rank 4 to 3 | 1,236 | 236 above 1,000

gpt-4o-2024-05-13: rank 3 to 4 | 3,125 | 2,125 above 1,000

Operative assumptions are exactly the reported Section 3.1 scenario: the historical voting testbed, the stated 95% detector with symmetric error assumption, passive behavior when the target is not detected, and the table’s own simulation results. A one-position change means an absolute rank difference of one. The quantities above are votes from Table 4(a), not the larger interaction counts in Table 4(b).

The eight adjacent movements available among the five models in Table 4 use 557, 454, 3,125, 163, 696, 903, 1,236 and 322 votes. Six of eight are below 1,000, and the full range is 163–3,125. Thus a statement about most tested movements is defensible; the strict universal bound is not.

Judgment: Minor. This is a bounded overstatement of a quantitative empirical summary. The approximate thousand-vote scale in the abstract and the broader conclusion that a few thousand simulated adversarial votes can change rankings remain compatible with the table. The repair is to state the observed range or qualify the sub-1,000 statement as applying to most tested adjacent movements. This is a direct consistency check, not a rerun validating the reported counts or an allegation about live voting.

# 6. Scope clarifications and unresolved evidence

Detector accuracy and simulation errors. In a balanced test set, accuracy = 1 - (FPR + FNR)/2. Accuracy of 95% does not identify both conditional error rates as 5%. The paper explicitly makes symmetry a simulation assumption in Section 3.1 and footnote 3; it is not treated here as a demonstrated false inference. Confusion matrices, prompt-selection validation and transfer from the 22-model detector pool to the larger ranking testbed would be needed to establish realized operating rates. Repeated values near 95.7–95.8% in Figure 3 alone do not prove class imbalance or a majority-class classifier, especially given the stated balanced construction. The split is not stated to be stratified, so the balanced-accuracy identity is not asserted to hold exactly for every 20-example test subset.

Vote versus interaction counts. Several table ratios are close to the target’s pair-inclusion probability in a large uniformly sampled model pool. Independent false positives on both non-target responses would ordinarily produce additional votes. The paper does not fully specify dependence between detector outputs, both-positive handling, or exactly which votes enter each count, and no simulation implementation was located. These ratios are a diagnostic requiring provenance, not a demonstrated omission of false positives or a basis for increasing severity.

Finite-sample calibration. Appendix B Eq.(3), p.13, reports an empirical Monte Carlo tail proportion K/m and rejects when it is below 0.01. Such a proportion is not automatically an exact finite-sample p-value. For exchangeable null statistics, the standard rank construction (K+1)/(m+1), with an appropriate conservative treatment of ties, avoids zero-probability Monte Carlo tail claims. The actual m used in Figure 4 is not reported, so no specific false-positive inflation in that figure is established. A bounded illustration and its limits appear in Appendix A below.

Benign-user assumptions and utility. Scenario 1 assumes known benign probabilities and independent votes. Persistent personal preferences, sampling changes and repeated looks would require corresponding calibration, but are not counterexamples satisfying that fixed null. The paper itself shows weak power against ranking-aware attackers and acknowledges that larger rating perturbations harm leaderboard utility. No claim of protection against every well-resourced adversary is attributed to it.

Cost model. The additive form ceil(N/m)c_account + N c_action + c_detector correctly separates accounts, actions and a one-time detector cost under its stated definitions. The approximately $2.20 per prompt follows the illustrative 10-proprietary/20-open-model pool, 512 output tokens and 50 responses using the price inputs supplied in Appendix C.3. This is a conditional output-token calculation, not a verified historical invoice or a current-price estimate. The 200-prompt budget and full eight-category acquisition scope should be distinguished; no unsupported claim of a measured total cost error is made.

Other experimental checks. The Table 8 difference for a 50-position move, from 1,246 votes at accuracy 1.0 to 1,383 at 0.9, is 137 and supports the text’s approximate 150-vote statement. Table 9 supplies alternative non-detection actions, but its full configuration and historical provenance are unavailable. Identity-probing results are appropriately limited by family-level names, disclosed post-processing filters and model-specific failures. A purely normative recommendation to use authentication or rate limiting is not assigned an invented experimental requirement.

from fractions import Fraction as F
from functools import reduce
from operator import mul

def prod(xs):
    return reduce(mul, xs, F(1))

def laws(w):
    k = len(w)
    q = [prod(F(w[i], w[i]+w[j])
              for j in range(k) if j != i) for i in range(k)]
    pair = F(1, k*(k-1)//2)
    p = [sum((pair*F(w[i], w[i]+w[j])
              for j in range(k) if j != i), F(0)) for i in range(k)]
    return p, q, [x/sum(q) for x in q]

pB, qB, zB = laws([1, 2, 4])
pM, qM, zM = laws([1, 4, 4])
r = [a/b for a, b in zip(pM, pB)]
