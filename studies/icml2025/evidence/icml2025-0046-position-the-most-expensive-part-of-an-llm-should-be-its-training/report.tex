\# 4. Problem 1: an unsupported affordability inference\par
\#\# Source claim and exact location\par
On p. 4, Section 3.2, the paper states that three of ten organizations have estimated data costs exceeding total annual revenue and treats this as making compensation financially infeasible. It then says the findings suggest that only the wealthiest companies could still train state-of-the-art LLMs if required to compensate creators. The company-level infeasibility inference is categorical; the broader population statement is qualified. Figure 3, p. 6, supplies the cost/revenue display.\par
\#\# Suspect step\par
\[D>R\;\not\Rightarrow\;\text{inability to finance an immediate payment of }D.\] Annual revenue is a flow over a period. An upfront purchase can be funded from existing unrestricted resources. A large D/R can describe burden, but the statistic does not identify the available stock of funds, other commitments, or financing. This is a structural failure of the asserted inference, separate from whether its conclusion happens to be true for any particular company.\par

\[D/R=2>1,\qquad D/T=100,\qquad E>T.\] \[B-D-E=1{,}925{,}000{,}000>0,\qquad R-E=1{,}925{,}000{,}000>0.\] The integer word count, wage and writing speed yield D exactly. Both the revenue threshold and the paper’s large data-to-training-cost premise hold. The company can pay the full D and prepay every specified other annual outlay before collecting the year’s revenue, while its annual nondataset balance is positive. B is already net of prior commitments. Nothing in the source excludes this configuration. It is an analytic countermodel with exact rational arithmetic, not a financial estimate for a real company.\par
\#\# Precise consequence and alternative readings\par
The same D and R are compatible with different available resources and different abilities to pay. Therefore the reported ratio does not establish the stated infeasibility conclusion for the three companies. The cash-rich hypothetical itself has substantial wealth: it does not disprove the separate population claim about only the wealthiest organizations, and it establishes no named company’s actual solvency.\par
A descriptive burden interpretation of D/R is legitimate. The error arises when it is used as a sufficient affordability test. Sections 4.3 and 5 discuss royalties and amortization, so the source must not be represented as claiming compensation impossible under every payment arrangement. Those passages do not rescue the specific upfront inference because this countermodel pays in full immediately. The normative preference for compensating creators is not tested or refuted by this balance-sheet example.\par

\# 5. Dependency, severity, and surviving contributions\par
The dependency is explicit. The p. 2 introduction says the analysis establishes impracticality outside the wealthiest organizations. The opening of Section 4 (p. 4) presents infeasibility outside resource-rich organizations as a tension surfaced by Section 3. Section 4.3 (p. 7, Sustainable Compensation Structures) invokes Section 3 to justify its claim that upfront compensation is prohibitive and then motivates alternative payment structures.\par
Major is supported by the demonstrated invalid inference at a central quantitative-to-practical step. The rating does not depend on refuting the entire position paper, proving changes to LLM scores, or making the argument hard to repair. Missing financial data alone would be Needs Evidence; the distinct demonstrated defect is that the stated statistic cannot entail the attributed conclusion.\par
What survives: the replacement-cost calculation conditional on its inputs, the large scale of the modeled expense, the ethical case for creator compensation, and the value of investigating royalties, data efficiency, and attribution. A royalty proposal can remain sensible after this justification is narrowed. The relative affordability of the actual organizations and the distributional prediction require additional evidence.\par
\# 6. Scoped repair and remaining evidence\par
Defensible replacement: under the stated replacement-cost model, Figure 3 displays data costs above one year’s reported revenue for three of ten organizations. This measures expense relative to revenue and does not by itself determine whether payment can be financed. The display counts and the logical scope of this replacement are checked. This repair does not restore the original affordability conclusion or independently validate the figure’s inputs.\par
To establish actual company affordability, one needs dated unrestricted resources, commitments, financing access, and payment timing. This is financial evidence, not a requirement for new LLM experiments. Reconstructing the 64-model comparison would require the selected rows, token-to-word mapping, treatment of repeated presentations and multimodal content, and whether engineering labor is present in each training-cost denominator. No specific double counting or denominator error is established.\par
\[nT>D\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n>D/T\qquad(D,T>0).\] Section 5 ultimately supplies the correct amortization dependency above. Its introductory wording is broader than this algebra, but the subsequent explanation qualifies the argument; no separate substantive Problem is retained. The future-growth statements and research proposals are interpreted as extrapolation and recommendations, not as new measured outcomes. The wage inputs, historical costs, forecast inputs and complete cited literature were not independently reconstructed.\par
