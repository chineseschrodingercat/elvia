Abstract: The deterministic reduction, its truncation on the stated regularity event, and the fixed-matrix Gaussian rescaling are reconstructed. One local range defect is established: Lemma 3.3 is false for unrestricted positive \(\varepsilon\), although its small-\(\varepsilon\) use admits a direct Schur-complement proof. Mixed spectral parameters, pole endpoints, and Gaussian event notation require clarification; the corrected local calculations are given explicitly. These checks do not certify the random regularity estimates, the complete Fourier replacement argument, or the main universality theorem. Scientific adjudication and independent acceptance remain pending.

The main random matrix has iid real subgaussian entries of mean zero and variance one. Constants can depend on the entry law. Theorem 1.2 compares its least singular-value distribution with the real Gaussian one, with relative error of order \((\log n)^{-1/16}\) and an exponentially small additive error. The small-scale proof works between exponential and polynomial thresholds; the large-scale and extremely small regimes invoke external results. No complex-matrix extension or uniformity over unbounded subgaussian constants is assumed.

Equation reference: eq:chi. For a deterministic \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\), remove its last row \(Y\) to obtain \(A^*\). When \(A^*\) has full row rank, let \[s_i=\sigma_i(A^*)>0,\quad u_0\in\ker A^*,\quad \|u_0\|=1,\quad
y_i=\langle u_i,Y\rangle,\quad y_0=\langle u_0,Y\rangle,\] where the \(u_i\) form an orthonormal singular basis. Put \[t=\varepsilon n^{-1/2},\qquad
 \widetilde\chi^2=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{y_i^2}{s_i^2}.
 \] All 29 source pages were read for statements and dependencies. Independent derivation is concentrated on Sections 3–4 and the fixed-matrix part of Section 9. Reading the remaining proofs is not a claim of their full verification.

# Problem 1: Lemma 3.3 needs its small-parameter range

Classification: Minor. Location: Lemma 3.3, p. 7, implication (21), and its proof on p. 8.

Equation reference: eq:claimed. The stated hypothesis is \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(s_{n-1}\geq \varepsilon^{3/4}n^{-1/2}\). The claimed implication is \[\sigma_n(A)\leq \varepsilon n^{-1/2}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 |y_0|\leq (1+\varepsilon^{1/4})\varepsilon n^{-1/2}\widetilde\chi .
 \] There is no upper bound on \(\varepsilon\) in this deterministic statement.

For any \(n\geq2\), take \(\varepsilon=1\), \(t=n^{-1/2}\) and \[A=\operatorname{diag}(t,\ldots,t,3t),\qquad u_0=e_n.\] Then \(A^*\) has full row rank with every positive singular value equal to \(t\). Its last row is \(Y=3te_n\), so \(y_i=0\) for \(i<n\), \(\widetilde\chi=1\), and \(|y_0|=3t\). Both the spectral-gap hypothesis and the antecedent hold with equality: \(s_{n-1}=t=\varepsilon^{3/4}n^{-1/2}\) and \(\sigma_n(A)=t\). The conclusion would require \(3t\leq2t\). Thus the unrestricted statement is false. The example uses a unit kernel vector and works for arbitrarily large \(n\); it does not exploit missing normalization or small dimension.

## Exact repair in the range actually used

Equation reference: eq:schur. For \(0<t<s_{n-1}\), a Schur complement in \(A^{\mathsf T}A-t^2I\) gives the exact criterion \[\sigma_n(A)\leq t
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 y_0^2\leq t^2\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}
                  \frac{y_i^2}{s_i^2-t^2}\right).
 \] Indeed the upper-left block is positive definite and its scalar Schur complement is \[-t^2+\frac{y_0^2}{1+\sum_i y_i^2/(s_i^2-t^2)}.\] This argument includes \(y_0=0\) and equality at the tested threshold.

If \(0<\varepsilon\leq1/16\) and \(s_{n-1}\geq\varepsilon^{3/4}n^{-1/2}\), then \(t<s_{n-1}\) and \[1+\sum_i\frac{y_i^2}{s_i^2-t^2}
 \leq \frac{\widetilde\chi^2}{1-\sqrt\varepsilon}.\] Writing \(q=\varepsilon^{1/4}\leq1/2\), \[(1+q)^2(1-q^2)-1=q(2-2q^2-q^3)>0.\] Taking square roots proves [eq:claimed] (#eq:claimed) throughout this corrected range.

The converse implication (22) also has a robust proof. If \(\sigma_n(A)>t\), rank-one interlacing gives \(s_{n-1}>t\); [eq:schur] (#eq:schur) then implies \(|y_0|>t\widetilde\chi\). If \(A^*\) is rank deficient, \(A\) is singular and this antecedent is impossible; the undefined inverse weights should not be evaluated on that stratum.

The counterexample lies outside the small-scale range used to prove the main theorem. Restricting the deterministic lemma as above preserves that use, and Section 4’s range \(\varepsilon\leq(\log n)^{-4}\) eventually lies inside it. This is the reason for a local Minor assessment. It is not a counterexample to a random-matrix theorem and does not establish a failure of the claimed leading probability constant.

# Spectral notation and pole endpoints

Equation reference: eq:det. Lemma 3.2, p. 7, mixes \(s_i^2-x\) in the product with \(s_i^2-x^2\) in the denominator, while calling \(x\) a singular value. The determinant calculation immediately below uses the squared spectral parameter. With \(\lambda\) denoting that parameter, the consistent polynomial identity is \[\det(A^{\mathsf T}A-\lambda I)
 =\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(s_i^2-\lambda)
   \left(1+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{y_i^2}{s_i^2-\lambda}\right),
 \qquad s_0=0.
 \] The right side is interpreted after polynomial cancellation, or by continuity at its apparent poles. To use the singular-value variable \(x\), replace every \(\lambda\) by \(x^2\).

For example, \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\) has singular values \(2,3\). The mixed formula printed in the lemma gives \((-x)(4-x)(1-9/x^2)\), which equals \(5\) at \(x=2\). The corrected determinant is \((4-\lambda)(9-\lambda)\). This check identifies a spectral-parameter inconsistency; the following determinant proof already points to the correction. It is treated as a clarification rather than an additional substantive Problem.

The assertion before (23) that the least singular value is always an interior secular root also needs an endpoint convention. For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)\) the true least singular value is \(1\), but the equation on \((0,1)\) becomes \(1=4/x^2\) and has no root there. The first singular direction has zero projection onto \(Y\), so the claimed divergence at that pole is absent. The Schur criterion [eq:schur] (#eq:schur) avoids this issue in the relevant range \(t<s_{n-1}\). No generic nonzero-projection assumption is silently imposed on discrete random entries.

# The truncation estimate on the regularity event

Equation reference: eq:lower. On the event \(R\) defined on p. 9, let \(\ell=\lfloor\sqrt{\log n}\rfloor\), \(k_0=\lfloor(\log\log n)^2\rfloor\) and \[\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{y_{n-i}^2}{s_{n-i}^2}.\] Rounding these indices does not change the asymptotic estimates. For sufficiently large \(n\), \(k_0\leq\ell\). The two conditions \(R_3,R_4\) give directly \[\chi^2\geq \frac{n}{(\log\log n)^6}
                  \sum_{i\leq k_0}y_{n-i}^2
       \geq \frac{n}{(\log\log n)^5}.
 \] Thus this lower bound applies to the retained sum itself, not merely to the full sum.

For \(i>\ell\), \(R_2\) gives \(y_{n-i}^2\leq i^{1/4}\) and \(s_{n-i}^2\geq i^{3/2}/n\), once \(n\) is large enough that \(\ell^{1/8}\geq\log\log n\). Consequently \[0\leq \widetilde\chi^2-\chi^2
 \leq1+n\sum_{i>\ell}i^{-5/4}
 \leq1+4n\ell^{-1/4}.\] Together with [eq:lower] (#eq:lower), this yields \[\frac{\widetilde\chi^2-\chi^2}{\chi^2}
 =O\!\left((\log\log n)^5
       \big((\log n)^{-1/8}+n^{-1}\big)\right)
 =o((\log n)^{-1/16}).\] The additional deterministic loss \(\varepsilon^{1/4}\) is smaller than the displayed logarithmic error in the intended range. This verifies the deterministic content of Lemma 4.3. It does not prove that the random event \(R^c\) has the small joint probability asserted in Lemma 4.2; that requires Sections 5–7 and their external inputs.

# A direct Gaussian rescaling check

Lemma 9.2, pp. 21–22, starts from the correct two-sided event \(|W_0|\leq\varepsilon f\) for independent standard Gaussians, but its proof subsequently writes \(W_0\leq\varepsilon f\) and uses a bound of order \(\varepsilon f\). Taken literally, the latter event has probability \(1/2\) at \(\varepsilon=0\), whereas the claimed rescaling has zero right-hand side. The initial lemma statement makes the intended absolute value unambiguous.

There is also a cutoff mismatch: Lemma 9.2 assumes \(s_{n-1}\geq(\log n)^{-1}n^{-1/2}\), whereas \(E^*\) on p. 18 only supplies \((\log n)^{-3}n^{-1/2}\). The latter event does not imply the former. The following calculation proves the rescaling under the weaker cutoff actually available, so these local issues do not supply a main-theorem counterexample.

Equation reference: eq:gaussian. Put \(L=\log n\) and \[f=\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{W_i^2}{s_{n-i}^2}\right)^{1/2},
 \qquad h(z)=\mathbb P(|W_0|\leq z)
            =\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\int_0^z e^{-r^2/2}\,dr .\] For all \(z\geq0\), \[0\leq \sqrt{2/\pi}\,z-h(z)\leq \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}6z^3,\] and \(h(z)/z\) decreases on \((0,\infty)\). Therefore, for \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_0\), \[0\leq \mathbb E h(\varepsilon f)
       -\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}\mathbb E h(\varepsilon_0f)
 \leq \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}6\,
          \varepsilon\varepsilon_0^2\,\mathbb E f^3.
 \] The independence of \(W_0\) from \(f\) is essential here.

If \(s_{n-1}\geq L^{-3}n^{-1/2}\), then \(f\leq L^3\|W\|_2\). Since \(\mathbb E\|W\|_2^4=\ell^2+2\ell\), Hölder’s inequality gives \(\mathbb Ef^3\leq C L^{39/4}\) for \(\ell\leq\sqrt L\). For \(\varepsilon_0\leq n^{-c/2}\), the error in [eq:gaussian] (#eq:gaussian) is consequently \[O\!\left(\varepsilon n^{-c}L^{39/4}\right)
   =o\!\left(\varepsilon L^{-1/16}\right).\] This proves the fixed-matrix rescaling, including \(\varepsilon=0\), with the correct event and the \(E^*\) cutoff. It avoids the printed one-sided probability and the inconsistent intermediate rescaling display on p. 22.

Passing from this calculation to Lemma 9.1 still requires the exceptional-matrix estimates, the extension to an independent Gaussian last row, and the external polynomial-scale universality and Gaussian distribution results. Those steps are not certified here.
