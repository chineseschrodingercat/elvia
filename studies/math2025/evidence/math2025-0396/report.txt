# Assessment and scope

The variational definition gives a useful framework for discussing nonsmooth cross-curvature. The finite-product argument, the algebraic cost-submersion argument, the compact Gromov–Hausdorff limit argument, and several explicit constructions can be checked directly. However, the submitted version requires major revision. Four substantive issues are established below: a counterexample to the random-variable stability proposition; a false graph inclusion in the measurable-selection argument supporting the principal lifting theorem; an invalid base-space hypothesis in the advertised LMP non-lifting example; and an incompatible use of two cone costs in the WFR application. These have different logical strengths. Only the first is presented as an all-hypothesis counterexample to its stated stability proposition. The second is a decisive failure of a central proof step. The third invalidates the stated counterexample to preservation. The fourth invalidates the written WFR formulas and the stated deduction for the sphere, without asserting that the correctly defined WFR space on the sphere is not NNCC.

Nine local Minor items follow, each with an explicit calculation or counterexample and a bounded correction. Severity reflects actual mathematical impact, with no quota. The whole 52-page source, including Appendices A and B and the reference list, was read. One external primary reference was additionally checked in the relevant cone, normalization, gluing, and truncation sections. Absence of author code is not treated as a mathematical defect; no author code was run.

# Major issues

## P1. Proposition 3.17 is false without control of intermediate expected costs

Location. Proposition 3.17, p. 25, and its proof, p. 26, especially the final integration of (3.11).

Claim and hypotheses. Here \(X,Y\) are Polish, \(c\) is lower semicontinuous and bounded below, and \((X\times Y,c)\) is NNCC. The proposition asserts NNCC for all random elements on any fixed probability space, with cost \(\widehat T_c(T,S)=\mathbb Ec(T,S)\). Unlike Theorem 3.7, it does not assume Assumption 3.5. The definition of a variational segment requires finite cost to the base at every intermediate time.

Counterexample. Set \[X=Y=\mathbb R,\qquad a(x)=4^x\sin^2(\pi x),\qquad
 c(x,y)=a(x)+(x-y)^2.\] This cost is finite, smooth, nonnegative, and NNCC. Indeed, \(a(x)\) cancels from every difference \(c(x,\bar y)-c(x,y)\), which is affine in \(x\); the ordinary affine segment therefore satisfies the NNCC inequality as an equality.

Take \(\Omega=\{1,2,\ldots\}\), all subsets measurable, and \(\Pr\{k\}=2^{-k}\). Define \[T_0(k)=k,\qquad T_1(k)=k+1,\qquad \bar S(k)=k.\] The endpoint expected costs are \(0\) and \(1\), respectively. Suppose that \(T_s\) has finite expected cost to \(\bar S\) and satisfies the required NNCC inequality. For an arbitrary atom \(j\) and arbitrary real \(h\), use the test target \[S^{j,h}(k)=k+h\,{\bf1}_{\{k=j\}}.\] Changing one atom preserves finiteness of all the expected costs used in this comparison. Cancellation gives \[2^{-j}\{2(T_s(j)-j)h-h^2\}
 \le 2^{-j}\{2s h-h^2\}.\] Both signs of \(h\) are allowed, so necessarily \(T_s(j)=j+s\). Every atom has positive mass; thus every possible intermediate random element is forced to have this value at every \(j\).

At \(s=1/2\), however, \[a(k+1/2)=2\,4^k,\qquad
 \mathbb Ec(T_{1/2},\bar S)
 =\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{-k}\left(2\,4^k+\frac14\right)=+\infty.\] There is no admissible midpoint. For an exact independent arithmetic check, the first \(N\) terms are \[2^{N+2}-4+\frac14(1-2^{-N}),\] which diverge. This is not a numerical approximation to a trigonometric zero: the integer and half-integer identities above are exact.

Impact and repair limit. Proposition 3.17, the asserted infinite-product extension, is false as stated. Pointwise cancellation does not justify subtracting two infinite expected costs. This example fails Assumption 3.5 because \(\inf_y c(x,y)=a(x)\) is unbounded; hence it does not refute Theorem 3.7 or its metric specialization. A sufficient replacement must provide measurable intermediate selections and integrability of their cost to the base. An integrable pointwise upper bound supplies the latter directly. Assumption 3.5 can also supply that bound through a measurable finite-cost comparison partner, once the selection issue in P2 is separately resolved. Adding an assumption does not by itself repair the existing selection proof.

## P2. The measurable-selection graph reconstruction is incorrect

Location. Lemma 3.3, p. 22; its Appendix B.4 proof, pp. 47–48, equations (B.13)–(B.15) and the asserted inclusion immediately after (B.15).

Decisive example under the lemma’s assumptions. Let \(X=Y=\mathbb R\), \(c(x,y)=(x-y)^2\), \(\gamma=\delta_{(0,2,0)}\), and \(s=1/2\). This is a continuous nonnegative NNCC cost, and it also satisfies Assumption 3.5. The unique variational midpoint is \(x=1\): expanding the NNCC inequality for arbitrary \(y\) forces \(x=(1-s)0+s2\).

Choose the nondecreasing continuous approximation \(c_n=c\) and, for every \(k\), choose the valid compact sets \[Z_k^1=Z_k^2=Z_k=\{(0,0),(2,0)\}.\] Both endpoint-plan measures give these sets mass one, and convergence is uniform there with error zero. Thus all the stated prerequisites for (B.13) hold. The tuple \((0,2,0,1)\) belongs to \(\operatorname{Graph}(\widetilde\Lambda_{1/2})\cap\widetilde Z_k\). But every \(F_{k,n,m}\) in (B.15) additionally requires \((x,\bar y)\in Z_k\), whereas \((1,0)\notin Z_k\). Consequently the displayed inclusion is false, as is the ensuing claimed graph identity, on the entire \(\gamma\)-mass.

The problem is not fixed by increasing \(n\) or \(m\). The compact sets were controlled using the two endpoint laws, not the law of an as-yet unselected midpoint. Furthermore, the restricted tests with \((x,z)\in Z_k\) do not automatically recover tests at every \(z\in Y\). Intersecting separately large sets for two different measures need not even give a large set for either measure; using a common Lusin set would only fix that additional preliminary defect.

Impact and repair limit. The proof of Lemma 3.3 does not establish the Borel selections it needs. Lemma 3.4 and the stated general Polish-space lifting theorem depend on it. This is a Major proof dependency, even though the quadratic example itself has the obvious measurable affine selection and is not a counterexample to the lemma’s conclusion. A replacement must establish the actual selection relation under the stated lower semicontinuity assumptions and respect the quantifiers in Definition 3.2: a common full-measure set for the complete selected path, not merely unrelated exceptional sets for individual times. No impossibility theorem for such a replacement is asserted here. Continuous explicit selections in standard examples remain available independently.

## P3. The base space in Example 3.21 does not satisfy global LMP

Location. Section 3.3, p. 28, first paragraph and Example 3.21; Figure 1, p. 29; the continuity requirement in (2.35), p. 18.

The claimed base cost is \(c(x,y)=-\log|x-y|\) off the diagonal in \(\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb R^2\). The text claims that the corresponding \(c\)-segments exist for every finite-cost endpoint pair, and that this space satisfies LMP. Both global assertions fail.

Use even the horizontal points already present in the example: \[x_0=(-1,0),\quad x_1=(1,0),\quad \bar y=(-1/2,0).\] All endpoint costs are finite. At a putative continuous LMP path \(x(t)\), first-order variations of the comparison target near \(\bar y\), with both signs in every direction, force \[\nabla_y c(x(t),\bar y)\in
 [\,\nabla_y c(x_0,\bar y),\nabla_y c(x_1,\bar y)\,]
 =[-2e_1,\tfrac23e_1].\] This follows directly by separating a point outside the segment from the two endpoint vectors in the first-order maximum inequality; differentiability of the path is unnecessary. Since \[\nabla_y c(x,\bar y)=\frac{x-\bar y}{|x-\bar y|^2},\] its value is continuous along any finite-cost continuous path and is never zero. Its scalar coordinate would have to move from \(-2\) to \(2/3\), so the intermediate value theorem gives a contradiction. An affine-gradient \(c\)-segment likewise cannot cross the missing zero vector.

What the figure does establish. For the displayed four specific curved segments, direct rational evaluation at \(s=1/2\) gives the two squared-distance ratios \(17/5\) and \(17/45\). Thus the plotted average is \[\frac14\log\frac{289}{225}>0,\] with zero endpoint values. The plotted failure for those lifts is real. It does not establish failure of preservation from an LMP base, because that prerequisite fails. Restricting to a smooth region of the cost is not the same as verifying global LMP on a specified Cartesian pair of domains. In particular, a restriction retaining the two horizontal endpoints and an open neighborhood of the displayed base point still has the obstruction above.

Impact and repair limit. The advertised non-lifting conclusion in Section 3.3 lacks a valid example under its own global definition. In addition, Lemmas B.9–B.11 are stated with compact domains and a smooth twisted cost, whereas the example is noncompact and singular on the diagonal. A replacement example must verify the base property and the hypotheses needed to infer a lifted path. The claim that some other valid example may prove non-preservation is left open by this audit.

## P4. The WFR application conflates entropy and Euclidean cone costs

Location. Section 4.5, pp. 35–37; (4.19), Example 4.18 and (4.22), Lemma 4.19 and its proof on pp. 48–49; Example 4.21.

For the KL entropy \(F(t)=t\log t-t+1\), direct minimization of (4.19), for \(r,s>0\), gives \[\inf_{z>0}\left[rF(z/r)+sF(z/s)+cz\right]
 =r+s-2\sqrt{rs}\,e^{-c/2}.\] The derivative vanishes at \(z=\sqrt{rs}\,e^{-c/2}\), and the second derivative is \(2/z>0\). Substituting the printed \(c=-\log(\cos^2(\min(d,\pi)))\) yields an absolute value of the cosine, not the signed cosine in (4.22).

For antipodal points on the unit sphere and \(r=s=1\), the printed entropy cost is \(c=0\). Equation (4.19) therefore gives \(0\), while the printed (4.22) gives \(4\). The resulting literal entropy-transport cost between the distinct unit Dirac masses is also zero: take the unit transport plan, for which both entropy terms and the transport term vanish. It cannot be the asserted WFR metric.

For the usual WFR normalization, the entropy cost is \[c_{\rm ET}(x,y)=-2\log\cos d(x,y)\quad(d<\pi/2),
 \qquad c_{\rm ET}=+\infty\quad(d\ge\pi/2).\] Its pointwise perspective cost is \[c_{\rm ET,C}=r+s-2\sqrt{rs}\cos(\min(d,\pi/2)).\] This differs from the Euclidean cone cost with cutoff \(\pi\). The two can induce the same minimized measure distance when both lifts and the cone-tip reservoir are allowed to vary, but they are not the same pointwise cost. The primary reference [LMS], Remark 7.2, Lemma 7.10, Remark 7.12 and Section 7.6, explicitly distinguishes these operations. Its normalization retains a cone-tip atom; its common-lift result is not a theorem for every prescribed lift.

There is a precise obstruction to using the Euclidean cone and the arbitrary prescribed-lift assertion together. Let \(x,y\) be antipodal and \(\mu=\delta_x,\nu=\delta_y\). Fix the target lift \(\widetilde\nu=\delta_{(y,1)}\), in the manuscript’s mass-radius convention. Any probability lift \(\widetilde\mu\) of \(\delta_x\) has positive-radius part at \(x\), satisfies \(\mathbb Er=1\), and is otherwise allowed to charge the tip. Its squared Euclidean-cone transport cost to the fixed target is \[\mathbb E(r+1+2\sqrt r)=2+2\mathbb E\sqrt r>2.\] The inequality is strict because \(\mathbb Er=1\). Yet the correctly defined squared WFR distance is \(2\), realized by annihilation and creation. The infimum \(2\) with this fixed target lift is approached by placing probability \(\varepsilon\) at radius \(1/\varepsilon\) and the rest at the tip, but it is never attained. Thus the cost-submersion attainment condition used in the paper fails for this Euclidean-cone interpretation.

Impact and repair limit. Correcting only the missing absolute value or changing one cutoff does not preserve the argument of Example 4.21. With the entropy perspective cost, the claimed isometry of the full spherical cone to Euclidean space no longer applies. With the Euclidean cone cost, the asserted fixed-lift attainment fails. The WFR sphere deduction therefore needs a new argument, not just a cosmetic edit. This finding does not refute a correctly formulated NNCC theorem for WFR. The unit-trace PSD example has nonnegative cosine and avoids this specific antipodal obstruction, but the hypotheses for its lifting step must still be supplied. Theorem 4.20 also needs a justified application of the probability-space lifting theorem, including its integrability conditions, for whichever cone cost is actually chosen.

# Local Minor issues with explicit corrections

## P5. Differential prerequisites and a one-sided limit

Location. The sentence preceding Definition 2.2 and (2.10), p. 8.

Bi-twist does not imply nondegeneracy. On \(X=Y=\mathbb R\), let \(c(x,y)=x^3y+xy^3\). For every fixed \(x\), \(y\mapsto3x^2y+y^3\) is strictly increasing onto \(\mathbb R\); the analogous statement holds with \(x,y\) exchanged. Thus smoothness, bi-twist, and the convex-image conditions hold, but \(c_{xy}(0,0)=0\). Keep (2.3) as an independent hypothesis. Theorem 2.4 already lists it explicitly, so this does not refute that theorem.

The division by \(t\) in the proof of Lemma 2.5 reverses the inequality for \(t<0\). For an explicit instance of the lemma’s assumptions, take \(c(x,y)=-x^2y^2\), \(x(s)=s\), and \(\bar y=0\). Then (2.9) is \(s^2y^2\le sy^2\), while (2.10) would assert \(s^2t\le st\), false for \(0<s<1,t<0\). The lemma remains valid: take only \(t\downarrow0\) with an arbitrary signed tangent vector, or differentiate the nonnegative comparison gap at its minimum.

## P6. The semi-geostrophic example requires positive gravity

Location. Example 2.11, p. 11.

The claimed parameter range includes negative \(g\). Let \(H=\mathbb R\), \(g=-1\), \(x_0=-4,x_1=4,a_0=a_1=1\), and base \((\bar y,\bar b)=(0,1)\). The finite endpoint costs are both \(7\). Differentiating the NNCC comparison gap in the target variables at its minimum forces \(x_{1/2}=0\) and \(a_{1/2}=-7\), exactly as the displayed segment formula gives. The latter lies outside \(a>0\); no alternative midpoint is possible. This pointwise necessity does not require continuity of the candidate path. With \(g>0\), the displayed \(a(s)\) is positive and the affine-difference verification works. Restricting to that range repairs the example and retains its physical gravity case.

## P7. Two projection remarks need explicit compatibility hypotheses

Location. Remarks 2.21–2.22, p. 14.

For LMP preservation, continuity of the first projection is needed. Take the quadratic cost on the usual real line upstairs, and the same set with the discrete topology as the first space downstairs. Let both projections be the identity as set maps, with the second target retaining its usual topology. All fibers are singletons and the cost-submersion equalities and attainments hold. Upstairs affine paths satisfy LMP; downstairs no continuous path joins \(0\) to \(1\). Requiring the first projection to be continuous repairs this implication.

For the metric conclusion, require the two quotient maps to be the same map. Otherwise take \(P_1(x)=x\), \(P_2(y)=y+1\) and \(c(x,y)=(x-y)^2\). This is a cost submersion with singleton fibers, but the projected square root is \(|u-v+1|\), which equals \(1\) on the diagonal and vanishes at distinct points. With a common quotient map, the usual lift argument does give separation, symmetry and the triangle inequality. Neither example refutes the purely algebraic NNCC part of Proposition 2.20.

## P8. Compact closure does not guarantee an attained interior support

Location. The paragraph following Theorem 2.35 and (2.40), p. 20.

Let \(X=Y=(0,1)\), \(c(x,y)=-xy\), and \(\psi(x)=-x\). All the smoothness, twist, nondegeneracy and convex-image conditions listed there hold, and \(c\) extends continuously to the compact closure. Nevertheless, \[\phi(y)=\psi^c(y)=\inf_{0<x<1}x(1-y)=0,\qquad
 \phi^c(x)=-x.\] For every interior \(x,y\), \(c(x,y)-\phi^c(x)=x(1-y)>0=\phi(y)\). Thus the finite \(c\)-concave function \(\phi\) has empty \(c\)-subdifferential at every \(y\). The infimum is attained only at an excluded boundary point. Actual compact domains with the appropriate properness assumptions, or explicit nonempty-support assumptions, repair the sentence. Proposition 2.36 already uses the appropriate support class and is not refuted by this example.

## P9. Matrix metric normalization, logarithm range, and completeness

Location. The discussion before Example 3.15, p. 25; (4.16) and Remark 4.11, p. 34.

The printed Frobenius norm is \(\operatorname{tr}(S^\top S)\); its square root is missing on both pages. Literally, in the scalar case the log-distance from \(1\) to \(e^2\) is \(4\), while the two distances through \(e\) sum to \(2\). The triangle inequality fails. Use \(\|S\|_F=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(S^\top S)}\).

The logarithm maps positive definite matrices onto all symmetric matrices, not onto positive definite matrices: \(\log I=0\), and every symmetric \(A\) is the logarithm of \(\exp A\). This corrected Hilbert-space identification proves the intended log-Euclidean NNCC example. The affine-invariant and log-Euclidean metrics should also be distinguished. At \(S=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)\), for an off-diagonal symmetric perturbation \(H\), the differential of \(\log\) multiplies the off-diagonal entries by \((\log4)/3\). Congruence by \(S^{-1/2}\) sends the base to \(I\) and multiplies those entries by \(1/2\). These differ: \(e^3>16\), already from the first six terms of its positive series, so \(\log4<3/2\). Therefore the log-Euclidean infinitesimal norm is not invariant under this congruence.

Finally, nondegenerate Gaussian measures are geodesically convex but not geodesically complete. In one dimension the unit-speed covariance path \(S(t)=(1-t)^2\), \(0\le t<1\), has Bures distance \(|t-u|\) and reaches zero variance in finite time. Adjoining degenerate covariances gives the completion. These corrections do not invalidate the corrected Hilbert quotient proof of Theorem 4.10.

## P10. Singular polar factors cannot be obtained from a right inverse

Location. Proposition 4.12 and its proof, pp. 34–35.

The matrices are allowed to be positive semidefinite, but the displayed formula asks for a right inverse of a possibly singular square matrix. Take \(S_2=0\), \(S_0=S_1=I\). No right inverse of zero exists. Replacing it silently by a Moore–Penrose inverse gives \(U_i=0\), which is not orthogonal, and gives \(M_i=0\) instead of the required \(M_iM_i^\top=I\).

The precise repair is to choose an orthogonal polar factor \(U_i\) satisfying \[S_2^{1/2}S_i^{1/2}
 =U_i\bigl(S_i^{1/2}S_2S_i^{1/2}\bigr)^{1/2}.\] Use an orthogonal completion on the null spaces in the singular case. Then \(M_i=S_i^{1/2}U_i^\top\) has the required covariance and is an optimal aligned factor; the quotient proof supplies the segment. The inverse formula is valid when the product is invertible. This local defect does not refute existence in Theorem 4.10.

## P11. The Fisher–Rao formula omits the square of Hellinger distance

Location. Equation (4.5), p. 31, compared with (4.4), p. 30.

Under (4.4), unit square-root densities satisfy \(\langle\sqrt\mu,\sqrt\nu\rangle=1-\mathcal H^2(\mu,\nu)/2\). The induced spherical distance is therefore \(\arccos(1-\mathcal H^2/2)\), with the normalization used in the paper. For disjoint unit Dirac measures, \(\mathcal H=\sqrt2\) and the angle is \(\pi/2\); the printed expression instead gives \(\arccos(1-\sqrt2/2)<\pi/2\). Insert the missing square. The subsequent spherical proof applies to the corrected distance.

## P12. The partial-transport cone formula has the wrong minimum

Location. The paragraph following Example 4.18, p. 36.

For \(F(t)=|t-1|\), \(c=d^q\ge0\), and positive \(r,s\), the definition (4.19) reduces to \[\inf_{z\ge0}\{|z-r|+|z-s|+d^qz\}
 =r+s-\min(r,s)\max(0,2-d^q).\] Splitting at \(0,\min(r,s),\max(r,s)\) proves the formula directly. With \(q=1,r=s=1,d=0\), the true value is \(0\), but the printed expression with \(\min(0,2-d^q)\) is \(2\). Replace that minimum by a maximum. The resulting expression is \(c_C\) as defined in (4.19), without an additional outer \(q\)-th power unless a new root-distance notation is explicitly introduced. This is a local example correction.

## P13. The translation bound in the Procrustes proof needs the means

Location. The last sentence of the proof of Theorem 4.28, p. 39.

Take \(\mu=\nu=\delta_a\) on \(\mathbb R\), \(a\ne0\). The isometry \(x\mapsto-x+2a\) is optimal with cost zero, but its translation is \(2a\), not bounded by \(W_2(\mu,\nu)=0\). For a general isometry \(x\mapsto Qx+b\) in the sublevel set of cost at most \(R=W_2(\mu,\nu)\), Jensen’s inequality instead gives \[|b|\le R+|\operatorname{mean}\mu|+|\operatorname{mean}\nu|.\] Together with compactness of \(O(n)\), this supplies the required compact parameter set and repairs the existence argument. The theorem is not refuted.
