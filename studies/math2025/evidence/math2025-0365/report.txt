Abstract: This review examines the 41-page preprint arXiv:2310.20674v3. The printed velocity-reconstruction matrix can be singular at a frequency in the upper half-plane and does not follow from the stated Euler equations. A separate defect occurs in the Sobolev semigroup bootstrap: its stated Hölder exponent does not exist, and a subsequent estimate has the wrong target integrability. These are Major issues in the written derivation, with identifiable routes to repair. Exact elimination confirms the underlying radial Rayleigh equation and its Liouville transformation. Further local corrections concern a nonuniform potential bound, the sign of the eigenvalue correction, the complex Hermite scaling, and a strict hypothesis that excludes the advertised constant-pitch example. No counterexample to the two principal instability conclusions is established. Independent scientific adjudication remains pending.

The review follows both the inner–outer construction and the nonlinear argument, retaining their different hypotheses.

In the linear part, the mode convention is \(e^{i(\alpha z-n\theta)}\), \(\lambda=-i\omega\), and \[\beta=\alpha/n,\qquad \gamma=\alpha W-nV/r-\omega=n\Lambda-\omega.\] Assumption A requires smooth even extensions of \(\Omega=V/r\) and \(W\), decay of their derivatives, positive bounded pitch, and a distinguished \(r_0,\beta>0\) with \(b'(r_0)=\Lambda'(r_0)=0\), \(b_0>0\), \(\Lambda''(r_0)>0\), and no other finite point at the same value of \(\Lambda\). Theorem 1.1 constructs localized radial solutions near this point; Section 3.2 must then recover a velocity eigenfunction.

The nonlinear theorem separately assumes a smooth bounded background, decay \(|\bar\omega|+r|\nabla\bar\omega|\lesssim r^{-b}\), \(b>0\), and an unstable vorticity eigenfunction in \(L^Q_\sigma\), \(1<Q<2\). It seeks arbitrarily small \(L^Q\cap H^N\) initial vorticity perturbations whose velocities later separate in \(L^\infty\). Its proof uses modewise spectral theory, the \(H^k\) semigroup estimate of Lemma 3.5, finitely many Picard iterates, a high-order remainder estimate, and a backward-time limiting solution. Neither a uniform estimate over all Fourier modes nor instability in the energy norm is among the proved statements under review.

# Substantive findings

Problem 1 (The stated velocity recovery can fail at an unstable frequency).  Equation (1.13), page 4, and its following determinant assertion are false as printed. The defect affects the recovery used in Section 3.2 and Corollary 3.2, pages 26–27.

Equation reference: eq:recovery, eq:det. Direct derivation. The angular and axial momentum equations in (1.7), after the prescribed Fourier substitution, are \[i\gamma u_\theta+\frac{(rV)'}r u_r-\frac{in}r p=0,\qquad
 i\gamma u_z+W'u_r+i\alpha p=0.\] Multiply the first by \(\alpha r\), the second by \(n\), add, and combine with incompressibility. The resulting matrix is \[\begin{pmatrix}
 -in\gamma&-i\alpha r\gamma\\ i\alpha&-in/r
 \end{pmatrix}
 \binom{u_z}{u_\theta}
 =
 \binom{(\alpha(rV)'+nW')u_r}{-u_r'-u_r/r}.
 \] Thus its upper-right entry is \(i\alpha r\omega+in\alpha V-i\alpha^2rW\). The visible source instead has \(i\alpha r\omega+in\alpha V+\alpha^3rW\). The determinant of [eq:recovery] (#eq:recovery) is \[-\left(n^2/r+\alpha^2r\right)\gamma,
 \] which is nonzero for real \(V,W\), \(r>0\), \(n,\alpha>0\), and \(\operatorname{Im}\omega>0\). The printed matrix has determinant \[(n^2/r+\alpha^2r)(\omega+nV/r)-n^2\alpha W/r-i\alpha^4rW.\] Its additional imaginary term invalidates the asserted nonvanishing argument.

Complete counterexample to the recovery assertion. Use the smooth Batchelor profiles with \(q=1/2\), and put \[E=e^{-1},\quad r=n=\alpha=1,\quad
 \omega=-\tfrac12+E+\tfrac i2 E,\quad
 u_r(1)=1,\quad u_r'(1)=-1.\] Here \(V(1)=(1-E)/2\), \(W(1)=E\), and \(\operatorname{Im}\omega=E/2>0\). The right side is \((-E,0)^{\mathsf T}\). The printed matrix is \[\begin{pmatrix}
 i(-1+i)E/2&i(1-i)E/2\\ i&-i
 \end{pmatrix}.\] Its rank is one, whereas its augmentation by the stated right side has rank two. Indeed, the second row requires \(u_z=u_\theta\), making the first left side zero, although its required value is \(-E\). The corrected matrix has determinant \((-1+i)E\) and gives \(u_z=u_\theta=(1-i)/2\).

These are admissible local Cauchy data for the radial equation: its coefficients are analytic near \(r=1\), its leading coefficient is nonzero, and \(\gamma\) cannot vanish because \(\operatorname{Im}\omega>0\). Local ODE existence supplies the corresponding radial solution. This tests the general reconstruction identity preceding Assumption A. It is not presented as a global \(H^1_0\) eigenmode or as a counterexample satisfying the separate, literally strict pitch condition in Assumption A.

Impact and repair. This is a Major defect in the stated passage from a radial solution to an Euler mode, not a merely cosmetic change to a determinant. Its repair is explicit: use [eq:recovery] (#eq:recovery) and [eq:det] (#eq:det). Independent symbolic elimination of pressure then gives exactly (1.9); the residual after comparison is zero. The Liouville substitution (1.17) also gives precisely the potential (1.19)–(1.20). Consequently this calculation does not disprove the radial construction or the possibility of the claimed velocity instability. The endpoint regularity argument must be attached to the corrected recovery formula.

Problem 2 (A missing estimate in the nonlinear semigroup bootstrap).  The derivation of Lemma 3.5 on page 31 does not justify its \(L^Q\cap H^k\) growth estimate as written.

The displayed estimate (3.35) invokes \[\|\bar\omega\cdot\nabla u\|_2
 \lesssim \|\bar\omega\|_p\|\nabla u\|_Q,\qquad
 \frac12=\frac1p+\frac1Q.\] For every allowed \(1<Q<2\), it requires \(1/p<0\). For example, the fully admissible value \(Q=3/2\) requires \(1/p=-1/6\). There is no Hölder exponent \(p\in[1,\infty]\) satisfying this relation.

For \(Q<6/5\), (3.37) estimates another forcing term in \(L^{6/5}\), whereas (3.39) requires an \(L^2\) forcing bound. The transport matrix semigroup in (3.33) is a multiplication operator and supplies no spatial smoothing. Even on one fixed Fourier mode, \(L^{6/5}\) control cannot alone imply \(L^2\) control: for a nonzero smooth function \(\chi\) supported in \((-1/4,1/4)\), \[f_\epsilon(r,\theta,z)
 =\epsilon^{-5/6}\chi((r-1)/\epsilon)e^{2i\theta}e_z\] is smooth, divergence-free, independent of \(z\), and supported away from the axis. Its \(L^{6/5}\) norm is bounded above and below independently of \(\epsilon\), while \(\|f_\epsilon\|_2\asymp\epsilon^{-1/3}\). This demonstrates the missing implication between the forcing norms. It does not assert that every such \(f_\epsilon\) is the particular nonlinear or Biot–Savart forcing of the manuscript.

Repair route and its limits. One must estimate the actual modewise operator \(B_{n,\alpha}\), using the derivative gain of velocity and the mode identities, rather than use the impossible exponent. For \(\alpha\ne0\), the two-dimensional resolvent \((-\Delta_{x'}+\alpha^2)^{-1}\) on this fixed axial mode gives an order-minus-one velocity operator; its kernel is locally of size \(|x'|^{-1}\) and decays at infinity. Young’s inequality with \(1/s=3/2-1/Q\), \(1<s<2\), gives \(\|u\|_2\lesssim_{\alpha,Q}\|\omega\|_Q\). The mode identity for \(\bar\omega\cdot\nabla u\) has bounded coefficients multiplying \(u\), and the other term is treated similarly.

For \(\alpha=0\), Lemma 3.1 supplies \(u\in L^{Q_2^*}\), \(Q_2^*=2Q/(2-Q)>2\). The coefficients in the modewise forcing are bounded near the axis and decay like \(r^{-b-1}\) at infinity. They belong to \(L^{Q/(Q-1)}(\mathbb R^2)\), giving the required \(L^2\) estimate by \[\frac12=\frac1{Q_2^*}+\frac{Q-1}{Q}.\] These observations identify a valid replacement for the base forcing estimate. The corresponding higher-derivative argument, with Cartesian components and coefficient bounds retained, should be written out; it is not supplied merely by changing the sign of the exponent in (3.35).

There is also a loss of the arbitrarily small spectral slack between (3.31) and (3.49), page 32. Lemma 3.5 provides \(e^{(S+\delta)t}\), not automatically \(e^{St}\). Choosing \(0<\delta<2a-S\) preserves the convergence of the backward integral. The estimates on Picard iterates used subsequently need only hold for \(t\le0\), not all \(t\in\mathbb R\) as printed.

The missing Sobolev bootstrap is Major because it is an essential input to the high-order nonlinear construction. This is a supported proof issue, not a claimed counterexample to nonlinear instability. The stated repair route is not an author-adopted correction or a completed replacement proof of all of Lemma 3.5.

Problem 3 (The first real-part bound is not uniform on its stated range).  The assertion \(\operatorname{Re}k=O(n^{3/2})\) in (2.58), page 16, is false on the full interval \(Cn^{-3/4}\le |r-r_0|\le C^{-1}n^{-1/2}\).

Equation reference: eq:scale. Fix any background satisfying the local hypotheses, choose \(\omega=\omega_{\rm app}\), and set \(r_n=r_0+c n^{-1/2}\), where \(0<c<C^{-1}\) is fixed. These points lie in that interval for all sufficiently large \(n\), and \(|\omega-\omega_{\rm app}|=0\) satisfies the frequency hypothesis for every \(B\ge1\). Write \(s=\sqrt{b_0}>0\) and \(t=\Lambda''(r_0)c^2/2>0\). The stated definitions and Taylor hypotheses give \[\frac{k(r_n)}{n^2}\longrightarrow
 p_0\left(1+\frac{s^2}{(t-is)^2}\right),\] and exact algebra yields \[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Re}k(r_n)}{n^2}
 =p_0\frac{t^2(t^2+3s^2)}{(t^2+s^2)^2}>0.
 \] This is incompatible with the claimed uniform \(O(n^{3/2})\) bound. Making \(C\) large does not remove the contradiction for fixed \(C\).

The coercivity argument only needs a lower bound on the negative part. Near this scale, the leading real expression in [eq:scale] (#eq:scale) is nonnegative; perturbing \(b\), \(p\), and the imaginary part of \(\omega\) gives the weaker bound \(\operatorname{Re}k\ge -C_1n^{3/2}\), which can be combined with the positive imaginary part. The incorrect two-sided size estimate should be replaced by that lower bound, and Figure 2 should be interpreted accordingly. No loss of outer coercivity is established by the positive \(n^2\) term. This issue is assessed as Minor, with the scope of the replacement stated explicitly.

Problem 4 (The eigenvalue-correction term has the wrong sign).  Equations (2.7), (2.76), (2.87), and (A.3), on pages 10, 20, 21, and 36, put a positive \(i\) in the leading \(\widehat\omega\) term of the potential error. The definitions give a negative \(i\).

At \(r_0\), \(\gamma=-is-\mu_m-\widehat\omega\), \(s=\sqrt{b_0}\). Direct differentiation gives \[\left.\frac{d}{dh}\left(1+\frac{s^2}{(-is-h)^2}\right)\right|_{h=0}
 =-\frac{2i}{s}.\] Consequently, on the prescribed scale, \[k_{\rm err}(r_0)=-\frac{2ip_0n^2}{s}\widehat\omega+O(n).\] For \(\widehat\omega=d\,n^{-1/2}\) with fixed sufficiently small nonzero \(d\), the difference from the printed sign is of order \(n^{3/2}\), which cannot be hidden in the stated \(O(n)\) remainder at \(r=r_0\). The displayed calculation (A.4) itself has the negative sign.

Both leading terms \(f\) and \(g\) in the reduced equation must receive the corrected sign. Their absolute-value estimates and the nonvanishing leading coefficient in Rouché’s theorem are unchanged. The magnitude bounds and qualitative zero count can therefore survive this correction; this local issue is assessed as Minor. The manuscript’s complex-valued Taylor remainder on page 38 should also be written in integral form, rather than requiring a single real intermediate point for both real and imaginary parts.

Problem 5 (The complex Hermite argument is misstated).  Lemma 2.5, page 14, gives \(G_m(e^{i\zeta}\,\cdot)\) where the eigenspace requires \(G_m(e^{i\zeta/2}\,\cdot)\).

The admissible choice \(m=1\), \(\zeta=\pi/4\) makes the printed function \(G_1(e^{i\pi/4}x)=e^{-ix^2/2}\), whose absolute value is one. It is not in \(L^2(\mathbb R)\), and substitution also fails the stated eigenvalue equation. The corrected function is \(e^{-e^{i\pi/4}x^2/2}\); direct differentiation verifies its eigenvalue \(-e^{i\pi/4}\) for \(d^2/dx^2-ix^2\). The correct half-angle already occurs in (2.34), (2.51), and Corollary 2.7, and is compatible with the standard parabolic-cylinder normalization [dlmf]. Correct the repeated argument in (2.52) and Remark 2.8 as well. Since the actual Weber ansatz and the adjoint range calculation use the half-angle, this is a Minor inconsistency, not evidence against the intended oscillator spectrum.

Problem 6 (The written pitch hypothesis excludes the stated example).  Assumption A, page 4, contains \(0<\inf q<\sup q<+\infty\), but the paragraph immediately following it identifies a constant-pitch Batchelor vortex as an example satisfying the assumption.

For its displayed profiles, \[-\frac{(rV)'}{W'}=q\qquad(r>0).\] Hence \(\inf q=\sup q\), and the middle strict inequality fails. This is an explicit mismatch of hypotheses and application, not an inadmissible counterexample to Theorem 1.1. The appropriate condition for the stated example is \(0<\inf q\le\sup q<+\infty\). The proof uses positive upper and lower bounds, not strict variation of the pitch. The Appendix A.1 parameter argument concerns the other conditions (1.14) and does not change this strict inequality. This local hypothesis correction is assessed as Minor.

# Coverage, surviving checks, and limitations

The inner construction was followed through its scaled Weber operator, closed range, adjoint projection, Gaussian estimate, overlapping cutoffs, and the projected-system contraction. The scalar solvability equation was checked against its nonzero bilinear integral and the Rouché radius. The two cases of reverse gluing were read separately; the corrected half-angle and frequency-error sign are needed consistently. This review does not claim a new proof of every cited theorem of Kato or of the full parabolic-cylinder asymptotic theory.

The outer estimates were checked by separating the \(n^{-3/4}\), \(n^{-1/2}\), and fixed-radius regimes. Besides Problem 3, the first estimate in the proof on page 17, \(|k-pn^2|\lesssim n^{-2}\), is not the order obtained by inserting \(\gamma=O(n)\): the lower-order terms generically contribute \(O(1)\). The needed relative control is weaker. A global lower bound for \(|\Lambda-\Lambda(r_0)|\) outside a finite neighborhood also needs care if \(\Lambda(r_0)=0=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Lambda(r)\); compact separation alone is insufficient. Decay of \(a,b\) provides a separate far-field route. These qualifications are retained rather than treated as counterexamples to coercivity.

For the nonlinear part, the zero and nonzero axial Biot–Savart components, modewise compact perturbation argument, smoothness bootstrap, finite-mode Picard construction, remainder energy estimate, and compactness passage were all read. The derivative-loss strategy and the choice \(2a>S\) are coherent once the required semigroup estimate and spectral slack are supplied. The argument estimates only finitely many modes at each fixed iteration; a uniform bound over all modes was not assumed. The exclusion of \((\pm1,0)\) in the selection on page 32 requires justification if used in full generality. No new unstable mode restricted to those indices was constructed here, so this point is retained as a limitation, not a confirmed counterexample.

The direct calculation also reproduces the parameter values in Figure 1 for \(q=1/4\): \[r_0=0.825200682117\ldots,\qquad
 \beta=0.158943819853\ldots,\qquad
 \sqrt{b_0}=0.193664783541\ldots.\] The sufficient threshold is \(1.251296996204\ldots\). These floating-point checks are diagnostics of the figure and appendix; the substantive contradictions above use exact algebra or asymptotic limits, not numerical guesses. The threshold is not a proof of stability outside the stated interval, and physical vortex breakdown is not a conclusion of the theorem.
