Abstract: The proposed product formula for generic complex realization components is natural and its reduction to subgraph fiber squares is useful. The printed proof nevertheless contains a false passage from ordinary generic integrality to irreducibility of general complex fibers. The invariant application also uses an invalid identification of arithmetic and geometric genus for smooth affine curves. Exact examples establish both problems. Further corrections are needed in auxiliary orientation and orbit lemmas, the induction boundary cases and the definition of a calligraph. These findings do not supply a counterexample to the main generic component-count formula. A separate static defect is recorded for a nondefault branch of the acquired, later PyRigi snapshot.

# Structure, scope and checks that support the approach

The variables are complex plane realizations, and edge labels are squared distances. The transformation group \(T=\mathbb C^2\rtimes\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb C)\) is irreducible of dimension three and excludes reflections. This distinction is necessary: two generic mirror images of a rigid triangle belong to different directed-isometry orbits.

For sparse graphs the edge map is dominant by the rigidity characterization, and its generic complex fiber has dimension \(2|V|-|E|\). Maximal tight blocks are edge-disjoint; in a sparse graph their common-edge union argument gives uniqueness of the decomposition, with isolated singleton vertices treated separately. The main assertion is \[c(G,\lambda)=\prod_i c(G_i)\] for a general edge assignment. It does not concern every special assignment, nor the number of real connected components.

The diagram relating the edge map, the subgraph map \(S_G\), and the product of block edge maps commutes because directed isometries preserve squared distances. The submersion calculation in Lemma 8 has the right basic mechanism: the infinitesimal motions of each tight block fill the kernel of its edge differential. Once dominance and the correct orbit hypotheses are supplied, the component-count factorization in Lemma 6 reduces the main question to the general fiber of \(S_G\). The induction then aims to prove irreducibility of its fiber square, controlling the loci where comparison-map fibers increase in dimension.

Several elementary tests are consistent with this strategy. A tree with nonzero general edge labels is built from a free root and irreducible complex distance conics, so its realization space is irreducible. A triangle has two directed-isometry classes, and two otherwise independent triangles sharing a vertex have four choices. A triangle with a leaf has maximal tight blocks consisting of the triangle and the leaf edge. These checks do not prove the general product formula, but they confirm its conventions and small cases.

For the curve application, pinning an edge of nonzero squared length supplies an explicit orbit section. Under the intended one-degree-of-freedom calligraph hypothesis and the cited thinness result \(m(G)=1\), projection to the distinguished vertex is generically one-to-one. Conditional on the quoted class values, the arithmetic in Examples 24–25 is correct: \[2(272)/16=34,\quad 17(15)+1=256,\qquad
 2(11)=22,\quad 11(9)-3(2)-3(2)+1=88.\] The class values themselves were not recomputed. The discussion of real connected components correctly separates a semialgebraic question from the generic complex count. The proposed higher-dimensional extension is explicitly left open; its illustrative three-dimensional count was not independently certified by a full elimination calculation.

# Major proof issues

Problem 1 (Major: ordinary generic integrality does not imply irreducible general complex fibers).  Lemma 27(b), p. 2158, states that a general fiber of a dominant morphism between irreducible varieties is irreducible if and only if \[R=\mathbb C[X]\otimes_{\mathbb C[Y]}\mathbb C(Y)\] is a domain. This is false, even for an everywhere smooth map. Take \[f:\mathbb C^\times\longrightarrow\mathbb C^\times,\qquad x\longmapsto x^2.\] Its generic ring is \(\mathbb C(t)[x]/(x^2-t)\), a field: a rational square has even valuation at \(t=0\), whereas \(t\) has valuation one. Nevertheless every nonzero complex fiber consists of two distinct points. Thus ordinary generic integrality counts one component over \(\mathbb C(t)\), while the geometric generic fiber and general complex fibers have two.

Lemma 32, p. 2160, in fact proves that ordinary generic integrality is automatic for every dominant morphism between irreducible varieties, by localization. Combining it with Lemma 27(b) would incorrectly make all such general fibers irreducible. Example 34 in the paper itself illustrates the same distinction after adjoining a square root.

This error occurs at the final step of the proof of Lemma 10(b), the bridge needed in Lemma 11 and Proposition 12. The conclusion of Lemma 10 is not disproved by the square-map example, since that example’s fiber square is reducible. The problem is that the supplied proof does not establish its conclusion.

The needed replacement is geometric generic irreducibility, or the corresponding regularity of the function-field extension in characteristic zero, followed by a justified passage to general closed fibers. One way to organize the argument is to descend the finite set of geometric generic components to a finite normal extension of the base field. If there were more than one, the pairs of equal components and the pairs of unequal components would be two nonempty Galois-invariant collections in the generic fiber square. This is the obstruction that irreducibility of the fiber square must exclude. The argument needs the geometric components, not merely that \(R\) is a domain.

Two preceding algebraic steps also need correction. In Lemma 29, localizing \(\mathbb C[X]\otimes_{\mathbb C[Y]}\mathbb C[X]\) at all its nonzero elements produces its fraction field, not in general \(\mathbb C(X)\otimes_{\mathbb C(Y)}\mathbb C(X)\). For \(X=\mathbb A^1,Y=\mathrm{point}\), these are \(\mathbb C(x,y)\) and the localization of \(\mathbb C[x,y]\) by nonzero polynomials in the two separate variables. The element \(x+y\) is not invertible in the latter. Localize only at the multiplicative set generated by nonzero elements from the two factors; the desired domain conclusion then follows.

In Lemma 30 the inner sums in the tensor factorization must start at \(j=0\), not \(j=1\), and the linear-independence argument should include \(1,e,\ldots,e^{d-1}\). For the quadratic \(x^2-t\) used above, the correct factor is \(e\otimes1+1\otimes e\). The printed factor omits its second term, so the asserted zero product is not obtained. Finally, the proof of Lemma 10 should not infer global smoothness merely from smooth source and target; the submersion assumption belongs to part (a), with generic smoothness used where appropriate in part (b).

Problem 2 (Major: the invariant proof confuses smooth affine curves with smooth projective completions).  Proposition 33(b), p. 2161, uses \[g(\rho(C))=p_a(C).\] With the preceding Hilbert-polynomial definition of arithmetic genus, this identity is false for a smooth affine curve: its projective closure can be singular at infinity.

An example satisfying the proposition’s hypotheses is \[X=\{(x,z,y)\in\mathbb C^3:y=x^3\},\qquad f(x,z,y)=z^2,\qquad
 \rho(x,z,y)=(x,z).\] The surface \(X\cong\mathbb A^2\) is smooth and irreducible. For a general nonzero value of \(f\), its two components \(C_\pm\) are smooth affine graphs of \(x^3\), and \(\rho\) is an isomorphism on the whole fiber onto two lines. Each projective closure is the plane cubic \[YW^2-X^3=0,\] singular at \([0:1:0]\). Thus \(p_a(C_\pm)=1\), whereas \(g(\rho(C_\pm))=0\). The two components still have equal respective invariants, so this example refutes the proof identity, not the equality conclusions of Proposition 33.

Part (a) also needs a more precise descent argument. An abstract isomorphism of function fields does not preserve degree or the Hilbert polynomial of a chosen embedding: a line and the affine cubic above both have rational function field. What is relevant is the semilinear Galois action on the embedded ideals, which preserves their graded Hilbert functions after projective closure. Choose a finite normal extension of \(\mathbb C(Y)\) over which the geometric generic components are defined, and use its Galois group over \(\mathbb C(Y)\). The printed extension \(E/\mathbb C(Z)\) is not the specified splitting extension of the base field. The leading coefficient of a Hilbert polynomial is generally \(\deg(C)/(\dim C)!\), a positive rational number, not always an integer.

For geometric genus, use the smooth projective normalizations of the component curves; Galois conjugation and birational projection preserve their genus. Finally justify specialization to general closed fibers after shrinking the base and controlling the relevant projective families. These changes can support the intended equal-degree and equal-genus conclusions, but the present proof does not provide them. The issue matters to Corollary 2 and its geometric-genus assertions, not merely to an unused auxiliary identity.

# Auxiliary and boundary corrections

Problem 3 (Minor: Lemma 15 omits orientation and isotropic exceptions).  In Lemma 15(e), pp. 2144–2146, take the ordered triples \[P=((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)),\qquad
 Q=((0,0),(1,0),(0,-1)).\] They are noncollinear and all corresponding pairwise squared distances agree, so the displayed hypothesis on \(\Lambda\) holds. Writing \(p_0=(x,y)\), \(q_0=(u,v)\), the equations defining \(\Gamma\) reduce to \(u=x,v=-y\), giving a two-dimensional reflection graph.

The unique directed isometry fixing the first two corresponding vertices is the identity. Hence the additional condition defining \(\Gamma'\) requires \(u=x,v=y\), and the third distance equation forces \(y=0\). Consequently \[\dim\Gamma=2,\qquad \dim\Gamma'=1,\] contrary to the stated equality. The proof’s assertion that congruent noncollinear triples admit a directed isometry ignores the reflected orientation.

There is a separate complex exception in part (c). Let \(p_1=q_1=0\), \(p_2=(1,i)\), \(q_2=(1,-i)\). The endpoints are distinct and both squared lengths are zero, but no matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) sends \(p_2\) to \(q_2\): its two linear equations are already inconsistent. Thus \(\Gamma'\) is empty. These two-point configurations have full edge-Jacobian rank and are not excluded by the definition of \(U_V\).

Distinguish direct and reflected congruence and treat isotropic pairs explicitly. Alternatively, shrink the generic open sets to exclude the needed null distances, while proving that this restriction preserves the desired generic component counts and supplying the omitted exceptional-stratum bounds. In Lemmas 17–18, equality of distances alone does not justify every claimed reconstruction of a transformation or the asserted surjectivity onto the exceptional locus.

These examples do not refute the main product formula. For instance, the reflected three-point case still has \(\dim\Gamma=2\), which is the upper-dimensional possibility used in one induction case, and a common rigid block fixes the orientation in another. The local statements and their precise uses must nevertheless be corrected.

Problem 4 (Minor: the orbit lemmas require stronger hypotheses and a generic assignment).  Lemma 5, pp. 2137–2138, is false for arbitrary topological spaces with free actions. Let \(X=Y=(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)^2\), let \(G=\mathbb R\), and fix irrational \(\alpha\). Define \[f(x,y)=(2x,y),\quad
 t\cdot_Y(x,y)=(x+t,y+\alpha t),\quad
 t\cdot_X(x,y)=(x+t/2,y+\alpha t).\] Both actions are free, \(G\) is connected, and \(f\) is equivariant. The fiber over \((0,0)\) has two points. Its inverse image over the \(G\)-orbit consists of two disjoint dense connected irrational-flow orbits, whose union is connected: either orbit is connected and dense in that union. Thus the two component numbers are two and one. In the proof, connectedness need not imply path-connectedness, and a free orbit map need not have a continuous inverse.

For the actual algebraic application, use locally closed algebraic orbits and an explicit orbit trivialization. Restrict to the relevant generic configurations. The action on the entire target in Lemma 6 is not free at coincident realizations or singleton blocks; isolated vertices can be handled separately, with their connected stabilizers. Dominance of \(S_G\) should be deduced from Lemma 8’s submersion, not solely from dominance of the other maps in a commuting diagram.

Lemma 21, pp. 2151–2152, also needs a general-\(\lambda\) hypothesis. For \(G=K_2\) and \(\lambda=0\), one irreducible component is \[r_2-r_1=a(1,i),\qquad a\in\mathbb C.\] It contains both the collapsed realization \(r_1=r_2=0\) and the distinct realization \(s_1=0,s_2=(1,i)\). They cannot be related by any directed isometry, since a bijection preserves coincidence. For a tight graph the existence of the equal-\(S_G\) data in Lemma 21(ii) would imply exactly such congruence. Thus (i) does not imply (ii) at this assignment.

Even generically, the proof’s claim that \(\pi^{-1}(C)=C\times T\) is a fiber component of \(S_G\) is dimensionally wrong for \(K_2\): it has dimension six, whereas an \(S_G\) fiber has dimension three. Use the equivariant product description instead. Corollary 1 already concerns a general assignment, so its stated scope is not contradicted by the special zero-length example.

Problem 5 (Minor: the induction base and comparison fibers need explicit completion).  For one vertex, Proposition 20 identifies \(P(G)\) with \((U_V\times T)^2\), of dimension ten. The defining equation \(t(r)=u(s)\) remains present. The correct parametrization is \[(r,t,u)\longmapsto(r,t;u^{-1}t(r),u),\qquad
 P(G)\cong\mathbb C^2\times T^2,\] of dimension eight, consistent with Lemma 16. The desired irreducibility follows from this corrected parametrization.

The reduction of \(K_2\) deletes its sole one-edge block, leaving \(I^m=\varnothing\) and \(V^m=\varnothing\). Thus the induction needs the additional base \(P(\varnothing)=\mathrm{point}\); it does not always terminate at one vertex. The notation \(\widetilde r_1\) in the universal setup of Lemma 18 is undefined for this example, so the leaf and disappearing-neighbor cases must be treated separately.

More generally, the comparison proof should establish nonempty general fibers or dominance before applying its lower dimension bound. An injective morphism with dense image in an irreducible variety does not alone make its source irreducible. For example, the disjoint union of \(\mathbb A^1\setminus\{0\}\) and a point maps bijectively to \(\mathbb A^1\). In the intended parametrization, prove that the image is an open subset and that reconstruction gives a regular inverse on the stated open set; then irreducibility follows. Supply this argument for the omitted cases and the corrected exceptional configurations.

Finally, the tangent-space calculation in Lemma 8 should treat a singleton separately: its edge kernel has dimension two, not \(2|V_i|-(2|V_i|-3)=3\). In the last line, the displayed choice \(dg(0,\beta)=dg(\alpha,0)-\gamma\) yields \(\gamma=dg(\alpha,-\beta)\). These are repairable details, but they should not be hidden inside the induction.

Problem 6 (Minor: the calligraph definition does not enforce a curve).  The definition on p. 2152 says that adjoining either \(\{1,0\}\) or \(\{2,0\}\) produces a tight graph. As written it includes an already tight triangle on \(0,1,2\): adjoining an existing edge changes nothing. The triangle also passes the stated thinness conditions on p. 2154, since the edge-deletion condition is imposed only for \(|V|>3\), and deletion of two vertices leaves one connected vertex. Its general pinned realization set has two points, not one-dimensional curves.

Require a genuine one-edge deficiency, for example that \(G\) is not tight, \(|E|=2|V|-4\), and at least one genuinely new indicated edge produces a tight graph. The intended examples \(L,R,C_v\) satisfy this requirement. Also state that projected coupler curves mean Zariski closures unless closedness of the projection image is proved; elimination gives the closure in general. In the higher-dimensional discussion on p. 2156, finite general fiber must likewise mean finite modulo Euclidean motions, since translation already prevents the unquotiented fiber from being finite.

Problem 7 (Minor: a nondefault branch of the related library skips size \(n-1\) components).  In the acquired PyRigi `generic.py`, the subgraph enumeration in `rigid_components` uses `range(2, graph.number_of_nodes()-1)`. Python excludes the upper endpoint, so subsets of size \(n-1\) are never tested.

For a triangle on \(0,1,2\) and a leaf edge \(\{2,3\}\), the graph is not rigid, and its maximal rigid blocks are \(\{0,1,2\}\) and \(\{2,3\}\). At \(n=4\), the enumeration tests only pairs. Even with an exact rigidity oracle, this branch therefore misses the triangle and retains its individual edges. A separate exhaustive combinatorial check confirms the correct blocks.

Include subset size \(n-1\). This concerns the `subgraphs-pebble`, `randomized` and `numerical` branches. The default two-dimensional `pebble` branch uses a different closure-based procedure and is not implicated by this check. This is static analysis of the dated snapshot, not an author-code execution or evidence that the paper’s examples used the affected branch.

# Assessment

Major revision is required to replace the ordinary-versus-geometric generic-fiber argument and the affine-genus justification. The orientation, orbit and induction corrections should be propagated through the comparison-map proof with precise open-set and exceptional-fiber hypotheses. The main product formula and the equal-invariant conclusions remain plausible and are not disproved by the explicit examples in this report; the established objections identify false auxiliary assertions and gaps in their printed derivations.

The checks are targeted algebraic and combinatorial validations, not a complete formal proof audit of all external inputs. In particular, no numerical realization-count or calligraph-class package was run. Scientific adjudication remains pending.
