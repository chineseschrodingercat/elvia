# Definitions, assumptions, quantifiers, and dependencies

Rings are associative and unital. In the tree-action statements the coefficient ring \(k\) is commutative and nonzero: the conclusion from vanishing \(N_{\rm ab}\otimes k\) for a free group \(N\) uses \(k\ne0\). The augmentation ideal \(I_G\) has left- and right-module versions; induction, the inverse-involution operation, and tensor products with diagonal action must retain their stated sides. The involution is not a vector-space dual.

The embedding of a group algebra into a Hughes-free division ring is an explicit hypothesis in statements over an arbitrary field. Its existence for the locally indicable groups used in characteristic zero is supplied by external results cited by the authors. The Linnell linear-independence property is stronger-looking input obtained there from Hughes freeness. This review checked the uses of these properties but did not independently reprove the cited division-ring existence theorem.

Non-positive immersions concern compact connected immersed complexes and allow the trivial-fundamental-group exception. The stronger NTPI inequality has an exception for free products of finite cyclic groups. That exception cannot be discarded when using Proposition 2.10. The asphericity conclusion in Corollary 2.8 also has a trivial-group alternative.

Reducibility involves a reducing edge which remains strictly involved; the absence of proper powers is a statement in the appropriate fundamental group after removing the cell. It is not merely a claim that a displayed word has no visible repetition. Bireducibility supplies two distinct reductions when at least two cells are present. Lemma 2.12 supplies the embeddings needed when passing between reductions and one-cell subcomplexes.

For the torsion variant in Theorem 5.6, the characteristic is coprime to the relevant finite orders. For Theorem 6.9, the ideal used in the quotient to the division ring is the two-sided kernel associated with the normal closure of \(W\). Confusing it with a one-sided augmentation ideal would invalidate the final zero-versus-one dimension calculation.

External foundations used but not independently reproved in full include the cited Freiheitssatz and one-relator torsion results, the domination theorem for tree actions of finitely presented groups, the Magnus–Moldavanskii hierarchy, the cited virtual free-by-cyclic theorem, the division-ring existence theorem, and the relevant immersion and \(L^2\)-Betti-number results. The report does not describe these references as independently retrieved or verified.

# Detailed mathematical verification and residual questions

## Projective modules and the homological criterion

Pages 16–18, Lemma 3.1 through Corollary 3.5, were checked without assuming that the ambient division ring is flat over the group algebra. For Lemma 3.1, let \(P\) be projective and let finitely many elements of \(P\) span \(D\otimes_RP\). Embed \(P\) as a summand of a free module \(L\) and choose a finite free summand \(L_1\) containing these elements. If \(N\) is the module they generate, right exactness gives \(D\otimes_R(P/N)=0\). The image of \(P\) in \(L/L_1\) therefore has zero tensor product with \(D\). Any submodule of the free module \(L/L_1\) maps injectively to its scalar extension as far as its own elements are concerned: its composite map to \(D\otimes_R(L/L_1)\) is injective because \(R\hookrightarrow D\). Consequently that image is zero. Thus \(P\subset L_1\), and projection from \(L_1\) onto the summand \(P\) proves finite generation. This resolves the apparent risk of illicitly using flatness.

For a finitely generated module of projective dimension at most one, its presentation kernel is projective. The long exact Tor sequence, together with the preceding finite-dimensional criterion, gives Proposition 3.2. If the ring has global dimension at most two and the division ring has weak dimension at most one, a finitely generated ideal \(J\) has \(\operatorname{Tor}_1(D,J)=\operatorname{Tor}_2(D,R/J)=0\); the criterion gives finite presentation and hence coherence, as in Corollary 3.3.

The scalar-extension step in Proposition 2.2 does not require arbitrary coefficient modules to be flat over \(\mathbb Z\). A projective resolution of \(\mathbb Z\) over \(\mathbb ZG\) splits as a complex of underlying abelian groups in the required short exact pieces, since the kernels are free abelian. Tensoring those pieces remains exact. The diagonal action on a free induced module is untwisted by \(g\otimes m\mapsto g\otimes g^{-1}m\). This justifies the cohomological-dimension bound used later.

The ascending-HNN argument in Theorem 3.4 was checked through the locally free kernel, the skew Laurent ring, Ore localization, and the passage from finitely presented modules to arbitrary modules by filtered colimits. In Theorem 3.6 rationalization is compatible with the Tor calculation because \(\mathbb Q\) is flat over \(\mathbb Z\). The proof is not an assertion that all group algebras are division-ring subrings in positive characteristic.

## The tree-action step

On pages 20–22 the decisive point is the passage from an \(\mathrm{FP}_2(k)\) group to a finitely presented cover with a \(k\)-acyclic relation-module kernel, followed by the cited domination theorem for trees. Let \(1\to N\to H\to G\to1\) be the cover, let \(H_i\) be the relevant vertex stabilizers, and put \(K=\langle\!\langle N\cap H_i\rangle\!\rangle\). The quotient tree is a tree because \(K\) is generated by elliptic stabilizers. In \(H/K\), the group \(N/K\) intersects each image of \(H_i\) trivially: an element represented in \(H_i\) and trivial in \(G\) lies in \(N\cap H_i\), which was killed. Hence \(N/K\) acts freely on the quotient tree and is free.

The quotient map on abelianizations gives \((N/K)_{\rm ab}\otimes k=0\) from \(N_{\rm ab}\otimes k=0\). A nontrivial free group has a nonzero free abelianization, whose tensor product with a nonzero ring is nonzero. Therefore \(N/K=1\). This establishes the step which could otherwise have concealed an unjustified passage from homological to ordinary coherence. The finite-presentability conclusion then uses finitely presented vertex groups and finitely generated edge groups. It does not require edge groups to be finitely presented.

The one-relator application still relies on the external hierarchy and virtual-subgroup inputs named above. Their invocation was traced; they were not reconstructed from first principles in this review.

## Flatness, Fox coefficients, and finite torsion

Lemma 5.1, pages 22–23, is a leading-degree argument for a diagonal module \(V\otimes_KD\). Its support condition is essential: the group elements in the chosen support have distinct images in the locally indicable quotient. After choosing an epimorphism of the finitely generated image to \(\mathbb Z\), the extreme-degree coefficient has fewer support terms. Clearing Ore denominators on the right commutes with the left action, and a leading-degree cancellation contradicts the inductive injectivity statement. No finite-dimensionality of \(V\) is used.

In the augmentation-ideal induction, the two factor modules are flat after induction and scalar extension. The relation-module map is injective by the Fox-coefficient support argument and Lemma 5.1. The Tor exact sequence then gives flatness; the inverse-involution and the augmentation sequence supply the dimension shift to weak dimension at most one.

For the torsion relation \(w=u^\ell\), let \(N_\ell=1+u+\cdots+u^{\ell-1}\). In the regular group algebra, multiplication by \(N_\ell\) gives an injection from coinvariants by \(u-1\): this is seen coset by coset in the cyclic subgroup of order \(\ell\). For an arbitrary module \(M\), if \(\ell\) is invertible and \(N_\ell m=0\), then \[m=\ell^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(1-u^i)m\in (u-1)M.\] Together with \(N_\ell(u-1)=0\), this proves \(\ker N_\ell=(u-1)M\). The coprimality hypothesis in Theorem 5.6 therefore has exactly the role required by the proof.

The earlier Fox-coefficient injection on the regular group algebra can be justified without an illicit flatness assumption. The equivariant map \[KG\longrightarrow KG\otimes_KD_{\overline G},\qquad
 g\longmapsto g\otimes\overline g\] is injective, and the distinct-support lemma applies on the target. The image is not the non-equivariant map \(v\mapsto v\otimes1\). This supplies the implicit justification of the injection used with the norm factor.

Reviewer calculations checked the norm-kernel equality on \(1\oplus K[C_\ell]\) for \(1\le\ell\le8\), both over \(\mathbb Q\) and over \(\mathbb F_p\) for \(p=2,3,5,7\). For the 32 finite-field cases the equality held precisely when \(p\nmid\ell\) in this test module; the failures in the excluded characteristics came from the trivial summand. These finite checks illustrate the exact argument; they are not a replacement for its proof.

## Intersection ranks and boundary cases

The Mackey decomposition in Section 6.4 is a decomposition of the permutation module by double cosets. Applying Tor gives the sum of the ordinary ranks of the intersections, so cyclic intersections must not be silently discarded by replacing ordinary rank with reduced rank.

For the quotient to \(D\), the image of a left ideal \(L\) has dimension zero if \(L\) lies in the two-sided kernel \(I\), and dimension one otherwise: a nonzero element of its image is invertible in \(D\). The augmentation presentation then gives \[\dim_D\operatorname{Tor}_1(D,\mathbb QF/L)=\operatorname{rank}L-1+\delta,\qquad
 \delta=\begin{cases}1&L\subset I,\\0&L\not\subset I.\end{cases}\] The lifted flat module from Theorem 5.11 kills the other Tor term. This is the algebraic source of the two branches of Theorem 6.9. For \(L=0\) the expression is \(0-1+1=0\); for \(L=\mathbb QF\) it is \(1-1+0=0\). The trivial subgroup belongs to the normal closure, so the negative bound in the other branch is never asserted for it.

Five explicit folded core graphs in the free group on \(x,y\) were checked with \(W=\langle x\rangle\). For \(U=\langle x,yxy^{-1}\rangle,\langle x,y^2\rangle,
\langle y\rangle,\langle x^2\rangle,1\), respectively, the number of cyclic intersection components was \(2,1,0,1,0\), and the applicable bounds were \(2,1,0,1,0\). These are finite boundary illustrations only.

The second proof of the Magnus-subgroup intersection property uses a double of the complex and Mayer–Vietoris. When the NTPI exception is a free product of finite cyclic groups, its rational second Betti number is already zero, so the finiteness argument still works. The finite bireducible-complex induction then uses these intersection properties to amalgamate coherent vertex groups.

## Notation and scope clarifications; no numbered defect

On page 13 the strictly reducible definition places a new-generator letter in a quotient of the old-generator group and shifts a group index. The following explanatory paragraph identifies the intended reduced free-product normal form and separately treats a pure-generator collapsing relation. These are notation/index issues; no substantive counterexample was established from them.

On page 29 the Fox display and the subsequent right-module resolution use inconsistent visible sidedness. The right Fox identity is \(w-1=\sum_i(x_i-1)\alpha_i^R\). If \(\alpha_i^L\) denotes the left Fox coefficient, the corresponding right coefficient is \(\alpha_i^R=x_i^{-1}(\alpha_i^L)^*w\). After imposing \(w=1\), support distinctness is preserved by inversion and translation. The corrected side therefore retains the injection argument. Exact word reductions were checked for a commutator, for \(x^2y^3\), and for a generator relation. This inconsistency is not promoted to a core mathematical defect.

On page 35 a tensor subscript is printed as the scalar field where the immediately following Tor-zero identification requires \(\mathbb QF\). The stated dimension computation determines the intended tensor product. The isolated use of \(H\cap U\) instead of \(H\cap M\) on page 33 is similarly recoverable from the defined groups.

Needs Evidence, ancillary scope. Section 2.5 defines staggering using total orders, whereas the infinite presentation displayed in the proof of Theorem 1.5 on page 33 is indexed by integers and its reduction to a finite interval uses that indexing. This review did not establish the reduction for a dense or otherwise non-integer order type, nor produce a staggered group contradicting coherence. A precise order-type convention or an additional reduction argument would close this scope question. The finite-complex result and the main one-relator result do not depend on treating that question as resolved. It is not counted as a proved Major or Minor finding.
