# Definitions and scope

The argument is for an arbitrary complete first-order theory with a sufficiently saturated and homogeneous monster, and independence over small elementary submodels. No simplicity, countability, base-monotonicity or existence assumption over arbitrary sets is silently added. In this article Kim-dividing is witnessed by coheir Morley sequences, not by all invariant types. Fact 2.2 explicitly supplies the symmetry criterion in this coheir formulation.

The distinction between one witnessing Morley sequence and every coheir Morley sequence is essential: the latter is Conant-dividing. A canonical coheir is defined using the canonical relation \(\mathrel{\perp}^{CK}\); it need not be a unique extension. Strong canonical coheirs carry the additional lifting property of Lemma 5.6. The weak independence theorem requires this stronger hypothesis and is not asserted for arbitrary coheirs.

The secondary application, Theorem 4.9 and Corollary 4.10, is stated under \(\mathrm{NDCTP}_2\). The possible extension to all \(\mathrm{NATP}\) theories is explicitly conditional. No such unproved extension is included in this review’s positive assessment.

# Proof-chain checks

## Descending combs and the replacement of an older dependency

Pages 3480–3482 distinguish weak \(k\)-\(\mathrm{TP}_1\) from \(k\)-\(\mathrm{DCTP}_1\). The current proof does not use the defective strong-indiscernibility assertion from the older weak-tree argument. Lemma 2.8 instead works with the descending sequence of forks \(\eta_i=0^i1\), fixes the previously chosen paths as parameters, and takes the subtree rooted at \(0^{n-1}\). Its position relative to those parameter paths preserves their quantifier-free tree configuration. The maximal consistent number of paths then gives the two inconsistent path-types needed for path collapse.

The path-collapse statement and proof in Chernikov–Ramsey’s arXiv:1505.00454v2, Lemma 4.6 (differently numbered in the journal reference), were consulted. They use strong indiscernibility over the parameter set, indiscernibility of the chosen path over the realizer, compactness and finite elongation. The corrected subtree placement in the reviewed article supplies precisely the sensitive parameter-set condition. This does not amount to a complete reproof of the general tree-modeling theorem.

## Canonical coheirs without an NSOP assumption

Section 3 first uses finite satisfiability and left extension to lift a coheir sequence to a sequence of larger tuples. The ideal-valued broom argument then turns relative Kim-forking into relative quasi-dividing. Right extension is used in proving that relative inconsistency defines an ideal; quasi-strong finite character is used when passing from an inconsistent infinite family to a finite subfamily. These are distinct uses and cannot be omitted.

The derived relation preserves existence, monotonicity, right extension and quasi-strong finite character. Lemma 3.12 makes successive relations stronger and provides the finite-stage chain condition. Their decreasing intersection has the desired chain condition by compactness. The proof that this is the weakest relation with the stated properties was also followed. The passage to a larger monster in Lemma 4.1, with the orientation \(b\mathrel{\perp}^{CK}_M c\), produces a global coheir; the converse orientation is not assumed.

## Finite trees, a uniform bound and symmetry

Lemma 4.4 extends the chain condition to finite-height coheir trees. Lemma 4.5 adds copies of a tree before adding its new root: paths are read towards the root, whereas descending combs are read in lexicographic order. A descending comb crossing different immediate subtrees consists of a comb in the earliest subtree and one final node in a later subtree. Any second later node would repeat the common meet instead of strictly lowering it. This explains why the induction preserves the required canonical sequence property.

Theorem 4.6 uses a fixed inconsistency degree \(k\): arbitrarily long consistent coheir sequences would give trees of every finite height with \(k\)-inconsistent combs, hence \(k\)-\(\mathrm{DCTP}_1\) by compactness. This yields the required finite bound, which is stronger than merely comparing two infinite Morley sequences.

Proposition 5.2’s removal of unused parameters uses the coheir lifting lemma. Finite disjunctions are handled along a single coheir sequence by an infinite subsequence and the pigeonhole principle. The canonical chain condition then supplies the trees in Theorem 5.5; the uniform bound from Theorem 4.6 makes their descending combs inconsistent, proving symmetry by contradiction.

## Strong extension and the final configuration

The larger-model construction in Lemma 5.6 supplies the strong canonical extension used in Proposition 5.8. Its role is to lift a prescribed tuple while preserving the chosen coheir marginal; ordinary right extension alone would not provide that step. The joint independence in Claim 5.9 is obtained using symmetry and a canonical sequence indiscernible over the other parameter.

In Section 6, failure of equality between Kim-dividing independence and Conant-independence is converted to pairwise inconsistency by Claim 6.1. The maximal independent block has positive length and is strictly shorter than the original inconsistency degree. Grouping consecutive blocks and then using a canonical sequence in the other variable supplies the claimed reduction to two-inconsistency. Claim 6.2 follows by extending an invariant type over the indiscernible sequence. The construction of the switching sequences \(J_n\) then uses the weak independence theorem with its strong canonical hypothesis intact.

# Minor finding P1: the last witness needs a one-step shift

## Exact location and evidence

On page 3496 (PDF page 22), condition (1) defines \(J_n\) to use \(b_i^2\) for \(i<n\) and \(b_i^1\) for \(i\geq n\). On page 3497 (PDF page 23), \(a_n\) is chosen to realize the instances of \(r\) along \(J_n\), and the text asserts that the resulting array satisfies Fact 6.3(1). With \(\varphi(x,d_j)=\rho(x,b_j^1)\) and \(\psi(x,d_j)=\rho(x,b_j^2)\), the construction gives \[\varphi(a_i,d_j)\quad(j\geq i),\qquad
 \psi(a_i,d_j)\quad(j<i).\] Fact 6.3(1), however, requires \(\psi(a_i,d_i)\) as well.

This is not merely a failure to infer a diagonal value. Condition (2) on page 3496 holds for \(i\leq j\), so \(\rho(x,b_i^1)\land\rho(x,b_i^2)\) is inconsistent. Since \(\rho(a_i,b_i^1)\) holds, \(\psi(a_i,d_i)\) is false. Thus the stated choice cannot satisfy the printed nonstrict condition at the diagonal. The bound publisher PDF images confirm both inequalities.

## Repair and actual impact

Choose \(a_n\) along \(J_{n+1}\) instead. Then \(\varphi(a_i,d_j)\) holds for \(i<j\), and \(\psi(a_i,d_j)\) for \(i\geq j\), exactly as required. In Fact 6.3(2), the undeclared \(b_i,b_j\) should also read \(d_i,d_j\). The correction is local; no new independence or compactness assumption is needed.

For completeness, the actual strict off-diagonal pattern already suffices for the terminal contradiction. Define \[\chi(y,z):=\neg\exists x\,(\varphi(x,y)\land\psi(x,z))\] and \[R((x,y),(x',y')):=
 \varphi(x,y')\land\psi(x',y)\land\chi(y,y').\] The constructed sequence \((a_i,d_i)\) satisfies \(R\) for \(i<j\). A three-cycle with vertices \((x_\ell,y_\ell)\), \(\ell=1,2,3\), would imply \(\varphi(x_1,y_2)\) from its first edge, \(\psi(x_1,y_3)\) from its third edge, and \(\chi(y_2,y_3)\) from its second edge, an immediate contradiction. All formulas involve finitely many coordinates, even when an earlier intermediate tuple was infinite. This gives the defining \(\mathrm{SOP}_3\) configuration and confirms that the indexing correction does not impair Theorem 1.6.
