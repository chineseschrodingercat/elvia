Abstract: Theorem 8.1 of the assigned preprint is false: a factorial-gap power series, analytic near the closed unit disk but singular at radius two, admits supergeometric polynomial approximation along a subsequence of degrees. Separately, the proof of Theorem B uses total rational degree where the Green function requires the local pole order. Proposition 7.6 also omits multiplicities from the interpolation conditions needed for uniqueness. The first two issues are Major because they affect an announced sharpness result and a core approximation proof, respectively. The third has a limited, nonessential consequence. This initial review does not refute Theorem A or the existence conclusion of Theorem B; independent acceptance and adjudication remain pending.

# Problem 1: arbitrary sparse degrees do not force entire extension

Classification: Major. Location: Theorem 8.1, p. 32; the replacement of a subsequence by consecutive degrees in its proof, p. 33; the corresponding announced sharpness claim, p. 4.

Equation reference: lower. The theorem states that if \(K\) has positive logarithmic capacity and \(f\) does not extend holomorphically to \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus P\), there is \(D>1\) such that, for every \(C>0\) and every admissible sequence \(R_n\), \[
 \|f-R_n\|_K>C D^{-n}\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.\] The quantifier on \(n\), including arbitrary subsequences, is essential.

## A counterexample satisfying every hypothesis

Equation reference: gap. Take \(K=\{|z|\le1\}\), \(P=\{\infty\}\), \(U=\{|z|<2\}\), and \[
 f(z)=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(z/2)^{j!},\qquad
 R_n(z)=\sum_{\substack{j\ge2\\ j!\le n}}(z/2)^{j!}.\] The series converges locally uniformly on \(U\), which contains \(K\). The disk has positive logarithmic capacity (indeed, capacity one). There is exactly one complementary component on the sphere, containing the single prescribed pole \(\infty\). Every \(R_n\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\), so its pole set is contained in \(P\).

The radius of convergence of \(f\) is exactly two: its nonzero coefficients at indices \(j!\) have \(j!\)-th root \(1/2\). More directly, along the real radius \(r\uparrow2\), any fixed number of summands tends to that number, so \(f(r)\to+\infty\). Consequently \(f\) cannot have a holomorphic extension to \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus P=\mathbb C\).

Equation reference: tail. At \(n=k!\), however, \[
 \|f-R_{k!}\|_K
 \le\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}2^{-j!}
 \le\sum_{m=(k+1)!}^{\infty}2^{-m}
 =2^{1-(k+1)!}.\] For every fixed \(D>1\), \[D^{k!}\|f-R_{k!}\|_K
 \le 2\exp\{k![\log D-(k+1)\log2]\}\longrightarrow0.\] Thus for every \(D>1\) and every \(C>0\), infinitely many degrees violate [lower] (#lower). The example also directly refutes the supergeometric-subsequence extension assertion on p. 4.

## Failed proof step and repair boundary

For consecutive degrees, the pole order of \(R_{n+1}-R_n\) at a fixed allowed pole is at most \(n+1\). The proof combines this with an error bound \(O(D^{-n})\) to obtain a summable geometric majorant on a smaller Green level. After replacing \(n\) by \(n_k\), the relevant order is instead at most \(n_{k+1}\). The resulting factor is of the form \[D_1^{\,n_{k+1}}\bigl(D^{-n_k}+D^{-n_{k+1}}\bigr),
 \qquad 1<D_1<D,\] which need not decay. The factorial gaps exploit precisely this loss.

An estimate holding for every sufficiently large degree is a different hypothesis, under which the consecutive-degree argument can be used where the Green-function setup applies. In the disk/polynomial case it yields a restriction on the limsup rate, not the claimed lower bound at every large degree. No general repair for arbitrary \(P\) or irregular compact sets is certified here. Theorem B’s existence of a geometric upper bound and Theorem A’s approximation statement are not contradicted by this example.

# Problem 2: total degree is not the Green-function pole order

Classification: Major. Location: equation (7.1) and the proof of Lemma 7.2, p. 29; the resulting estimate (7.10) in the proof of Theorem B, p. 31. The controlling definition is (4.1) and Notation 4.2, pp. 15–16, reiterated on p. 28.

Equation reference: local. The source defines each Green function with a unit logarithmic singularity, and sums one such function per prescribed pole. In Section 7 there is one pole \(p\) per grey face \(F\). If \(r\) has local pole order \(m_p\) there and \(L_r=\{|r|=1\}\), uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem gives, inside that face, \[
 \log|r(z)|=m_p G_F(z,p).\] At a finite pole, both sides have principal term \(-m_p\log|z-p|\); at infinity they have principal term \(m_p\log|z|\). Equation (7.1) instead writes \(u_{L_r,P}=d^{-1}\log|r|\), where \(d=\deg r\). When there is more than one pole, \(m_p<d=\sum_{p\in P}m_p\), and the logarithmic coefficients do not match. The discrepancy occurs inside grey faces, so it is independent of the additional need to truncate to zero on white faces.

For a concrete admissible geometry let \[r(z)=\frac{z^2+1}{3z},\qquad d=2,\qquad P=\{0,\infty\}.\] Both poles are simple. On \(|z|=1\), \(|r(z)|\le2/3<1\), separating the grey component at zero from the one at infinity. The only critical points are \(\pm1\), with critical values \(\pm2/3\); hence the level \(L_r(1)\) is regular. Each grey face contains one pole. On the unbounded face, \[G_F(z,\infty)=\log|z|+O(1),\qquad
 d^{-1}\log|r(z)|=\tfrac12\log|z|+O(1),\] which disproves (7.1).

The subsequent separation implication can also fail for this \(r\). Put \(K=\{|r|\le3/4\}\). Its two smooth boundary curves enclose an annular compact set; its complementary components contain exactly \(0\) and \(\infty\). In those components \(u_{\partial K,P}=\log(|r|/(3/4))\). For \(\rho=3/2\) and \(R=4\), one has \(C_\rho=\{|r|=9/8\}\) and \(C_R=\{|r|=3\}\). A neighborhood \(U=\{|r|<4\}\) meets the required containment and separation conditions. Although \(L_r(1)\) separates \(K\) from \(C_\rho\), the asserted higher level is \(L_r(64/9)\), beyond \(C_R\), and does not separate \(K\) from \(C_R\). This example refutes the proof’s claimed implication for the chosen \(r\); it is not by itself a disproof of the existential statement of Lemma 7.2 with unrestricted poles.

## Impact and a local repair calculation

The normalization enters the degree-dependent estimate used to prove Theorem B, so its effect is substantive even though the existence theorem may survive. Using [local] (#local), let \(\ell=\min_{p\in P}m_p>0\). The same facewise comparison instead gives a usable level \((R/\rho)^\ell\), and hence an estimate of the form \[\|f-R_{dn-1}\|_K\le C_0(R/\rho)^{-\ell n}.\] In terms of degree, a possible geometric base is \(B=(R/\rho)^{\ell/d}>1\), with a suitably enlarged prefactor. This calculation repairs the local normalization and retains a geometric rate, conditional on the existing separation and regularization inputs; it is not a certification of every part of the full revised proof. In addition, the elementary contour estimate uses \(\|f\|_{C_R}\), not \(\|f\|_K\), in \(C_0\). Enlarging the constant also handles the finitely many initially unassigned degrees and the requirement \(A>1\). These constant adjustments are not counted as separate substantive Problems.

# Problem 3: uniqueness omits zero multiplicities

Classification: Minor. Location: Proposition 7.6 and its equation-count argument, p. 32.

The proposition prescribes \(R^{(j)}(a)=f^{(j)}(a)\) for \(0\le j<n\) at each zero \(a\) of \(r\), then claims uniqueness among degree-\(\le dn-1\) rational functions with the same poles and pole orders as the constructed approximant. The proof counts \(dn\) conditions without assuming that \(r\) has simple zeros.

Take \(K=\{|z|\le1/2\}\), \(P=\{\infty\}\), \(U=\{|z|<3\}\), \(f(z)=z\), \(r(z)=z^2\), \(d=2\), and \(n=1\). Choose \(R=4\) and \(\rho=3\); then \(C_R=\{|z|=2\}\), and both \(L_r(1)\) and \(L_r((R/\rho)^2)=\{|z|=4/3\}\) satisfy the required separation. The defining Cauchy formula gives \[R_1(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=2}
 \frac{\zeta}{\zeta-z}\left(1-\frac{z^2}{\zeta^2}\right)\,d\zeta
 =\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=2}(1+z/\zeta)\,d\zeta=z.\] But \(2z\) also has degree one, the same pole at infinity of order one, and the sole required condition \(R(0)=f(0)=0\). It is distinct from \(R_1\).

If \(a\) has multiplicity \(m_a\), the defining error actually vanishes to order at least \(nm_a\). Replacing the printed conditions by all jets \(0\le j<nm_a\) supplies \(nd\) conditions, since all zeros are finite after the chosen pole at infinity. With the same pole divisor, a nonzero difference has at most \(dn-1\) zeros, so the corrected uniqueness argument works. Alternatively, simplicity of all zeros would suffice, but is not stated. The source expressly says this proposition is not needed for Theorem B; its error therefore has limited impact and is classified Minor.
