Abstract: This report examines the voracious projection and geodesic-language proof in Osajda and Przytycki’s arXiv version 2. An exact example in \((C_2\times C_2)*C_2\) shows that the lexicographic complexity printed in the projection-existence argument need not decrease. Adding its omitted height-zero coordinate gives a complete termination correction. The invalid termination step is a Major defect in the core projection proof; the verified repair does not assert a counterexample to biautomaticity. The review separately traces bounded projection steps, both fellow-traveller estimates, finite-state recognition, and the hypotheses of the building transfer. Conclusions are restricted to the assigned author manuscript; scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Definitions, quantifiers, and the proof chain

Section 2 explicitly uses a finite Coxeter generating set \(S\). Thus the Main Theorem is read under the paper’s finite-rank convention. The group element represented by a word \(v\) has length \(\ell(v)\); prefixes are held constant after the endpoint when testing fellow travelling.

For \(g\in W\), let \(\mathcal W(g)\) be the separating walls closest to \(g\) in the separation order, and define \[P(g)=\{p:p\preceq g,\ 
       \text{no wall in }\mathcal W(g)\text{ separates }p\text{ from }\operatorname{id}\}.\] The order \(p\preceq g\) means that \(p\) is on a geodesic from \(\operatorname{id}\) to \(g\). Theorem 2.1 gives a largest element \(p(g)\) of \(P(g)\). For \(g\ne\operatorname{id}\), it is strictly below \(g\).

The recursive language \(\mathcal V\) attaches any minimal word for \(p(g)^{-1}g\) to an allowed word for \(p(g)\). The strict length decrease makes the recursion well founded. Since \[\ell(g)=\ell(p(g))+\ell(p(g)^{-1}g),\] induction proves that its words are geodesic and that every element has a representative. Finite \(S\) then implies finitely many representatives per element. The empty word is the only allowed representative of \(\operatorname{id}\). No assertion about infinitely generated Coxeter systems is required.

The principal dependency chain is:

1. Theorem 2.1 and the rank-three wall lemma establish the projection.

2. Proposition 4.1 bounds every final block by one constant \(C(W)\).

3. Section 5 establishes synchronous right and left fellow travelling for every pair of allowed representatives, not merely selected words.

4. Section 6 supplies finite-state recognition; finite-to-one geodesicity then permits the stated biautomaticity criterion.

5. Invariance under automorphisms preserving \(S\) is the additional input for the external building-transfer result.

# A missing coordinate in the termination complexity

Problem 1 (Invalid termination step in the projection proof; Major).  The proof of Theorem 2.1 on p. 6 defines the complexity of its embedded path as \((n_1,\ldots,n_{\ell(g)})\), where \(n_j\) counts height-\(j\) vertices. Its prescribed valley replacement need not decrease this tuple when the valley is the identity.

## A fully specified example

Take \[W=\langle s,t,u\mid s^2=t^2=u^2=1,\ st=ts\rangle
   =(C_2\times C_2)*C_2,\qquad g=stu .\] There are no further relations involving \(u\) with \(s\) or \(t\). The Cayley graph consists of squares joined by \(u\)-edges. The only reduced words for \(g\) are \(stu\) and \(tsu\), so \[[\operatorname{id},g]=\{\operatorname{id},s,t,st,stu\}.\] The three walls separating \(g\) from \(\operatorname{id}\) are \(W_s,W_t,stW_u\). The last wall is the midpoint of the final bridge and separates \(g\) from each of the first two. Hence \[\mathcal W(g)=\{stW_u\},\qquad P(g)=\{\operatorname{id},s,t,st\}.\] This verifies the hypotheses on the endpoints and the whole path, rather than presenting an arbitrary sequence of heights.

The geodesic \(\pi=(s,\operatorname{id},t)\) is an allowed path in \(P(g)\). The rank-two residue is the commuting square; the prescribed second path is \(\pi'=(s,st,t)\). Their printed complexities are \[(n_1,n_2,n_3)(\pi)=(2,0,0),\qquad
 (n_1,n_2,n_3)(\pi')=(2,1,0).\] The new tuple is larger in lexicographic order.

The original reviewer program `checks/main/complexity_witness.py` represents the two finite factors by exact normal forms, computes the weak interval, checks separating reflections and the nearest-wall set, and then evaluates the tuples. Its actual output agrees with the displayed calculation. This is not author code and is not a computation over an unverified finite truncation of a general Coxeter group.

## Complete local correction and impact

Use instead \[\kappa(\pi)=(n_0,n_1,\ldots,n_{\ell(g)}).\] For the example this changes \[(1,2,0,0)\longmapsto(0,2,1,0),\] which strictly decreases. In general a valley at height \(j\) removes one height-\(j\) vertex; the internal vertices of the opposite rank-two path have strictly greater height. Counts at smaller heights are unchanged. Erasing a resulting loop cannot increase any coordinate, so the strict decrease survives.

All vertices remain in the finite interval \([\operatorname{id},g]\) and the path is made embedded after each replacement. Thus only finitely many corrected count tuples occur, proving termination. A path without an interior down-then-up step has an up-then-down height profile; its peak lies above both endpoints. The directedness argument then gives the largest point of the finite set \(P(g)\).

This correction retains the geometric content of Lemma 3.2 and the rank-two replacement. The example does not contradict the existence of \(p(g)\); indeed \(p(g)=st\) in the example. The original termination argument is invalid in Theorem 2.1, which supplies the projection used by the core normal-form construction. Its original proof impact is therefore Major. The complete verified repair and survival of the intended conclusion are recorded separately.

# Rank-three reduction and the projection invariant

The reflection subgroup in Lemma 3.2 has rank at most three, not automatically exactly three from the rank bound alone. The disjoint wall supplies an infinite reflection product. An infinite rank-two Coxeter group is infinite dihedral and cannot contain the given finite, noncommuting reflection pair. Thus the rank is exactly three. After choosing an ambient chamber in the specified acute sector, that pair consists of ambient simple generators. The canonical reflection-subgroup construction retains them as simple generators. Dyer’s author-hosted thesis, Theorem 1.8, Remark 3.14 and Corollary 3.16, was checked for precisely these assertions [Dyer].

Write the three finite or ideal triangle angles as \(\alpha=\pi/m,\ \beta=\pi/n,\ \gamma=\pi/k\), with \(m<\infty\), and put \(x=\cos\alpha,\ y=\cos\beta,\ z=\cos\gamma\). For the dihedral walls, the positive roots are \[v_i=a_i r+b_i q,\qquad
 a_i=\frac{\sin((i+1)\alpha)}{\sin\alpha},\quad
 b_i=\frac{\sin(i\alpha)}{\sin\alpha}.\] The generic branch of Figure 1 follows by checking \(y a_i+z b_i\ge1\) for every nonexceptional \(i\). For \(n,k\ge3\), use \(y,z\ge1/2\) and \(a_i+b_i\ge1+2x\ge2\). If \(k=2\), the finite-\(n\) exceptions are precisely those separated out in the paper; when \(n=\infty\), \(y=1\) and \(a_i\ge1\) suffice. In the \(n=3,k=2\) branch, infinitude forces \(m\ge6\), and \(\sin(3\alpha)/\sin\alpha=4x^2-1\ge2\).

For the exceptional cutting walls, the relevant inner products include \[1-2x^2-2y^2\le-1,\qquad
 -8x^4+6x^2-1\le-1.\] The equality cases are parallel or asymptotic walls, and remain disjoint in the required sense. The intermediate wall in Figure 2 has inner product \(-2x(2x^2-1)\); its intersecting subcase is \(m=6\). The bounded check also verifies wall sides and the complete two-, three-, four-, and six-chamber caps, so a disjointness calculation is not being substituted for the separation conclusion. The last reflection identity is \[r(srqrW_s)=srqW_r .\] An original exact polynomial calculation checks the reflection and cap-boundary identities. A separate finite numerical sweep is recorded as supporting evidence only; the all-parameter conclusions use the displayed inequalities and the explicit chamber reconstruction.

For the parabolic application in Theorem 2.1, set \(h=p^{-1}g\) at a valley \(p\). Both adjacent generators \(a,b\) are left descents of \(h\). Every positive root of the standard subsystem on \(\{a,b\}\) is therefore mapped by \(h^{-1}\) to a negative root. This embeds that subsystem’s positive roots in the finite inversion set of \(h\), forcing the subsystem to be finite. Inversion-set inclusion then puts every element of its standard parabolic group below \(h\) in weak order. Translating by \(p\) puts the whole residue on geodesics to \(g\).

Both initial residue walls are inversion walls excluded from \(\mathcal W(g)\); the wall lemma excludes every additional residue wall. Consequently the replacement stays in \(P(g)\). Its initial geodesic also stays there, since \(P(g)\) is an intersection of the relevant wall halfspaces. These facts close the invariant used in Problem Problem 1 (#prob:zero)’s correction. The exact cited Ronan page was not acquired; the preceding specific consequence was independently derived from the root and inversion framework, rather than inferred from an unverified citation.

# Bounded steps and the fellow-traveller constants

The uniformity required is in \(g\) and the separating wall, for one fixed finite Coxeter system. A bounded radius meets only finitely many walls because the Cayley graph has finite degree. The Parallel Wall Theorem consequently bounds \(|\mathcal W(g)|\) by one \(N(W)\). Its cited source, Brink–Howlett Theorem 2.8, states finiteness of elementary roots [BH]. The translation was checked: domination is inclusion of the negative halfspaces, so a non-elementary root has an intervening wall. For a positive root \(\rho\), \[d(\operatorname{id},U_\rho)=\operatorname{dp}(\rho)-\tfrac12.\] Taking the largest depth among the finitely many elementary roots and then left translating gives the uniform parallel-wall constant \(Q\). This accounts for the difference between the literal root statement and the wall formulation.

The geometric bound in Lemma 4.3 uses the finitely many shapes of spherical Coxeter cells. A vertex is a positive distance from each wall section not containing it, so the minimum relevant angle is strictly positive. With the source’s notation, the CAT(0) comparison gives \[c_2\sin\alpha\le d,\qquad
 c\le\operatorname{diam}(\sigma)+d/\sin\alpha .\] Here is the missing geometric detail made explicit. The first exit face of the initial Coxeter cell is a parabolic coset \(wW_U\) not containing the initial vertex. A first letter of its shortest representative starts every reduced face-vertex word, so its simple wall separates the initial vertex from that face. The initial straight segment must cross that wall before exiting. Convexity of fixed-point walls excludes a transverse crossing of any other simple wall; thus the wall is \(W_s\). For a fixed cell, its relevant positive vertex-to-wall distances and bounded diameter give a positive angle bound. Reflecting the geodesic tail in \(W_s\), then applying CAT(0) convexity of distance, yields the displayed inequality. This argument requires no global assertion that the geodesic never touches another wall.

For the at-most-\(N\) iteration, an unpassed wall of \(\mathcal W(g)\) must remain eligible at the current weak-order predecessor. A hypothetical new wall between that predecessor and the original wall would also separate \(g\) from the original wall, contradicting membership in \(\mathcal W(g)\). This supplies the needed preservation condition; previously passed walls stay passed under further weak-order descent. The resulting point is in \(P(g)\), and maximality gives \[d(g,p(g))\le C_0N=:C.\]

The right-fellow-traveller proof interleaves the \(p\)-iterates for adjacent endpoints. In either alternating interval of prefix lengths, both prefixes are within \(C\) of a suitable projection anchor. The second path need not literally contain that anchor: the intervening weak-order segment has exactly the length difference needed for the same bound. The resulting constant is \(2C\).

For left multiplication by \(s\), the case where \(W_s\) is not a nearest wall gives \(p(sg)=sp(g)\), permitting induction on \(\ell(g)\). In the other case the reflection displacement is at most \(2Q\). Chaining the right-fellow-traveller estimate along a generator path of length at most \(C+2Q\), then adding the displacement, gives \[C'=2C(C+2Q)+2Q .\] The padded tail case also has a direct bound \(2C\): the two remaining geodesic tails have lengths at most \(C\) and \(C-1\) when \(\ell(sg)=\ell(g)+1\). These checks retain the distinction between right adjacency and left translation.

# Finite-state recognition and the building implication

The state set is the power set of the finite set of elementary walls near the identity. Condition \(p(w)=\operatorname{id}\), together with Proposition 4.1, bounds the transition block length by \(C\); finite \(S\) therefore makes both the candidate block set and the collections of minimal word labels finite. A finite word-labeled automaton can be expanded into an ordinary letter-labeled automaton by adding finitely many states.

The identity block produces only an empty-word loop at the initial empty state. At a nonempty state, condition (b) rules it out because its walls were defined to have no wall separating them from the identity. Removing empty initial loops makes the last block of every nonempty accepted word nonempty, justifying strict word-length induction. If more than one block gives the same ordered pair of states, the finite label collections can be united on that arrow; no actual collision is asserted without evidence.

Here are the recognition implications with their invariant stated. At a reachable state \(a=p^{-1}\mathcal W(p)\), every old inversion wall is either in \(\mathcal W(p)\) or shielded by one of those walls. Condition (a) therefore gives \(p\preceq pw=g\), and condition (b) hides every old wall from \(g\). Hence \(\mathcal W(g)=p\mathcal W(w)\). In the forward direction, \(p=p(g)\) has these properties. If \(q=p(w)\), then \(pq\in P(g)\), so maximality of \(p(g)\) forces \(q=\operatorname{id}\). In the reverse direction, (a) and (b) give \(p\in P(g)\). Writing \(p(g)=pq\) then gives \(q\in P(w)\), and condition (c) forces \(q=\operatorname{id}\). The final block is therefore exactly the recursive block. The empty-loop observation above makes the induction strict whenever the word is nonempty.

Amrhein’s Theorem 6 requires a regular onto language whose evaluation map has finite fibers over a finite inverse-closed generating alphabet [Amrhein]. All these hypotheses hold here. To connect the paper’s separate conditions (ii) and (iii) to that criterion, insert a representative of \(a\bar v\) when comparing \(avb\) with another representative of the same element. A left prefix shifts time by one step, and a final generator costs one further step; a finite bound follows from the two established fellow-traveller constants. If both added letters are empty, compare both representatives with one of \(\bar v s\), using the right estimate twice. The empty generating-set case is trivial. Thus neither equal endpoints nor padded tails leave a gap.

An automorphism of \(W\) preserving \(S\) preserves the wall data and weak order. Uniqueness gives equivariance of \(p(g)\); allowing all minimal final-block words makes \(\mathcal V\) invariant. This algebraic invariance supplies the extra hypothesis of Świątkowski’s Theorem 6.7 [Swi]. Theorem 6.1 assumes a simplicial building action with finite panel residues, finitely many chamber orbits, and finite chamber stabilizers. Its remark identifies these conditions with proper discontinuity and cocompactness on the Davis realization. Theorem 6.7 removes type preservation when the geodesic (bi)automatic language is invariant under the induced subgroup of \(\operatorname{Sym}(W,S)\). The language here is invariant under the whole group, so the stated corollary follows under that action convention. Both numbered statements and the intervening remark were actually read in the author-uploaded journal HTML; an official earlier DVI copy was also retained.
