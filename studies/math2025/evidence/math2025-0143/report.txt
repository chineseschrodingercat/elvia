Abstract: The inspected author manuscript contains three demonstrable local construction problems: a missing valuation factor in the explicit Cox-ring map, and a normalization assertion that fails for choices of generators permitted by the text. A further boundary example obstructs the regular toric perturbation asserted in Lemma 4.3. The examples do not refute the Craw–Ishii theorem. The review also identifies unresolved obligations in the nonabelian Type 0 wall argument and in the passage from ambient toric data to the required maps and sections. The supported local findings are Minor; the outstanding core proof checks are marked Needs Evidence rather than being presented as established errors.

# An explicit Cox-ring formula

Problem 1 (Minor: the valuation factor in (3.4)).  Equation (3.4), p. 17, gives \[\phi_C(h)=\phi(h)\prod_k t_k^{-r_k\operatorname{ord}_{E_k}(h)},\] where the following sentence defines \(\operatorname{ord}_{E_k}(h)\) as the coefficient of \(E_k\) in the zero divisor \(D_h\). This conflicts with the associated lift in (3.1), p. 10, and with the degree of a weight-zero invariant.

Take \(G=\langle\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)\rangle\), and take the resolution \(X\) given by the minimal resolution of the \(A_1\) surface singularity times \(\mathbb A^1\). There is one exceptional divisor \(E\), with \(r=2\) and \[\nu(x)=\nu(y)=1,\qquad \nu(z)=0.\] The length-two \(x\)-cycle \(h=x_0x_1\) in the McKay quiver is an invariant of weight zero. Thus \(\phi(h)=x^2\), and the coefficient of \(E\) in its pullback zero divisor is \(\nu(x^2)/2=1\).

Set \(a=xt\) and \(e=t^{-2}\), using the coordinates of (3.1). The geometric definition of \(\phi_C\) sends \(h\) to the degree-zero element \[x^2=a^2e.\] The printed formula instead gives \(x^2t^{-2}=a^2e^2\). Since \(e\) has degree \([E]\), this changes the degree and adds an exceptional component to the divisor. It is therefore not simply a choice of a nonzero scalar representative.

For the divisor convention stated in the paper, the corrected formula is \[\phi_C(h)=\widetilde{\phi(h)}
             \prod_k t_k^{-r_k\operatorname{ord}_{E_k}(h)}
 =\phi(h)\prod_k t_k^{\nu_k(\phi(h))-r_k\operatorname{ord}_{E_k}(h)}.\] It passes the invariant test above. Equation (3.8), p. 20, already uses the associated lifts and provides substantial counterevidence to interpreting (3.4) as a failure of the geometric construction. The correction is local; no failure of Theorem 4.1 or Proposition 4.14 follows from this example.

# Compatibility of the chosen generators

Problem 2 (Minor: Lemma 3.3 for the permitted choices).  The construction on p. 18 chooses Cox generators \(f_i\), then semi-invariant generators \(h_j\) whose images have specified monomial presentations in the \(f_i\). Lemma 3.3, pp. 18–19, identifies the resulting map \(\overline Y_W\to Y_W\) with normalization. The stated choices do not ensure this.

Let \(G=\{1\}\), so \(W=X=\mathbb A^3\), \(\Theta=0\), \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)=0\), and there are no exceptional divisors. Choose \[(f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4)=(x,y,z,x+y),\qquad
 (h_1,h_2,h_3)=(x,y,z),\] with the presentations \(\phi(h_i)=f_i\) for \(i=1,2,3\). These are homogeneous generating systems of the required rings. Minimality of the Cox generators is not imposed; the remark on p. 14 only adds a neatness property when they are minimal.

The cone \(\sigma_W=\sigma_A\) is the positive orthant in \(\mathbb R^4\). In the notation of pp. 18–19, \[S_W=\mathbb N^4,\qquad
 S'_W=\mathbb N^3\times\{0\}.\] Consequently \(\overline Y_W=\mathbb A^4\), \(Y_W=\mathbb A^3\), and the alleged normalization map is the coordinate projection \(\mathbb A^4\to\mathbb A^3\). It is neither finite nor birational. The polynomial ring in three variables is already integrally closed; adjoining an independent fourth variable is not its integral closure.

The proof on p. 19 tests regularity after restriction to \(\overline W\). Here the fourth ambient coordinate restricts to \(x+y\), which is regular on \(W\). That fact cannot establish the required equality of ambient semigroup rings: the restriction has forgotten a relation. Thus the example also isolates the failure of that inference.

Adding \(h_4=x+y\) with the presentation \(\phi(h_4)=f_4\) repairs this example, as does choosing the compatible minimal systems. It does not by itself prove the general saturation assertion or the claim that normalization preserves the specified closed immersion. A revised construction should specify compatible generators and prove the asserted integral-closure and quotient properties. This is a counterexample to the unrestricted auxiliary construction, not to the existential embedding theorem or the Craw–Ishii conclusion.

# The perturbation in Lemma 4.3 at a boundary face

Problem 3 (Minor: the claimed regular toric perturbation need not exist).  Lemma 4.3, pp. 31–32, allows any face \(F\) with \(\operatorname{relint}(F)\cap\overline C\ne\varnothing\) and \(\psi_C(F)\) not full-dimensional. Its proof moves an exceptional ray to a general ray in \(\sigma_k\), asserting that the resulting regular toric morphism still induces the same quotient isomorphism. The following allowed boundary case prevents those properties from holding simultaneously.

For \(G=\frac12(1,1,0)\), use \[W=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf C[a,b,c,d,z]/(ad-bc),
 \qquad \mathrm{wt}(a,b,c,d,z)=(1,1,-1,-1,0).\] The positive chamber quotient is the \(A_1\) resolution times \(\mathbb A^1\). Its Cox coordinates \(u,v,w,z\) have degrees \(1,1,-2,0\), and the positive-chamber map is \[(a,b,c,d,z)=(u,v,uw,vw,z).\] At the zero-section point represented by \((a,b,c,d,z)=(1,0,0,0,1)\), the orbit cone is the positive ray. Its face \(F=\{0\}\) satisfies both hypotheses of the lemma: it meets \(\overline C\), and \(\psi_C(F)=\{0\}\) has dimension zero in the one-dimensional Picard space.

The two exceptional ray valuations on \((a,b,c,d)\) are \[\tau_+=(0,0,1,1),\qquad \tau_-=(1,1,0,0).\] The proof’s general ray in their cone has strictly positive values on all four coordinates. A regular toric morphism gives integral orders of vanishing; thus each is at least one along \(w=0\). Every generator of the quotient singular-locus ideal \[I=(ac,ad,bd)\subset
 \mathbf C[ac,ad,bd,z]/((ac)(bd)-(ad)^2)\] would then pull back with order at least two. But the original Cox quotient pulls this ideal back to \[(u^2w,uvw,v^2w),\] of order exactly one along the prime divisor \(w=0\). An isomorphism of the quotient preserves its singular-locus ideal, so it cannot produce this change of order. Allowing an automorphism of the quotient does not remove the contradiction. Using a fractional interior ray instead would not define the asserted integral regular toric map.

The lemma’s conclusion is true in this example: the zero section contracts to the \(A_1\) singular locus. Omitting the unnecessary perturbation at \(F=0\) handles this case. The classification is local because this group supplies no flop contradicting the main application. A compatible construction for the faces used in the general flop argument still requires proof. The example establishes a failure of the proposed auxiliary construction, not a failure of the Craw–Ishii theorem.

# Core proof obligations not resolved by this review

## Reducedness in Lemma 4.5

The proof on p. 34 uses a coordinate ideal \(I_S\), generated by arrows from \(R_1\) to \(R_2\), and asserts that \[f^m\in I_N+I_S\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_0^m\in I_N,\] where \(f_0\) deletes terms containing those arrows. Commutativity relations alone do not justify this inference.

An exact test can be made within a McKay representation space. Let \(G=\langle\operatorname{diag}(\zeta,\zeta^{-1},1)\rangle\), \(\zeta^3=1\), and index a regular-representation basis by \(0,1,2\). Take \(X\) to be cyclic shift, \(Y=X^{-1}\), and \(Z=0\); these commute and describe a free orbit. Split \(R_1=\langle e_0,e_1\rangle\), \(R_2=\langle e_2\rangle\). Deleting the forbidden entries \(X_{21},Y_{20}\) gives \[X'=E_{10}+E_{02},\qquad Y'=E_{01}+E_{12},
 \qquad [X',Y']=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,0).\] More explicitly, the \((0,0)\) commutator relation is \(p=X_{02}Y_{20}-Y_{01}X_{10}\). Its surviving part \(p_0=-Y_{01}X_{10}\) belongs to \(I_N+I_S\), but takes the value \(-1\) at the original commuting point and hence does not belong to \(I_N\). The asserted inference already fails at \(m=1\) for this coordinate split.

This split has not been shown to arise from a Type 0 wall. In fact this surface-quotient product does not supply the compact unstable divisors needed for that use. The computation therefore does not refute Lemma 4.5 in its actual wall setting. It does show that the stated general polynomial mechanism is insufficient: a Type 0-specific argument or a different proof of reducedness is needed. Ambient smoothness alone does not ensure reducedness of a coordinate section. An attracting-locus argument would also have to control the limiting split representation, which need not remain in the smooth stable locus.

## Product structure and the subsequent \(K\)-group argument

Lemma 4.10, pp. 38–39, uses \[G\text{-}\operatorname{Ext}^1(S,Q)
 \simeq G\text{-}\operatorname{Ext}^1(Q,S)\simeq\mathbf C,\] then passes from the tangent-space calculation and the map of Lemma 4.9 to a product of curves. The preceding discussion explicitly allows nonprimitive or decomposable factors. The review did not complete the justification of these Ext dimensions in that generality, the scheme-level product identification, or the independently selectable contractions of the two factors. Lemma 4.12, p. 41, further needs a global splitting statement for the particular bundles on the Dynkin trees; splitting on individual \(\mathbb P^1\) components alone does not establish it.

These are recorded as Needs Evidence, not as counterexamples. Once the stated geometric and pairing inputs are assumed, the checked \(K\)-group calculation is consistent: \(\chi(E,E)=0\) makes \[T_E=I-E\otimes\chi(-,E),\qquad
 T_E^{-1}=I+E\otimes\chi(-,E),\qquad \det T_E=1.\] The image-collinearity and divisor-independence argument in Proposition 4.13 then gives connectedness, and the elementary-transform identities give the claimed rank-one action. Thus a failure of this conditional linear algebra is not alleged.

## Toric perturbation and prescribed sections

The perturbation in Lemma 4.3, pp. 31–32, requires compatible lattice maps, preservation of the equations defining \(W\), and the asserted quotient identification. Preservation of projected cones alone does not verify all of these requirements. The bounded check did not finish that construction. The local coordinate-stratum descriptions also require care: an orbit cone can correspond to a union of coordinate strata, and a chosen locally closed stratum need not be closed in the semistable locus.

In Proposition 4.14, p. 43, the existence of a homogeneous section with the specified scheme-theoretic zero divisor is the remaining geometric obligation. Normality by itself is not a general section-surjectivity theorem. The particular toric contraction may supply the needed property, but that step was not independently established here. Conditional on the asserted section \(f\) with \(\phi_C(f)=\widetilde f_i\), the final algebraic generation deduction is valid: expand \(f\) in the homogeneous generators \(h_j\), and use the corrected formula to express every \(\phi_C(h_j)\) as its associated lift times nonnegative powers of the exceptional generators. General valuation cancellation is therefore not a counterexample to that final deduction.
