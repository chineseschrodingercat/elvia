# Statements and assumptions

Theorem 1.2, pp. 2–3, assumes normal finite-type complex domains, a pure extension \(R\hookrightarrow S\), a reduced divisor \(D\), and an effective \(\mathbb Q\)-Weil divisor \(\Gamma\) sharing no component with \(D\). It also assumes finite generation of \[\bigoplus_{i\geq0}\mathcal O_X(\lfloor-i(K_X+D+\Gamma)\rfloor)\] and reduced cycle-theoretic pullback \(E=f^\natural D\). There are two distinct conclusions: \[\operatorname{adj}_E(Y,E+f^*\Gamma)
 \subseteq \operatorname{adj}_D(X,D+\Gamma)\mathcal O_Y\] under faithful flatness, and \[f_*\operatorname{adj}_E(Y,E+f^*\Gamma)\cap\mathcal O_X
 \subseteq\operatorname{adj}_D(X,D+\Gamma)\] under purity with the additional disjointness of the prime components of \(E\). For the latter, the paper further requires either the \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier condition on \(K_X+D+\Gamma\), or finite generation of the indicated divisor algebras on \(Y\). These conditions were not dropped.

The definitions on pp. 5–8 use elements avoiding the generic points of \(D\), and distinguish plt or klt type from a classical \(\mathbb Q\)-Gorenstein singularity. The real exponent of an auxiliary ideal is positive in the later descent statements; the simpler displayed theorem has no auxiliary ideal. The special case \(D=0\) is the multiplier-ideal case and must be read separately from intermediate statements formulated for a prime divisor.

# Closure and ultraproduct checks

## Finite extensions and the distinguished prime

Sections 3–4, pp. 12–20, first construct the parameter test submodule and identify it with a trace image after a sufficiently large finite extension. The choice of a prime in the absolute integral closure above \(D\) matters. Definitions 4.2 and 5.2, and Remark 5.3, explicitly retain that choice; the construction is not asserted to be canonical independently of it.

In Lemma 3.9, pp. 17–18, the quotient is localized at a minimal point of its support, producing a finite-length dual module. The two uses of the equational lemma have different targets: first the ambient absolute integral closure, then the quotient by the distinguished prime. The injectivity of the connecting map after the big Cohen–Macaulay vanishing is what transfers Frobenius stability to its preimage. This step does not follow merely by taking an arbitrary preimage under a surjection.

The finite-extension trace result, its Matlis-dual kernel description, and the codimension-two comparison yield Proposition 4.6: \[\tau_D(R,D+\Delta)=\bigcap_J (J:J^{+_D(D+\Delta)}).\] The codimension-two comparison in Lemma 4.5 only needs the kernel and cokernel of the multiplication map to have support in codimension at least two. Top local cohomology then gives the required isomorphism. A literal tensor-product injectivity assertion is stronger than this necessary argument, so possible torsion in that tensor product does not itself supply a counterexample to the cohomology statement.

## The injectivity needed in Theorem 5.7

The natural map from local cohomology of an arbitrary ultraproduct to the ultraproduct of local cohomology is not assumed injective in general; p. 12 expressly leaves that open. Lemma 5.6, pp. 21–22, instead uses the exact sequences for \(\mathcal B(I_D)\), \(\mathcal B(R)\), and \(\mathcal B(R/I_D)\).

The cited auxiliary injectivity statement was checked in Yamaguchi’s Lemma 4.7 [bcm]. Its applicable setting is an ultraproduct of big Cohen–Macaulay algebras. The regular-sequence colon equality removes the potentially unbounded denominator-clearing exponent in each characteristic. Hence a vanishing ultraproduct class has a representative already in the ideal generated by a fixed power of the parameters. This gives the two outside injections in the diagram of Lemma 5.6; an elementary diagram chase then gives the middle injection.

For Theorem 5.7, pp. 22–24, the first inclusion uses an element \(x\in J^{\mathcal B_D(D+\Delta)}\), a fixed finite set of generators of \(J\), and approximation of the adjoint ideal by divisorial test ideals. It does not require transferring an arbitrary infinite conjunction through the ultrafilter. The reverse inclusion uses the log-resolution cohomology diagram and the just-established injection. The finite presentation in Lemma 2.14 justifies tensoring through the ultraproduct when that step is used. The review retains the characteristic-\(p\) comparison theorem and the equational lemma as external dependencies.

# Divisors and purity

## Natural and asymptotic pullbacks

Definition 6.1, pp. 24–25, distinguishes \[\operatorname{ord}_E(f^\natural D)=v_E(\mathcal O_X(-D))
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 \operatorname{ord}_E(f^*\Gamma)
   =\inf_{m\geq1}\frac{v_E(\mathcal O_X(\lceil-m\Gamma\rceil))}{m}.\] Additivity or arbitrary commutation with finite pullback is not available. Proposition 6.4 uses the correct inequality direction, and Remark 6.5 gives a strict example.

Proposition 6.7, pp. 26–27, requires \(E\) to dominate \(D\), compatible choices of the primes in absolute integral closures, and a boundary on the target dominating the asymptotic pullback. At the distinguished prime, put \(h=\operatorname{ord}_{D_\lambda}
(\operatorname{div}f+\pi_\lambda^*D)\). Membership in the source fractional module gives \(h\geq1\). If the ramification multiplicity is \(e\geq1\), then \[eh-1\geq h-1\geq0.\] This checks the final valuation inequality including its subtraction of one. At other primes the comparison is supplied by Proposition 6.4.

Proposition 6.9, p. 28, adds a fixed positive perturbation before taking approximations. It does not claim exact commutation of the asymptotic pullback with reduction. For effective \(\Gamma\), the possible support is controlled by a single ideal \(\mathcal O_X(-m\Gamma)\); the relevant valuation set is finite. Thus the choice of a common sufficiently divisible index is compatible with the finite collection of inequalities. The order is: fix \(\varepsilon>0\), choose the index, and then take almost all primes.

## The annihilator argument

The pure inclusion in Theorem 8.5, pp. 35–37, embeds each module \(M\) into \(M\otimes_RS\). The lower module comparison need not be injective after tensoring for the required kernel containment. Taking annihilators reverses the containment. The auxiliary-ideal extension uses intersections indexed by \(n\) and by elements \(f\in\mathfrak a^{\lceil tn\rceil}\cap R^{\circ,D}\). The proof separately invokes the generator argument in Lemma 8.3; it does not simply replace this indexing family by every element of an \(S\)-ideal without justification. Finally right continuity removes the positive boundary perturbation.

For the flat case, pp. 30–32, completion and intersection flatness justify the passage from annihilators to extended ideals. The completeness assumption in Lemma 7.5 is material: ordinary flatness alone does not justify commuting an arbitrary intersection with tensor product. Corollary 8.8 also retains disjointness of the components of \(E\). The non-\(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier extension in Theorem 8.10 uses the finite-generation hypotheses to construct the stated small models and affine open sets. Those minimal-model-program existence results were not independently reproved.

# Independent boundary calculations

## The nonflat example

For Example 7.8, p. 33, write \[R=\mathbb C[xy^2,xyz,xz^2]_{\mathfrak m},
 \quad S=\mathbb C[x,y,z]_{(x,y,z)},\quad
 \mathfrak mS=x(y,z)^2.\] The exponent polyhedron of this monomial ideal is described by \(a\geq1\), \(b+c\geq2\), and nonnegative coordinates. Applying the usual interior-shift criterion gives the inequalities \[a+1>1,\qquad b+c+2>2\] for a monomial \(x^ay^bz^c\) in its multiplier ideal. Equivalently, \(a\geq1\) and \(b+c\geq1\), giving \((xy,xz)S\).

An invariant monomial satisfies \(2a=b+c\). Under this relation the last conditions hold exactly for nonconstant invariant monomials; the contraction is \(\mathfrak m\). On the quadric cone the minimal resolution has discrepancy zero and the maximal ideal has order one along the exceptional curve, giving \(\mathcal J(X,\mathfrak m)=\mathfrak m\). In particular \(xy\) belongs to the multiplier ideal upstairs but not to \(\mathfrak mS\). This confirms the paper’s distinction between contraction and extension; it is not a counterexample to its pure-descent statement. The attached independent calculation checks the exponent inequalities on a bounded box; the unrestricted conclusion follows from the displayed inequalities, not from enumeration.

## The log-canonical limit

For Lemma 8.12, pp. 39–40, let \(a_1\geq-1\) be the discrepancy for the lc boundary, and let \(a_2+v(f)>-1\) encode membership in the other multiplier ideal. For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), \[(1-\varepsilon)a_1+\varepsilon a_2+\varepsilon v(f)>-1.\] This gives the required membership after adding \((1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{div}(f)\). Conversely, with the fixed \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier pair, the inequalities \(a_E+\varepsilon v_E(f)>-1\) for every positive \(\varepsilon\) imply \(a_E\geq-1\) by taking the limit. One fixed positive \(\varepsilon\) would not suffice. Exact rational arithmetic independently checked representative boundary values.

Theorem 8.13 retains its additional non-klt-locus condition; the general lc-descent question is not claimed solved. In the one-dimensional residual case a normal base is smooth, and domination by the locus forces the dimension bound used in the proof.
