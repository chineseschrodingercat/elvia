# Definitions, hypotheses, and dependencies

Equation reference: asym. Write \(V=|\Omega|\), \(S=\mathcal H^{d-1}(\partial\Omega)\) and \[R_\gamma^\#(\lambda)=\operatorname{Tr}(-\Delta_\Omega^\#-\lambda)_-^\gamma,\qquad
 L_{\gamma,d}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}
 {(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(\gamma+d/2+1)},\quad
 \tau_{\mathrm D}=-1,\quad\tau_{\mathrm N}=1.\] For the main asymptotic statement, \(d\ge2\), \(\gamma>0\) is fixed, and \(\Omega\) is a bounded open set with Lipschitz boundary. Theorem 1.1 asserts \[R_\gamma^\#(\lambda)
 =L_{\gamma,d}V\lambda^{\gamma+d/2}
 +\frac{\tau_\#}{4}L_{\gamma,d-1}S\lambda^{\gamma+(d-1)/2}
 +o(\lambda^{\gamma+(d-1)/2}).
 \] The strict counting convention at \(\gamma=0\) does not alter the integral formulas for \(\gamma>0\). Neumann zero eigenvalues must remain included.

Theorem 1.2 assumes convexity and gives bounds for every \(\lambda>0\). Its auxiliary parameter is \(\alpha=1\) for \(\gamma\ge1\), and any \(0<\alpha<\gamma\) for \(0<\gamma<1\). It does not claim the endpoint \(\alpha=\gamma\) in the latter range or uniformity as \(\gamma\downarrow0\). Constants depending on \(\gamma\), \(\alpha\), or the domain must not be read as uniform across those parameters.

The proof uses the local estimate in Theorem 2.1 and near-boundary bounds in Propositions 2.2–2.4; the \(\gamma=1\) Dirichlet result from the article’s reference [27]; Brown’s heat-trace theorem as stated in Theorem 4.1; the companion heat-trace estimate cited as [31]; and a Tauberian argument. The present review checks how these inputs are used, but does not independently certify all their original proofs. This qualification is material to the scope of the outcome.

# Verified reasoning and boundary checks

## Integration of the local remainder

In Section 3, the boundary-strip quantity is \[\Theta_\Omega=\sup_{s>0}s^{-1}
 |\{x\in\Omega:d_\Omega(x)\le s\}|.\] The layer-cake calculation in equation (16), PDF p. 26, gives \[\int_\Omega\min\{\lambda^{(1+\gamma)/2},d_\Omega^{-1-\gamma}\}\,dx
 \le (1+\gamma)\Theta_\Omega
 \int_{\lambda^{-1/2}}^\infty s^{-1-\gamma}\,ds
 =\frac{1+\gamma}{\gamma}\Theta_\Omega\lambda^{\gamma/2}.\] Multiplication by the pointwise prefactor \(\lambda^{(\gamma+d-1)/2}\) gives \(\lambda^{\gamma+(d-1)/2}\), exactly the required order. The integral requires \(\gamma>0\); its divergence at \(\gamma=0\) explains why Proposition 3.6 instead has a logarithm. No passage to the excluded endpoint is needed.

The Neumann proof splits according to whether \(r_{\mathrm{in}}(\Omega)\ge\lambda^{-1/2}\) (PDF pp. 26–27). This prevents a bulk estimate from being applied when the bulk region is empty. For convex domains, equation (18), pp. 28–29, uses disjoint \(\ell\)-balls, a covering by \(2\ell\)-balls, and bounds on inner and outer boundary layers. The comparison of \(\ell^d/|\Omega\cap B_\ell(x)|\) with the radius-\(4\ell\) expression is applied only at \(x\in\Omega\), where Lemma A.5 supplies the needed monotonicity. The interior layer is bounded by \(2\ell S\) and the exterior layer by \(C_d\ell S[1+(\ell/r_{\mathrm{in}})^{d-1}]\). These give the two terms in the stated convex Neumann estimate.

## The first-order Neumann boundary coefficient

On PDF pp. 31–32, put \(f=R_1^{\mathrm N}-R_1^{\mathrm D}\). The variational principle gives \(N_{\mathrm N}(\mu)\ge
N_{\mathrm D}(\mu)\), so \(f'(\mu)\ge0\) almost everywhere. Thus the positive measure needed for the Tauberian step is the derivative of this difference. Its transform is \[\int_0^\infty e^{-t\lambda}\,df(\lambda)
 =t^{-1}\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}e^{t\Delta_\Omega^{\mathrm N}}
              -\operatorname{Tr}e^{t\Delta_\Omega^{\mathrm D}}\bigr)
 \sim \frac{S}{2(4\pi)^{(d-1)/2}}t^{-(d+1)/2}.\] The resulting coefficient is \(\tfrac12 L_{1,d-1}S\lambda^{(d+1)/2}\). Adding the Dirichlet boundary coefficient \(-\tfrac14L_{1,d-1}S\) therefore produces \(+\tfrac14L_{1,d-1}S\), as required. Equation (19), p. 30, extends this to \(\gamma>1\) by a beta integral: for a single spectral value with \(a>0\), \(\gamma(\gamma-1)\int_0^a t^{\gamma-2}(a-t)\,dt=a^\gamma\). The normalization agrees with the definition of \(L_{\gamma,d}\).

## Interpolation down to every positive order

Equation reference: exponent. For the signed counting remainder \(f\) in Section 5, its normalized fractional primitive satisfies \[f^{(\gamma)}(\lambda)=
 \frac{L_{\gamma,d}V\lambda^{\gamma+d/2}
 +\tfrac{\tau_\#}{4}L_{\gamma,d-1}S\lambda^{\gamma+(d-1)/2}
 -R_\gamma^\#(\lambda)}{\Gamma(\gamma+1)}.\] Fix \(0<\kappa<\gamma<1\) and let \(\theta=(\gamma-\kappa)/(1-\kappa)\in(0,1)\). Proposition 7.1, applied using the fractional-integration semigroup property, yields equation (25): \[|f^{(\gamma)}(\lambda)|\le C
 \left(\sup_{\Lambda\le\lambda}|f^{(\kappa)}(\Lambda)|\right)^{1-\theta}
 \left(\sup_{\Lambda\le\lambda}|f^{(1)}(\Lambda)|\right)^\theta .\] The powers combine exactly because \[(1-\theta)\left(\kappa+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
 +\theta\left(1+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)
 =\gamma+\frac{d-1}{2}.
 \] The first supremum is \(O(\lambda^{\kappa+(d-1)/2})\) and the second is \(o(\lambda^{1+(d-1)/2})\). Local boundedness and positivity of these exponents justify retaining the little-\(o\) estimate after taking the supremum. This proves the claimed transfer conditional on the stated prerequisites.

The proof of Proposition 7.1 (PDF pp. 44–48) was followed through the semigroup identity, the real-valued mean-value argument of Lemma 7.2, the split at \(\Lambda_1\), the two competing powers of \(\Lambda-\Lambda_1\), and the discrete interpolation for orders greater than one. The equal-endpoint case in Lemma 7.2 is satisfied directly by taking \(\lambda_0=\lambda_1\); it need not use the intermediate density construction. No obstruction was found in the real-valued application used in Section 5.

## Quantified convex bounds and the zero mode

For \(0<\alpha<\gamma<1\), taking \(\kappa=(\gamma-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\) gives \(0<\kappa<\gamma\) and \(\theta=\alpha\). This accounts for the open range of \(\alpha\) and the Dirichlet exponent \(-\alpha/11\). In Section 4.2, the measure correction for Proposition 8.1 has exponent \(\nu=(d+1)/2\ge1\) and its Riesz order is \(\gamma-1\); hence its error is \(k^{-\gamma}\). Choosing \(k\) proportional to \(\log(r_{\mathrm{in}}\sqrt\lambda)\) with coefficient smaller than \(1/11\) yields the stated large-parameter logarithmic decay (PDF pp. 33–35). The small-parameter range is handled separately.

The Neumann factor \((r_{\mathrm{in}}\sqrt\lambda)^{1-d}\) is essential: \[S\lambda^{\gamma+(d-1)/2}
 (r_{\mathrm{in}}\sqrt\lambda)^{1-d}
 =Sr_{\mathrm{in}}^{1-d}\lambda^\gamma .\] It has precisely the power needed to accommodate the Neumann zero mode as \(\lambda\downarrow0\). Omitting it would give a different and generally untenable uniform claim; the article retains it.

# Independent computation, alternative readings, and outcome

For the unit square, all original geometric assumptions hold. Separation of variables gives the exact cancellation identity \[R_\gamma^{\mathrm N}(\lambda)-R_\gamma^{\mathrm D}(\lambda)
 =\lambda^\gamma+2\sum_{n\ge1}(\lambda-\pi^2n^2)_+^\gamma.\] The boundary prediction is \(2L_{\gamma,1}\lambda^{\gamma+1/2}\) since \(S=4\). An independently written Python calculation, using no author code, gave the following ratios of the exact finite spectral sum to that prediction: \[\begin{array}{c|cc}
\gamma&\lambda=10^4&\lambda=10^6\\ \hline
0.1&0.993429121&1.000347518\\
0.5&0.999054013&1.000029589\\
1.0&0.999960462&1.000000699
\end{array}\] This checks normalization and the zero mode on a valid example. Finite floating-point results do not prove a uniform asymptotic theorem. SymPy simplifications of [exponent] (#exponent), the parameter conversion for \(\alpha\), and the small-energy exponent each returned exactly zero residual.

Apparent endpoint objections are resolved by the explicit strict inequalities on \(\gamma\), \(\kappa\), and \(\alpha\); objections based on a signed second-order counting remainder do not apply to the nonnegative comparison measure actually used.
