# Confirmed issue: the openness assertion in Lemma 2.1

Location and quantifiers. At the bottom of p. 9 and top of p. 10, put \(P=\operatorname{Pic}(X)\otimes\mathbb C\), \(V=\Lambda\otimes\mathbb C\) and \(w=(W,-)|_P\). The proof asserts that \[S=\{g\in O(V):\ker((g\delta,-)|_P)\ne\ker w\}\] is Zariski open. Here \(X\) is projective, \(\operatorname{rk}\operatorname{Pic}(X)\ge2\), \(W^2<0\), and \(\delta^2=2-2n\ne0\) for \(n\ge2\). By the Hodge index signature, \(P\) is nondegenerate and \(w\ne0\).

## Counterevidence under these hypotheses

Write \(d_0=\delta^2\). The nondegenerate space \(P^\perp\) has complex dimension at least two. Choose \(v\in P^\perp\) with \(v^2=d_0\) and \(g_0\in O(V)\) with \(g_0\delta=v\). Then the restricted functional is zero: \(\ker((v,-)|_P)=P\ne\ker w\), so \(g_0\in S\).

Choose an analytic function \(c(z)\) near zero such that \(c(0)=1\) and \(c(z)^2=1-z^2W^2/d_0\). On the nondegenerate plane spanned by \(v,W\), define \[R(z)v=c(z)v+zW,\qquad
 R(z)W=-z\frac{W^2}{d_0}v+c(z)W,\] and extend \(R(z)\) by the identity on its orthogonal complement. The displayed identity for \(c(z)\) proves directly that \(R(z)\) is orthogonal. Thus \(g(z)=R(z)g_0\) is a curve in \(O(V)\) with \(g(0)=g_0\). For every sufficiently small nonzero \(z\), \[(g(z)\delta,-)|_P=z\,w,\qquad
 \ker((g(z)\delta,-)|_P)=\ker w.\] Consequently \(g(z)\notin S\). The set \(S\) is not even analytically open at \(g_0\), and hence is not Zariski open. This tests the zero-functional boundary that is omitted in the printed openness justification.

## Complete repair and downstream effect

Use instead \[U=\{g\in O(V):((g\delta,-)|_P)\wedge w\ne0\}.\] This is a Zariski open set, defined by nonvanishing of at least one two-by-two minor. It is nonempty: choose \(p\in P\) for which \((p,-)|_P\) is independent of \(w\), then choose \(v'\in P^\perp\) with \((v')^2=d_0-p^2\). Over \(\mathbb C\) such a vector exists. The nonzero-norm vector \(p+v'\) is the image of \(\delta\) under an orthogonal transformation, which belongs to \(U\).

The integral orthogonal group is Zariski dense as used in the paper, so choose \(g\in O(\Lambda)\cap U\). The rational hyperplane \(g(\delta)^\perp\cap\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb Q\) is not contained in \(W^\perp\). Choose a rational vector there with nonzero pairing with \(W\), clear denominators and take its primitive part \(A\). Both lattice intersections are saturated, so \(A\) remains in the intersection. The lattice \(g(\delta)^\perp\) is a unimodular K3 lattice, hence a primitive vector there has divisibility one in \(\Lambda\). This proves exactly \((A,W)\ne0\) and \(\operatorname{div}(A)=1\).

Impact and repair limit. The false openness assertion is the stated justification for applying integral orthogonal-group density to obtain the divisibility-one class used in Proposition 2.2. That class feeds the Mukai construction in Section 2.2, the wall-avoidance argument of Proposition 2.4, and Theorem 0.3. This is a Major error in a core proof step, even though the nonempty open subset \(U\) above supplies a complete repair. The corrected argument preserves the later constants, Brauer classes and Fourier–Mukai construction. No counterexample to Lemma 2.1 or the derived-equivalence theorem is asserted; integral-group density and global Torelli remain imported inputs.

# Definitions, moduli components and wall reduction

The paper works over \(\mathbb C\), with projective varieties in the main theorems and possibly nonprojective intermediate marked manifolds. Theorem 0.3 quantifies over every Brauer class and gives the naturally corresponding class on a birational partner. Theorem 0.2 is its zero Brauer-class case. For \(n=1\), birational K3 surfaces are isomorphic. For Picard rank one, the absence of a birational wall gives the same trivial case; the construction in Section 2 therefore legitimately assumes an algebraic negative wall class and Picard rank at least two.

## A moduli component check

The space \(\mathcal M_\phi\) requires the rational Hodge isometry to send some Kähler class to a Kähler class. Equality of periods alone is not sufficient. Lemma 1.1 uses the componentwise surjectivity in Markman’s Lemma 5.7 [Markman]. Lemma 1.2 can be justified without assuming that every path lifts merely because a projection is surjective.

Indeed, let \(C,C'\) be the components containing its two quadruples. Their first projections are the same marked-manifold component. Choose a point of Picard rank zero in that component and use surjectivity to find a quadruple over it in each of \(C,C'\). The second projections lie in the same component because the original second factor is the same. Their periods are prescribed by \(\phi\). At Picard rank zero the marked partner of this period in that component is unique by the relevant Torelli statement; the Kähler compatibility is automatic for these quadruples. Thus the two quadruples coincide, so \(C=C'\). Markman’s proof also constructs appropriate generic twistor paths. The manuscript’s abbreviated path argument is not counted as a separate confirmed defect.

## Brauer representatives and walls

For a projective hyper-Kähler manifold, the rational Picard and transcendental spaces split orthogonally. The exponential-sequence description used in (2)–(3) therefore lets a Brauer class be written \([-B/d]\) with integral \(B\in T(X)\) and \(d>0\). Since \(T(X)_\mathbb R\) has two positive directions, a positive integral vector \(v\) exists. Replacing \(B\) by \(B+dNv\) for sufficiently large integer \(N\) preserves the Brauer class and makes \(B^2=2e>0\). Thus the positivity assumption on p. 9 does not restrict the theorem.

The birational Kähler cone is convex within the positive cone. A compact segment between interior points meets finitely many walls by local finiteness; a small perturbation avoids wall intersections. It therefore suffices to treat adjacent chambers. If \(\phi^{-1}W\) is not proportional to an MBM class, its hyperplane does not coincide with a wall on the partner. On a small open part of the separating facet, local finiteness allows a point avoiding those walls. A partner Kähler chamber then meets the images of both adjacent chambers. Its birational model \(Y''\) supplies the two quadruples used in Section 1.6. Lemma 1.2 keeps both in the initial component.

Projectivity is preserved here because the rational Hodge isometry transports a positive rational \((1,1)\) class. The two transported twisted equivalences can therefore be composed. The Brauer classes on \(Y''\) agree because their defining rational classes and marking agree. Inverting one equivalence uses the dual kernel with the appropriate shift; this does not obstruct existence of the composition.

# Divisibility and the Mukai-vector construction

## Proposition 2.2, p. 10

Choose \(\omega\in W^\perp\cap\operatorname{Pic}(X)\) of positive square. The repaired Lemma 2.1 supplies \(A\) with \((A,W)=C_1>0\) and divisibility one; \(A,\omega\) are independent. Choose integral \(\mu,\nu\) such that \[(A,\mu)=1,\quad b=(\omega,\mu)\ne0,\qquad
 (A,\nu)=0,\quad c=(\omega,\nu)\ne0.\] Existence of \(\nu\) follows from independence over \(\mathbb Q\); adjusting \(\mu\) by a multiple of \(\nu\) ensures \(b\ne0\).

The printed prime argument is valid for a sufficiently large prime \(q=|1+tb|>|c|\): a common divisor of \(q\) and \(tc\) divides \(c\) since \(\gcd(q,t)=1\). The final divisibility by \(c\) is not an independent assumption. There is also an elementary infinite choice: take \(t=|c|j\), \(j>0\). Then \(\gcd(1+tb,tc)=1\), so \(\operatorname{div}(A+t\omega)=1\), while its square tends to infinity. This also applies to choosing a suitable \(t\) in \(D+4gtdB\).

## Section 2.2, pp. 10–12

Set \[q=4td^2,\quad r=gq^2=16gt^2d^4,\quad
 s=1+4gt^2d^4(n-1)+16gt^2d^2e,\quad H^2=2gs.\] Then \(m=\epsilon q\), \(\epsilon=\pm1\), and \(m^2H^2=2rs\), so \((r,mH,s)\) is isotropic. Every prime dividing \(r\) divides \(2gtd\); the nonconstant terms of \(s\) are divisible by that prime. Hence \(\gcd(r,s)=1\), giving primitivity and the fine-moduli condition.

The classes \(H-2gtd^2\delta\) and \(D+4gtdB\) have the same square: \[2gs+(2gtd^2)^2(2-2n)=2g+32g^2t^2d^2e.\] Both have divisibility one. The primitive polarization \(H\) has divisibility one in the unimodular K3 lattice; the other class was chosen with that property. The cited primitive-lattice and monodromy criterion therefore gives the parallel transport in (10), allowing the orientation sign \(\epsilon\).

For the local-freeness criterion, divide the rank and first Chern class by their common factor \(q\). The primitive pair has rank \(gq\) and first Chern class \(\epsilon H\). Since \(g>1\), \[gq\nmid H^2/2+1=gs+1.\] Rank one of the Picard group makes \(H\) general in the sense used in Yoshioka’s Lemma 1.2(2) [Yoshioka]. That lemma, whose statement and proof were checked, gives both slope stability and local freeness. Thus the actual bundles satisfy the required stability hypothesis.

Yoshioka’s Theorem 2.2 gives primitive isotropic dual fibers. His Appendix A gives \(\widehat H^2=H^2\) here because \(\gcd(r,s)=1\). Writing their vector as \((r,k\widehat H,\widehat s)\), isotropy yields \(\widehat s=s(k/q)^2\in\mathbb Z\). As \(\gcd(s,q)=1\), prime valuations imply \(q\mid k\). Write \(k=q\ell\). Primitivity forces \(\gcd(r,\ell)=1\), since a prime dividing both divides all three entries. Consequently \[\gcd(r,k)=q,\qquad \frac r{\gcd(r,qk)}=g.\] These identities, including either sign of \(k\), are the exact arithmetic input for Proposition 2.4.

# Brauer sign and exclusion of a second wall

## The prescribed twist, p. 12

The normalized Chern-class formula, Markman’s (7.11) [Markman], gives \[\frac{a_{S^{[n]}}}{\operatorname{rk}U^{[n]}}
       =\frac{\epsilon H}{4gtd^2}-\frac\delta2 .\] Using \(\rho(H-2gtd^2\delta)=\epsilon(D+4gtdB)\), both orientation signs give \[\rho\left(\frac{\epsilon H}{4gtd^2}-\frac\delta2\right)
   =\frac Bd+\frac{D}{4gtd^2}
      +\frac{\epsilon-1}{2}\rho(\delta).\] The second summand is in \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)_\mathbb Q\) and the last is integral for both \(\epsilon=1\) and \(\epsilon=-1\). The induced target twist is therefore \([-B/d]=\alpha\). For a product kernel, the convention is source twist with a minus sign and target twist with a plus sign in its twisting class. The minus sign in the paper’s definition \(\alpha_E=-[c_1(E)/\operatorname{rk}E]\) is consistent with the displayed source/target equivalence. No factor of two remains unaccounted for.

## Proposition 2.4, pp. 12–13

Suppose \(\phi^{-1}W=(b/a)W'\) for coprime nonzero integers \(a,b\) and a primitive MBM class \(W'\). If \(u=-W^2\) and \(v=-(W')^2\), isometry gives \(ub^2=va^2\). Coprimality implies \(a^2\mid u\), \(b^2\mid v\), whence \[a^2<C_0,\qquad b^2<C_0\] by the MBM square bound in Theorem 1.5. Transport to the base point preserves the lattice and the deformation-invariant MBM property. Write \(\rho^{-1}W=W_{\rm K3}+\lambda\delta\).

The block decomposition (5) implies \(\phi_U^{-1}(aW_{\rm K3})\) is integral. In Buskin’s Chern-character calculation [Buskin], the only possibly nonintegral mixed contribution is the rank denominator: for \(c_1(U)=mH+k\widehat H\) it is proportional to \((\gamma,H)mk/r\). The K3 intersection form is even, so the remaining Chern-character contribution is integral. Thus integrality requires \[\frac r{\gcd(r,mk)}\mid (H,aW_{\rm K3}).\] Here \(m=\epsilon q\) and the calculation on the preceding page makes the divisor exactly \(g\). The sign convention normalizing the rational Hodge isometry does not affect this integral subgroup.

Equation (10) and \((B,W)=0\) now give \[(H,aW_{\rm K3})=\epsilon aC_1+
     2gtd^2a\,(\rho(\delta),W),
 \qquad g\mid aC_1.\] Since \(a\ne0\), this implies \(g\le |a|C_1\le a^2C_1<C_0C_1\), contradicting \(g>C_0C_1\). This also covers negative \(a\). The proof establishes exactly the wall-avoidance condition needed in Section 1.6. The main result is not refuted by the local openness finding.
