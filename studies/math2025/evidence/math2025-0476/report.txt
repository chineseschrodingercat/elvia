Abstract: The acquired manuscript’s free-cokernel characterization of interval decomposability passes the detailed checks described here. Its proof uses the cokernels of composite maps in an essential way; checking only neighboring arrows is insufficient. Exact integer examples verify both the obstruction and the intended compatible-basis construction. The printed matrix algorithm and a kernel-filtration formula contain endpoint indexing errors with small counterexamples. The surrounding high-level construction supports direct repairs. The assessment is Major for the acquired arXiv version: the literal algorithm fails its central output guarantee on a legal input. The free-cokernel criterion is not refuted, and no finding is transferred to the journal-final text.

The acquired file has 33 pages. Its associated journal DOI is 10.1007/s10208-025-09732-8, but the reviewed bytes are the arXiv manuscript, not a verified journal-final copy. Theorem 1, PDF pp. 2 and 10, concerns a finite totally ordered persistence module \(f:\{0,\ldots,m\}\to R\text{-Mod}\), where \(R\) is a PID and each \(f_a\) is free and finitely generated. It asserts \[f\text{ decomposes into }R\text{-intervals}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \operatorname{coker}f(a\le b)\text{ is free for every }a\le b.\] The interval maps inside their support are identities. The assertion does not extend as stated to arbitrary rings, pointwise torsion modules, or multi-parameter indexing.

The later convention \(f_0=f_m=0\), explicitly imposed on PDF p. 9, is obtained by adjoining zero endpoints and relabeling. This does not change the obstruction: new maps from zero have the already free pointwise modules as cokernels, and maps into zero have zero cokernel. The half-open interval convention is understood with these terminal indices.

# The main proof chain

Necessity, Lemma 2 on PDF p. 10, follows directly from an interval basis: at a target index the image is spanned by the intervals covering both indices, and the remaining interval basis elements form a free complement.

For sufficiency the key issue is primitivity. A submodule \(U\) of a finite free PID module \(V\) has a complement exactly when \(V/U\) is torsion-free, equivalently when \[rz\in U,\quad r\ne0,\quad z\in V
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z\in U .\] Finite generation and the PID hypothesis justify replacing torsion-free by free. Lemma 3, PDF p. 11, uses these conditions correctly. Intersections of primitive submodules remain primitive by this saturation test, and if \(L\subset H\subset V\) with \(L\) primitive in \(V\), then \(H/L\subset V/L\) is free. These are the needed contents of Lemmas 4–5, PDF pp. 12–13.

For \(x\le a\le y\), set \[K=\ker f(a\le y),\qquad I=\operatorname{im}f(x\le a).\] The natural map induced by \(f(a\le y)\) gives \[f_a/(K+I)
 \cong \operatorname{im}f(a\le y)/\operatorname{im}f(x\le y)
 \ \subset\ f_y/\operatorname{im}f(x\le y).\] The final module is free by the hypothesis on the composite \(f(x\le y)\). Therefore the first quotient is free, and \(K+I\) has a complement. This verifies the substantive step of Theorem 7, PDF p. 12. It avoids the false general assertion that the sum of two primitive submodules is necessarily primitive.

To check the construction in Algorithm 1 and Theorem 8, PDF pp. 13–14, write \(J=\operatorname{im}f(i-1\le i)\) and \(K_j=\ker f(i\le j)\). The propagated nonzero elements of the previous basis form a basis of \(J\), adapted to \(J\cap K_j\): the relevant preimage is \(\ker f(i-1\le j)\). The algorithm adds a basis of a complement of \[(J+K_{j-1})\cap K_j\] inside \(K_j\). The preceding primitivity results guarantee existence. Since \[(J+K_{j-1})\cap K_j=(J\cap K_j)+K_{j-1},\] the added vectors extend a basis of \(J+K_{j-1}\) to one of \(J+K_j\), preserving the filtration adaptation. At \(j=m\), \(K_m=f_i\). Each adjacent map then sends a basis vector either to zero or to a distinct next-node basis vector. Following those vectors yields the interval summands. This verifies the main sufficiency mechanism.

The conditional uniqueness proof on PDF p. 9 also passes the check. Tensoring two interval decompositions with \(\operatorname{Frac}(R)\) preserves their interval multisets. Uniqueness over the fraction field therefore gives uniqueness of that multiset over \(R\). It does not assert that the fraction-field barcode classifies every integral persistence module before decomposability has been shown.

# Exact tests of the hypotheses

Consider the integer module, with zero endpoints, \[0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z
 \xrightarrow{A}\mathbb Z^2
 \xrightarrow{B}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0,\qquad
 A=\binom10,\quad B=(2\ \ 1).\] The adjacent cokernels are \(\mathbb Z\) and zero, but \(BA=[2]\) has cokernel \(\mathbb Z/2\). Thus the module does not decompose into integral intervals. At the middle node \[\operatorname{im}A=\mathbb Ze_1,\qquad \ker B=\mathbb Z(1,-2),\] and their sum has index two. This is precisely the obstruction excluded by the composite-cokernel assumption. It is a check supporting the theorem, not a counterexample to it.

The obstruction is also visible after changing coefficients. For characteristic different from two the barcode is \([1,4)\) together with \([2,3)\). In characteristic two it is \([1,3)\) together with \([2,4)\). Pointwise free integer modules alone therefore do not imply a field-independent barcode.

For a positive exact test, replace \(B\) by \((1\ \ 1)\). The middle basis \(e_1,e_2-e_1\) is unimodular. The first map sends \(1\) to its first vector, and the second sends those two vectors to \(1,0\), respectively. Hence the decomposition is \(I_{\mathbb Z}^{1,4}\oplus I_{\mathbb Z}^{2,3}\).

A second exact test uses \[0\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^2
 \xrightarrow{(2\ \ 3)}\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.\] Here \[(2\ \ 3)
 \begin{pmatrix}-1&3\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}
 =(1\ \ 0),\qquad
 \det\begin{pmatrix}-1&3\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}=-1.\] Thus the domain basis \((-1,1),(3,-2)\) gives intervals \([1,3)\) and \([1,2)\). The controlling invariant is the unit Smith divisor \(\gcd(2,3)=1\), not the first unreduced coefficient.

# Corrections to the explicit matrix construction

Problem 1 (Major: Algorithm 2, PDF p. 15).  The printed loop endpoints omit a potentially nonzero final node and the final death block.

Line 3 iterates \(i=1,\ldots,m-2\); line 5 iterates \(j=i+1,\ldots,m-1\). But line 11 concatenates the blocks through \(Y_i^m\), which that inner loop has not constructed. The output also includes the initialized, uncomputed \(\beta_{m-1}\).

Take \(m=2\) and \(f_0=f_2=0,\ f_1=R\). Every required cokernel is free and the module is \(I_R^{1,2}\). The outer loop is empty, so the printed algorithm returns \(\beta_1=\varnothing\), which is not a basis of \(R\). This disproves the literal output guarantee without any numerical or implementation assumptions.

The natural corrected ranges are \[i=1,\ldots,m-1,\qquad j=i+1,\ldots,m .\] They already occur in Algorithm 1, PDF p. 13, and the proof of Lemma 6, PDF pp. 15–16, explicitly uses the terminal blocks. The known zero maps from \(f_0\) and into \(f_m\) supply the endpoint operations. With these ranges the minimal example forms \(Y_1^2=[1]\), giving the required basis. The associated prose, coherence convention and loop counts should be aligned as well; the asymptotic operation count is unchanged.

Problem 2 (Major: Proposition 5, PDF p. 28).  The displayed kernel-block formula has its target index shifted by one and omits the first kernel block.

The notation \(F_a\) represents \(f(a\le a+1)\). The proposition defines \[X^k=T_a^+\cdots T_{k-1}^+T_k^-\] but claims that \([X^{a+1}|\cdots|X^k]\) spans \(\ker f(a\le k)\). The factor \(T_k^-\) instead concerns the product \(F_k\cdots F_a\), whose target is \(k+1\).

For an exact witness set \(F_a=[0]\), \(F_{a+1}=[1]\). Then \(T_a^+\) has no columns, so the printed \(X^{a+1}\) has no columns, whereas \(\ker F_a=R\). This can be embedded in the permitted finite module with \(m=4\), \(a=1\), \(F_1=0,F_2=1,F_3=0\), and zero endpoints. All of its structure-map cokernels are free.

A consistent death-index convention is \[X^{a+1}=T_a^-,\qquad
 X^j=T_a^+\cdots T_{j-2}^+T_{j-1}^-,
 \quad a+2\le j\le m .\] Then \([X^{a+1}|\cdots|X^b]\) gives the kernel through target \(b\). To see the intended induction, at each Smith step \(T_k^-\) spans the kernel of the remaining restricted product, while \(T_k^+\) carries its complementary coordinates to the next step. The nonzero Smith diagonal entries give an injective map on that complement over the domain \(R\). Successive unimodular changes therefore split off exactly the new kernel blocks. The first block is necessarily \(T_a^-\). The block pattern in Figure 3, on the same page, is consistent with retaining that initial block.

The assessment of both construction errors is Major. The abstract advertises an algorithm that decides decomposability and returns an interval decomposition. Algorithm 2’s output promise and Theorem 9 on PDF p. 17 make that guarantee explicit. Problem 1 refutes it on a legal input, while Algorithm 2, line 4, explicitly uses Proposition 5 for the kernel filtration affected by Problem 2. Thus the errors damage a central constructive claim, even though short index repairs are available.

The surviving results bound this assessment. Algorithm 1 supplies the required endpoints, the main complement proof is unaffected by these counterexamples, and the corrected indexing has the stated kernel meaning. Neither the free-cokernel criterion nor every result of the paper has been refuted. No general implementation was executed, and this review does not certify every detail of the lattice-based rejection proof.

# Applications, complexity, and remaining limits

Theorem 4, PDF pp. 6–7, assumes that both \(H_k(K_a;\mathbb Z)\) and \(H_{k-1}(K_a;\mathbb Z)\) are free and finitely generated at every index. The exact sequence \[0\longrightarrow\operatorname{coker}\phi_a^b
 \longrightarrow H_k(K_b,K_a;\mathbb Z)
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{im}\partial\longrightarrow0\] then splits because its last term is a subgroup of a free abelian group. This verifies the stated equivalence between free cokernels and free relative homology under those assumptions. The field-independence equivalence also invokes the cited prior result; that external proof was not independently reaudited here.

The universal-cycle discussion on PDF pp. 8–9 correctly retains torsion checks in both dimensions. The lower-dimensional condition removes the \(\operatorname{Tor}\) contribution in the universal coefficient theorem, permitting the natural identification of integer homology tensored with a field and field homology. It should not be dropped in applications.

Proposition 2, PDF p. 17, counts \(O(m^2)\) matrix multiplications/Smith factorizations and \(O(m)\) inversions, expressed as \[O\!\left(m\max\{mM_d,mS_d,R_d\}\right).\] Theorem 10, PDF p. 18, assumes that all structure-map matrices have already been computed before stating the decision bound \(O(m^2S_d)\). These are the operation-level claims checked here. An independent analysis of integer coefficient bit lengths or polynomial degrees throughout all intermediate basis changes has not been completed; no counterexample to the intended polynomial-time claim is inferred from that limitation.
