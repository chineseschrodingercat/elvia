Abstract: The acquired manuscript’s scalarization, Hadamard-space recognition, and maximal-cell interior reduction pass the bounded checks given below. The general boundary construction, however, uses a false assertion that a geodesic between two incident cubes must cross their common face. Four unit squares in the Euclidean plane give an exact counterexample, including an incorrect directional derivative. This invalidates the stated construction of the derivative functions used by the boundary semidefinite programs. A separate distinction between bicombing convexity and convexity along every geodesic requires a qualification in an auxiliary attainment proof. The overall assessment is Major for the acquired arXiv version, with the scope of each conclusion specified below.

Author: AI-assisted technical review

Title: Weighted-mean recognition and the geometry of cell boundaries

# Source, hypotheses, and scope

We review A. Goodwin, A. S. Lewis, G. López-Acedo, and A. Nicolae, Recognizing weighted means in geodesic spaces, arXiv:2406.03913v1, submitted 6 June 2024, with title-page date 7 June 2024 [paper]. The acquired PDF has 39 pages. The associated journal DOI is 10.1007/s10208-025-09733-7. The reviewed bytes are the arXiv manuscript; no identity with, or persistence of these findings in, the journal-final version is asserted. Page numbers below are one-based PDF pages.

The set \(A\) is nonempty and finite, and \[\Delta_A=\{w\in\mathbb R^A:w_a\ge0,\ \sum_a w_a=1\}.\] Zero weights are permitted. An \(A\)-minimal point has no competitor strictly closer to every \(a\in A\). This differs from the Pareto condition, which forbids a simultaneous nonincrease with at least one strict decrease, and from strong minimality, which forbids every distinct simultaneous nonincrease. Theorem 6.1, PDF pp. 15–16, equates \(p\)-means for all \(p\ge1\) with \(A\)-minimal points in a convex metric space. Corollary 6.5 strengthens this to the Pareto and strong-minimality properties in a Hadamard space.

The detailed checks cover these distinctions, the finite-dimensional duality step, the angle and Euclidean tangent criteria, the relative-interior algorithm, and the general boundary construction. The proof of general primal attainment is also tested at its convexity step. All calculations below are exact symbolic arguments. No author program or semidefinite solver was executed. The external geodesic-complexity theorem, a bit-complexity bound for exact SDP decisions, and the numerical sampling experiments were not independently certified. The acquisition record reports no standalone supplement found in its bounded search; that record does not establish that no additional material exists.

# Checks supporting the principal recognition results

Assumption 3.1 on PDF p. 8 gives strict primal feasibility and dual feasibility with \(c\ne0\). In Theorem 3.4, PDF pp. 10–11, the latter condition bounds the perturbation value function below by a finite affine function. Strict feasibility makes it finite near the perturbation \(b\). Metric convexity and the convexity of \(t\mapsto t^p\), \(p\ge1\), give convexity of the value function. A subgradient therefore exists at \(b\); variations in the slack and in \(y\) imply \[\bar w\ge0,\qquad G^*\bar w=c.\] In particular \(\bar w\ne0\), so normalization is legitimate.

For an \(A\)-minimal point \(\bar x\), the intersecting-balls problem with radii \(d_A(\bar x)\) already has the optimum \((\bar x,0)\). The dual certificate in Example 5.4, PDF p. 14, thus establishes the scalarization in Theorem 6.1 without invoking the separate general primal-attainment theorem. In the Hadamard setting, the variance inequality on PDF p. 16, \[\sum_a w_a\bigl(d(a,x)^2-d(a,\bar x)^2\bigr)
 \ge d(x,\bar x)^2,\] excludes every distinct simultaneous nonincrease. This remains valid when some weights vanish.

The angle criterion, Proposition 6.8 on PDF pp. 17–18, also passes the check. A common strict distance decrease gives a strictly acute angle to each point of \(A\). Conversely, minimality along a sequence on a fixed outgoing geodesic supplies an active point of \(A\); finiteness permits a subsequence with the same active point, and first variation gives an angle at least \(\pi/2\). The finiteness hypothesis is used substantively.

When the tangent cone is Euclidean, Theorem 7.12 on PDF p. 23 identifies the mean deficit with \[\operatorname{dist}\left(0,\operatorname{conv}\{v_a:a\in A\}\right),
 \qquad v_a=([\bar x,a],d(\bar x,a)).\] If \(q\) is the closest point of this convex hull to zero and \(q\ne0\), the projection inequality gives \(\langle q,v_a\rangle\ge |q|^2>0\) for every \(a\). First variation then yields a common decreasing direction. If \(q=0\), the convex-combination weights give the certificate. This uses a Euclidean tangent cone, not an arbitrary CAT(0) cone.

Theorem 9.3, PDF pp. 28–29, and Algorithm 9.5, PDF p. 30, require a point in the relative interior of a maximal cell. Its Euclidean neighborhood justifies contracting the geodesics into that cell and extending their initial vectors in its ambient space. The reciprocal contraction factors used to form intermediate weights cancel the factors used in transferring the certificate back. Later bisections preserve already obtained strict distance decreases by convexity.

For an exact elementary check, let \(X=C=[0,1]^2\) and \[A=\{(1/4,1/2),(3/4,1/2)\}.\] At \(\bar x=(1/2,1/2)\), the projection gives zero deficit and weights \((1/2,1/2)\). At \(\bar x=(1/2,3/4)\), it gives \(z=(1/2,1/2)\), deficit \(1/4\), and \[d(z,a)=1/4<\sqrt2/4=d(\bar x,a)\qquad(a\in A).\] Thus \(z\) is the claimed negative certificate. A positive numerical tolerance in the algorithm gives an approximate declaration, rather than an exact positive certificate.

# The general cell-boundary construction

Problem 1 (Major: the common-face distance formula).  On PDF p. 35, Section 11 chooses a cell \(C\) incident to \(\bar x\) and a cell \(Q_a\) containing the initial segment \([\bar x,x_a]\) of the geodesic toward \(a\). It sets \(F_a=C\cap Q_a\) and asserts that every geodesic from \(x_a\) to \(y\in C\) passes through \(F_a\). The resulting formula is \[d(x_a,y)=\min_{x\in F_a}
       \bigl(|x_a-x|_{Q_a}+|x-y|_C\bigr).                 \tag{1}\] The stated cubical hypotheses do not imply this assertion.

Take \(X=[-1,1]^2\), subdivided into four unit squares and their faces, with its Euclidean intrinsic metric. This is a finite, complete CAT(0) cubical complex satisfying the definition on PDF p. 27. Put \[\bar x=0,\quad Q_a=[-1,0]^2,\quad C=[0,1]^2,\quad
 a=x_a=(-1/2,-1/2),\quad y=(1/2,1/4).\] The points \(a,y\) are in the relative interiors of the two maximal cells; the geodesic \([\bar x,a]\) is a single nontrivial segment in \(Q_a\). Their common face is just \(\{0\}\). The actual straight-line distance is \[d(x_a,y)=\sqrt{1+(3/4)^2}=\frac54,\] whereas the right-hand side of (1) equals \[\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt5}{4}>\frac54.\] The strict inequality also follows from strictness in the Euclidean triangle inequality: the segment does not contain zero. It passes through a third square. This example satisfies the additional restriction to maximal cells used later in Section 11.

The discrepancy persists at the derivative used by the algorithm. For \(u=e_1\in T_C(0)\), \[d_a(tu)=\sqrt{(t+1/2)^2+1/4},\qquad
 d'_{a,C}(0;u)=\frac1{\sqrt2}.\] The expression in (1) instead becomes \(\sqrt2/2+t\), whose right derivative is \(1\). Shortening the initial segment toward \(a\) does not fix the directional mismatch. Scaling the finite-distance example toward zero also preserves its strict inequality, so a merely local interpretation is insufficient.

The dependency is material. Page 35 uses (1) to construct a semidefinite representation of \(d_{x_a,C}\), then its subdifferential and support function, identified with the genuine directional derivative \(f_{a,C}=d'_{a,C}(\bar x;\cdot)\). The recognition programs \[(P_C)\qquad \inf_u\max_{a\in A}f_{a,C}(u)\] and the weight-certificate sets \(V_C\) on PDF pp. 36–37 depend on those functions. The counterexample therefore invalidates the given general construction supporting an algorithm advertised in the abstract. This is the basis for the Major assessment.

Several limits are essential. The example does not establish an incorrect final recognition decision: both derivatives in the displayed direction are positive. It does not show that a different semidefinite representation is impossible. If the genuine derivatives are supplied, the conditional convexity argument for common descent on PDF p. 36 remains meaningful. The interior method has a separate justification and is unaffected.

Formula (1) is valid under the additional hypothesis that the relevant geodesic crosses \(F_a\): the triangle inequality gives one bound, and splitting that geodesic at its crossing gives the other. It also works in a tripod. With three unit arms and \(A\) their endpoints, the derivative along arm \(i\), at speed \(t\), is \(-t\) for its own endpoint and \(t\) for the others. The weighted squared-distance objective along that arm is \[1+2(1-2w_i)t+t^2.\] The common vertex is a barycenter exactly when \(w_i\le1/2\) for all \(i\), and equal weights provide a certificate. Thus the construction succeeds in some branching cases. A repair for arbitrary complexes must account for paths through other cells and prove that the resulting derivative representation has the claimed computational properties. Those properties are not established by the restricted correction above.

# A qualification in the auxiliary attainment proof

Problem 2 (Minor: two notions of geodesic convexity).  PDF p. 11 defines a convex set as one containing every geodesic between its points. Theorem 4.3 on PDF pp. 12–13 assumes reflexivity for closed bounded convex sets and a conical bicombing. Its proof shows only that each set \(F_k\) contains the selected bicombing geodesic between its points, then invokes reflexivity. These convexity notions need not coincide.

Let \(X=(\mathbb R^2,\|\cdot\|_\infty)\), \(A=\{0\}\), \(p=1\), \(Y=\mathbb R\), \(Gy=y\), \(c=1\), and \(b=0\). Strict and dual feasibility hold with \(\hat x=0,\hat y=1,\hat w=1\). The space is proper, hence satisfies the stated nested-intersection property, and linear interpolation is a conical bicombing. The primal optimum is \(\nu=0\). For \(r=1/k\), the proof’s set is \[F_k=[-r,r]^2.\] Both \((-r,r)\) and \((r,r)\) lie in \(F_k\), but the curve \[\gamma(s)=\bigl(-r+2rs,\ r+2r\min(s,1-s)\bigr),
 \qquad 0\le s\le1,\] is a geodesic with midpoint \((0,2r)\notin F_k\). Indeed its first-coordinate difference is \(2r|s-t|\), and the second-coordinate difference is no larger, so \(\|\gamma(s)-\gamma(t)\|_\infty=2r|s-t|\). Thus the intermediate assertion of all-geodesics convexity is false even in an admissible finite-dimensional example.

This demonstrates a gap in the stated general argument, not nonattainment: the optimum in this example is attained. In a uniquely geodesic space with the stated bicombing, the two notions coincide and the proof goes through. Alternatively, one can assume the nested-intersection property for sets convex along that chosen bicombing. These are verified sufficient qualifications, not a proof that the original broader hypotheses already imply the needed property. Hadamard spaces present no such ambiguity, and the principal recognition theorem does not depend on general primal attainment.

The overall conclusion is consequently specific: the abstract’s general boundary SDP construction needs a substantive replacement for its geometric step, while the inspected main recognition criteria and maximal-cell interior mechanism retain their support. The auxiliary attainment argument needs a narrower convexity convention or hypothesis. Neither finding is an assertion that every result of the paper fails, and neither has been transferred to an unexamined journal-final version.
