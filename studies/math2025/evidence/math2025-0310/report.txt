Title: Initial scientific review
Constraint maps with free boundaries: the Bernoulli case

Paper. Alessio Figalli, André Guerra, Sunghan Kim and Henrik Shahgholian.

DOI 10.4171/JEMS/1683. The binding source is the 43-page accepted author manuscript from CVGMT record 6723. That record was created on 8 August 2024 and updated on 19 August 2024; these are record dates, not verified PDF revision dates. Publisher metadata gives online publication on 8 August 2025. No equivalence to the publisher’s subscription version or to arXiv:2311.03006v3 is assumed.

# Assessment and scope

The paper combines harmonic-map compactness, Bernoulli nondegeneracy and scalar free-boundary regularity in a substantial argument. Three Major issues require attention: the uniform geometry used in the half-space approximation, admissibility in the scalar reduction for a general target, and the dimension endpoint in the full-regularity corollary. The first two findings identify invalid proof steps, with explicit counterexamples to the assertions actually used. They do not exhibit a discontinuous small-energy minimizer or disprove the main free-boundary separation theorem. The dimension finding concerns an unsupported extension of the cited classification; no conjectural five-dimensional cone is asserted to exist.

All 43 pages, including Appendices A–C and references, were read as extracted text. The earlier EMFILE failure and incomplete state are preserved. This remains an initial review, with scientific decision pending and no human reviews. No author code was run.

The maps take values in \(\overline M\), while the volume penalty uses the open set \(M\). The distinction is essential in the examples below. The parameter is \(q\ge0\) for small-energy regularity, but \(q>0\) for the quantitative separation of singularities from the noncoincidence set. Uniform convexity of the complement is used only in the second part of the paper. A singular point of the map is not the same as a singular point of its free boundary.

# Proof-chain coverage

The signed distance is positive in \(M\), and the chosen normal points into \(M\). The projection/distance decomposition and the identities for tangential derivatives in Section 2 were checked. Uniform principal-curvature bounds alone do not give the uniform tubular radius claimed in the varying-target argument; P1 isolates this issue. The almost monotonicity formula in Appendix A follows from domain variations of both energy terms. Its nonnegative \(q^2\) contribution gives the stated error of order \(q^2r^2\). Zero-homogeneous tangent maps minimize the Dirichlet energy, since the rescaled weight is \(rq\to0\). Appendix B yields strong convergence on compact subdomains, rather than the global convergence stated in Lemma 2.3; see P4.

Section 3’s distance inequality uses an inward variation supported where the distance is positive. Tangential flows preserve the phase indicator and give the projection equation. The \(W^{2,p}\) estimate then follows from its quadratic right-hand side. The next scalar-minimization argument permits arbitrarily long normal rays; P2 is precisely where the general target geometry does not justify that permission.

Section 4 rescales a small-energy sequence by its energy size. For a genuinely uniform tubular neighborhood, flattening errors, truncation, Luckhaus interpolation on a thin annulus and the vanishing annular penalty give the intended local strong limit. The finite-distance case is supposed to decouple into harmonic tangential components and a scalar one-phase minimizer. The infinite-distance case instead has a harmonic limit. P1 explains why the stated curvature-only class also admits a different, two-boundary limit and needs an additional argument. Minor corrections to the auxiliary flattening choices are recorded in P7.

Section 5 applies weak maximal-function estimates, a Vitali covering argument and a geometric distribution-tail bound to obtain finite \(W^{1,p}\) norms. The printed strong maximal inequality has the wrong target norm, P6, but the finite-\(p\) conclusion also follows directly from pointwise domination by the maximal function. The ensuing bootstrap gives a Hölder map, then a Lipschitz projected component, and finally invokes the scalar reduction for the Lipschitz distance component. Thus P2 is a core dependency of Theorem 1.1, not an unused remark. The phase-boundary assertion in Corollary 5.7 needs the restriction in P5.

Sections 6–9 were followed separately under convexity. A nonnegative zero-homogeneous subharmonic distance is constant; a positive constant gives a harmonic tangent map, while a zero constant and the subharmonic local supremum estimate force continuity of the original distance. At singular points, tangents lie in \(\partial M\). In Proposition 7.1, strong \(W^{1,2}\) convergence, strict tangential curvature and unique continuation for a nonconstant limiting harmonic map force the phase density to vanish. The product passage is valid against bounded tests using strong \(L^1\) convergence of the quadratic coefficient and weak-star convergence of indicators; it is not an \(L^\infty\) bound on that product.

Proposition 8.1 uses a truncated harmonic radial barrier, projection to the convex obstacle and Young’s inequality. Its absorption requires \(q>0\). The contradiction in Lemma 9.2 then compares linear nondegeneracy with higher-order vanishing. The passage involving suprema needs the subharmonic estimate spelled out in P7; almost-everywhere convergence by itself is insufficient. The compactness application also needs control of translations of the image, since its displayed energy bound controls derivatives, not the full \(W^{1,2}\) norm.

Sections 10–11 use the scalar Bernoulli equation with a tangentially regular lower-order term, flatness improvement, hodograph regularity and transmission estimates. Piecewise higher regularity of \(\Pi\circ u\) yields \(C^{1,1}\) across a regular free boundary, not global \(C^2\) across it. The dependence on target smoothness and the distinction between BMO and bounded second derivatives were retained. Appendix C uses the one-sided target condition to exclude nonconstant tangent maps through the cited removable-singularity theorem. Its proof explicitly involves the bound on \(q\).

# Findings and decisive evidence

## P1. Major: curvature control does not justify the half-space limit

Location. (2.4)–(2.5), pages 8–9; Proposition 4.1 and the uniform tubular-neighborhood assertion on page 17; Lemma 4.3, pages 18–22.

Two parallel boundary components can approach each other while all their principal curvatures remain zero. Take \[M_k=\mathbb R\times(-\epsilon_k/2,\epsilon_k/2),\qquad
 \epsilon_k\downarrow0 .\] These are connected smooth domains with a common curvature bound. The nearest boundary point is not unique on their midline, and a two-sided tubular neighborhood with unique nearest projection has width at most \(\epsilon_k/2\). There is no lower width bound depending only on curvature. Moreover \(\epsilon_k^{-1}M_k=\mathbb R\times(-1/2,1/2)\) is a strip, not a half-space.

This is also compatible with the variational, mean and energy data used in the limit argument. On \(B_4=(-4,4)\) let \[v(x)=\max\{-1/2,\min\{x,1/2\}\},\quad
 u_k(x)=(0,\epsilon_k v(x)),\quad q_k=\epsilon_k .\] The mean of \(u_k\) is zero, \(\int_{-4}^4|u_k'|^2=\epsilon_k^2\), and \(q_k/\epsilon_k=1\). Each \(u_k\) is a global minimizer with its endpoint values. For any absolutely continuous competitor, its second component must traverse the strip’s full height; on the interior phase, \[|p'|^2+\epsilon_k^2\ge2\epsilon_k|p'|,
 \qquad
 \int_{\{|p|<\epsilon_k/2\}}|p'|\ge\epsilon_k .\] The resulting lower bound \(2\epsilon_k^2\) is attained by \(u_k\); tangential motion only adds energy.

Choosing the lower nearest boundary point gives the proposed half-space \(H=\{a_2>-1/2\}\). The limit \((0,v)\) does not minimize the one-phase energy in \(H\): shifting its transition to the right by \(d\in(0,1)\) preserves the endpoint data and Dirichlet energy but reduces the positive-phase length by \(d\). For \(d=1/2\), its half-space energy drops from \(11/2\) to \(5\). The strip energy stays equal to \(2\).

This example verifies the curvature, weight, mean, energy and minimality premises used for the geometric/variational inference. It is not asserted to satisfy the hypothetical failure-of-approximation condition (4.3); the example itself is Lipschitz. It therefore does not disprove Proposition 4.1. It shows that the proof cannot exclude two-boundary limits from curvature control alone. A uniform reach or chart radius, included in the constants and preserved under the contradiction sequence, would remove this obstruction. Alternatively, the additional possible flat-target limits need their own approximation argument. The fixed-target main theorem may survive such a repair; the claimed curvature-only uniformity has not been established.

## P2. Major: unrestricted normal competitors can leave the target

Location. Lemma 3.9, first paragraph of its proof, page 16; use in (5.19), page 28.

The proof takes any \(w\ge0\) with the distance’s boundary trace and declares \(V=\Pi\circ u+w\nu\circ u\) admissible. This implication is false for a general smooth target. Let \(M=B_1(0)\subset\mathbb R^2\), choose \(p\in\partial M\), and let \(u\equiv p\) on a source ball. This is a minimizing constraint map for every \(q\ge0\): its energy is zero. Its image is in the tubular neighborhood and \(\Pi\circ u\) is Lipschitz. Here \(\rho\circ u=0\) and \(\nu(p)=-p\). Choose a nonnegative smooth compactly supported \(w\) equal to \(3\) on a smaller ball. It has exactly the required zero trace, but \(V=(1-w)p=-2p\) there, so \(V\notin\overline M\).

All assumptions of Lemma 3.9 and of this competitor construction are satisfied before the invalid admissibility conclusion. The calculation refutes that step, not the claimed scalar minimality for this constant example. The general-target Lipschitz bootstrap relies on the reduction, so a local-height comparison argument with justified scalar regularity is needed. Restricting heights to an admissible normal segment is not by itself a proof of minimality against all scalar competitors. When the complement is convex, the outward normal rays remain outside it and this particular obstruction disappears.

## P3. Major: the low-dimensional corollary includes an unsupported endpoint

Location. Corollary 1.10 and the paragraph after Theorem 1.11, page 5.

The source states \(n_*\in\{5,6,7\}\) and regularity for \(n\le n_*\). The cited scalar classification instead gives regularity below the first singular dimension \(d_*\). Jerison–Savin, Theorem 1.1, proves flatness in dimensions \(n\le4\); its introduction distinguishes the conjectural higher dimensions and the De Silva–Jerison singular minimizer in dimension seven. De Silva–Jerison, Theorem 1.1, explicitly constructs that seven-dimensional global minimizer. Together these give \(d_*\in\{5,6,7\}\) and the condition \(n<d_*\), not \(n\le d_*\).

The target’s uniform convexity does not remove the scalar obstruction: if \(w\) is a scalar nonnegative global minimizer, then \(u=(1+w)e_1\) is a constraint minimizer for \(M=\mathbb R^m\setminus\overline B_1\). For every admissible competitor \(V\), the scalar map \(|V|-1\) has the same scalar trace, the same phase indicator, and no larger Dirichlet energy. Thus scalar minimality proves vectorial minimality, with the same free boundary and a smooth uniformly convex obstacle.

In particular the known seven-dimensional example lifts within the target class. This does not refute the corollary’s bare existential wording by itself: its parameter might be five or six. The decisive problem is that the cited argument does not establish the asserted dimension-five conclusion or the included critical endpoint. No unknown five-dimensional counterexample is asserted. State \(n<d_*\), or use a largest regular dimension in \(\{4,5,6\}\), unless an additional classification theorem is proved. The unconditional low-dimensional consequence for the image map needs the same correction.

## P4. Minor: compactness is local, not global

Location. Lemma 2.3, page 10; Appendix B, pages 39–41.

On the unit disk in \(\mathbb R^2\), take the fixed target \(M=\{y_2>0\}\) and \(q_k=0\), and set \[u_k(z)=(k^{-1/2}\operatorname{Re}z^k,1).\] These are unconstrained harmonic Dirichlet minimizers and therefore constraint minimizers with their own traces. Their \(W^{1,2}\) norms are bounded and \(u_k\to(0,1)\) in \(L^2\), but \[\int_{B_1}|Du_k|^2=\pi,\qquad
 \int_{B_r}|Du_k|^2=\pi r^{2k}\quad(0<r<1).\] No subsequence converges strongly in \(W^{1,2}(B_1)\). The proof actually establishes strong \(W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}\) convergence, which suffices for the tangent-map applications that restrict to a smaller ball. The global conclusion needs additional boundary control or replacement by the local one.

## P5. Minor scope correction: the negligible nonreduced boundary needs positive weight

Location. Corollary 5.7, page 29, stated for \(q\ge0\).

Let \(q=0\), \(M=\mathbb R^2\setminus\overline B_1\), and \(u(x)=(1,x_1)\) on a source ball in \(\mathbb R^n\). This affine harmonic map lies in \(\overline M\) and is an unconstrained Dirichlet minimizer, hence a constraint minimizer. Its positive phase is \(\{x_1\ne0\}\), whose characteristic function is one almost everywhere. Its reduced boundary is empty, but its topological free boundary is \(\{x_1=0\}\), of positive \(\mathcal H^{n-1}\) measure in the ball. Thus the final measure-zero assertion in Corollary 5.7 fails.

The distributional identity with the factor \(q=0\) is not contradicted by this example. Nor is the main \(q>0\) separation theorem. Separate the zero-weight obstacle geometry from the positive-weight Bernoulli perimeter assertion.

## P6. Minor: maximal-function target norm and weak-form sign

Locations. Theorem 2.9(i), page 12; footnote 5, page 16.

The strong \((p,p)\) maximal inequality must have an \(L^p\) norm on its left, not \(L^\infty\). In one dimension with \(p=2\), \(f_N=\sqrt N\,\mathbf1_{(-1/N,1/N)}\) has \(\|f_N\|_2=\sqrt2\), whereas the source’s normalization gives \(Mf_N(0)=2\sqrt N\). The later weak estimate is correctly available, and \(|Du|^2\le cM(|Du|^2)\) gives the needed finite-\(p\) conclusion.

The strong equation in Lemma 3.9 is \(\Delta(\rho\circ u)-H=q\,\mathcal H^{n-1}\), where \[H=\operatorname{Hess}\rho_u(Du,Du)\chi_{\{u\in M\}} .\] Its weak form has \(-\int H\phi\), not the printed plus sign. For example, on a small interval let \(u(x)=(a,x)\) with \(a>1\) and exterior-unit-disk target, \(q=0\), and image in the tube. Then \(\rho\circ u=\sqrt{a^2+x^2}-1\) and \[(\rho\circ u)''=H=\frac{a^2}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}>0 .\] The printed weak left side equals \(2\int H\phi\) for a nonnegative nonzero test, while its right side is zero. The strong displayed equation has the correct sign.

## P7. Minor local corrections and gaps that have explicit repairs

Locations. Pages 18–20, 25–27, 31–32 and 35.

In (4.12), \(|\rho_k(0)|/\epsilon_k\to|\gamma|\) does not give \(|a_k|\le2|\gamma|\epsilon_k\) when \(\gamma=0\); use \((|\gamma|+1)\epsilon_k\). For (4.17), additionally choose \(\widetilde R_k\le\widetilde\epsilon_k^{-1/2}\) (with the obvious convention at zero) so that \(\widetilde\epsilon_k\widetilde R_k\to0\). The missing factor \(\epsilon^2\) in the threshold in (5.5) must also be restored when applying the weak bound. These changes preserve the intended limiting estimates.

In (7.3) the factor \(\overline\chi_k\) is needed on the right, as the subsequent argument itself uses. The product \(H_k\overline\chi_k\) has the required weak pairing limit from strong \(L^1\) convergence of \(H_k\); it need not converge weak-star in \(L^\infty\). For Corollary 7.3, a modulus-dependent radius does not by itself give the claimed uniform constants. Instead apply Proposition 7.1 at each rescaled radius, using the common small upper bound on the distance and the almost-monotonicity energy bound, and then iterate (7.5). This gives a uniform density factor without an unspecified individual modulus.

Finally, the passage from (9.2) to a supremum bound for the limit cannot rely only on almost-everywhere convergence. For each fixed small \(r\), subharmonicity gives \[\sup_{B_{2r}(x_k)}\rho\circ u_k
 \le \frac{C}{|B_{3r}|}\int_{B_{3r}(x_0)}\rho\circ u_k\] for large \(k\), after a harmless enlargement of the outer radius. Local \(L^1\) convergence passes the right side to the limit, and the higher-order bound makes it \(O(r^2)\), contradicting (9.2). This supplies the missing passage. A separate justification is still needed for the full-norm compactness hypothesis in Lemma 9.2: derivative-energy boundedness alone does not control target translations. No sequence satisfying its bad-energy premise is claimed here to disprove that lemma.

# Verification limits and requested revision

The reviewer-written independent program checks the strip energies and mean exactly, the inadmissible normal competitor, the disk harmonic-mode energy, the scalar-distance Hessian identity and the maximal-function scaling. These calculations support the analytical constructions; they do not replace the proof of the main regularity theorems.

The CVGMT record, publisher metadata, arXiv version history and an author publication page were checked for code, supplementary material and corrections. No separate relevant code archive, supplement or erratum was located. The manuscript’s data-availability statement says its necessary data are included. Absence of code is not a defect. The related arXiv HTML was used to check selected notation, without replacing or identifying it with the frozen PDF. The critical-dimension references were checked at their theorem statements; their full proofs, Luckhaus’s extension lemma, the scalar flatness theorem, unique continuation, and the cited transmission/hodograph results were not independently reproved.

The revision should first make the target geometry in the approximation argument uniform and justify the scalar comparison class for general targets. The dimension statement and the auxiliary scope/formula corrections should then be made consistently. The central strategy may remain viable; the findings establish the defects specified above, not failure of every principal conclusion. Exact source, TeX, compiler, dependencies, PDF, actual-view records, retained logs and preserved failed image attempts are listed in the per-paper provenance and inventory.
