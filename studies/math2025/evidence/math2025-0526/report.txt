Abstract: The barrier argument and the mechanism preserving complementarity in the hydrodynamic limit were examined in the frozen journal version. Two core proof defects and five local corrections are identified, with explicit examples, dependency boundaries and repairs. They concern the time range of an auxiliary estimate, an overbroad density bound, boundary orientation, the initial-time density statement, the coupling construction, martingale formulas, and the hypotheses of an imported linear PDE result. No counterexample to the intended positive-time hydrodynamic limit or to weak-solution uniqueness is established.

Title: Technical Review of A Weak Formulation of Free Boundary Problems and Its Application to Hydrodynamic Limits of Particle Systems with Selection

# Identity, assumptions, and review scope

The reviewed paper is Rami Atar, A weak formulation of free boundary problems and its application to hydrodynamic limits of particle systems with selection, Annals of Probability 53(5) (2025), 1825–1856, DOI 10.1214/24-AOP1751 [atar]. The frozen author-hosted journal PDF has 32 pages. All its definitions, statements, proofs and references were read. Page numbers below are journal page numbers; PDF page \(k\) is page \(1824+k\).

The individual diffusion has bounded coefficients \(b,c\), with \(b\in C^1\), \(c\in C^2\), bounded \(c'\), and \(c\) bounded away from zero. Its branching rate is \(\kappa\geq0\), and its forward operator is \[L^*u=\partial_x^2(au)-\partial_x(bu)+\kappa u,\qquad a=c^2/2.\] The initial measure \(\xi_0\) is any probability measure, including an atomic one. The injection measure \(\alpha\) is nonnegative and locally finite in time. Its cumulative mass \(I\) satisfies either the spatial domination condition \(\alpha(dx,dt)\leq C\,dx\,dI_t\) with positive temporal Hölder regularity, or temporal Hölder regularity strictly greater than \(1/2\). The removal mass \(J\) is nondecreasing, absolutely continuous, Hölder continuous with a positive exponent and zero at zero. The mass equation \[m_t=1+\kappa\int_0^t m_s\,ds+I_t-J_t\] has a strictly positive global lower bound. The stochastic primitives satisfy the independence and adapted martingale requirements (S.6)–(S.7), and their normalized data converge in probability as in Assumption 2.3.

The weak formulation couples \(\partial_tu-L^*u=\alpha-\beta\) with \(\beta(\mathbb R\times[0,t])=J_t\), nonnegativity, and \[\beta\{(x,t):U(x,t)>0\}=0,\qquad
U(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^x u(y,t)\,dy.\] This orientation describes removal on the left. Absolute continuity of \(J\) is operative: it makes this condition invariant under changing a density on a Lebesgue-null set of times.

# Two auxiliary estimates

Problem 1 (Minor: Lemma 3.8 needs a small-time restriction).  Lemma 3.8, pp. 1841–1842, is stated for every \(\delta>0\), but its proof uses (3.2), p. 1834, which is established only for times at most one. Its unrestricted statement is false.

Proof. Take \(b=1\), \(c=1\), \(\kappa>0\), no injection, and \(J_t=\kappa t\). Put \[\lambda=\sqrt{2\kappa},\qquad v=1+\lambda,\qquad
q(z)=2\kappa z e^{-\lambda z}\mathbf1_{\{z\geq0\}},\] and define \[u(x,t)=q(x-vt),\quad \xi_0(dx)=q(x)\,dx,\quad
\beta(dx,dt)=\kappa\delta_{vt}(dx)\,dt.\] These are admissible data with \(m_t=1\). In particular, \(J\) is Lipschitz and the first injection alternative holds trivially for \(\alpha=0\). Direct calculation gives \[\int q=1,\qquad
\tfrac12q''+(v-1)q'+\kappa q=0\quad(z>0),\qquad
\tfrac12q'(0)=\kappa.\] Since \(q(0)=0\), the distributional boundary term is precisely \(-\kappa\delta_{vt}\); thus this pair solves the weak equation and complementarity condition.

For the drift-one Brownian diffusion, \(\int_{-\infty}^x p_t(x,y)\,dy=\Phi(-\sqrt t)\). Hence \(c_*=\Phi(-1)\) is a valid constant in (3.2), and set \(C_*=2/c_*\), the constant denoted \(c^*\) in Lemma 3.8. For \(n=2\), \[j_2(\delta)=e^{\kappa\delta}-1,\qquad
\rho_{2,\delta}=2v\delta.\] Let \(F(z)=1-(1+z)e^{-z}\), the distribution function of a unit-rate Gamma distribution of shape two. When \(p=C_*(e^{\kappa\delta}-1)<1\), the lemma’s domain condition holds, but its proposed bound is \[\Lambda_p(u_\delta)=v\delta+\frac{F^{-1}(p)}{\sqrt{2\kappa}}.\] For \(\kappa=10^{-4}\), \(\delta=100\), independent high-precision evaluation gives \[p=0.1266918912,\qquad
\rho_{2,\delta}=202.8284271,\qquad
\Lambda_p(u_\delta)=144.8644698.\] The claimed inequality is reversed by about \(57.964\). This is not dependent on an optimized choice of \(c_*\): for any fixed valid \(c_*>0\), take \(\kappa\delta=h\) small enough that \(C_*(e^h-1)<1\), then let \(\kappa\downarrow0\). The boundary displacement grows like \(\kappa^{-1}\), whereas the quantile offset grows like \(\kappa^{-1/2}\). ◻

Counterevidence and impact. Proposition 3.7 uses Lemma 3.8 only for sufficiently small \(\delta\), and uniqueness takes \(\delta\downarrow0\). Restricting Lemma 3.8 to \(0<\delta\leq1\) repairs the displayed proof without changing that limit argument. Alternatively a constant depending on a specified time horizon can be used. The example disproves the unrestricted lemma, not Theorem 3.1.

Problem 2 (Minor: the \(v\)-bound in Lemma 3.3(iii) is too general).  On p. 1835, \(v\) is defined in part (i) using an arbitrary measure \(\gamma\) whose cumulative mass has some positive Hölder exponent. Part (iii)’s assertion \(v\in L^\infty_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty);L^\infty)\) does not follow for that \(v\).

Take standard Brownian motion, \(\kappa=0\), any probability initial measure, and \[\gamma(dx,ds)=\delta_0(dx)(1-s)^{-3/4}\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(s)\,ds.\] Its cumulative mass is \(K_t=4[1-(1-t)_+^{1/4}]\), a nondecreasing \(1/4\)-Hölder function starting at zero. For \(t=1-\varepsilon\), \(|x|\leq\sqrt\varepsilon\), and \(0<\varepsilon<1/3\), retaining only \(r=t-s\in[\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]\) gives \[(S*\gamma)(x,1-\varepsilon)
\geq
\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt2\,3^{3/4}}\,
\varepsilon^{-1/4}.\] Thus the essential supremum is unbounded near time one. This is a positive-measure phenomenon, not just an infinite value at a single point.

Counterevidence and repair. The proof on p. 1836 estimates \(S_t\xi_0+S*\alpha\), using the stronger admissibility alternatives for \(\alpha\). The \(v\) actually used in Lemmas 4.3–4.4 is explicitly this latter function. Setting \(v=S_t\xi_0+S*\alpha\) in part (iii), or adding the same admissibility conditions on \(\gamma\), removes the overstatement. The example does not refute the boundedness of that intended dominating density or part (i)’s \(L^1_tL^q_x\) assertion.

# Boundary orientation and initial time

Problem 3 (Minor: Remark 2.6(i) identifies the opposite selection boundary).  Remark 2.6(i), p. 1833, states that a classical solution of (1.2) produces a solution of (2.10) by taking \(\beta(dx,dt)=c_0\delta_{\ell_t}(dx)\,dt\). This is incompatible with the left-tail complementarity condition.

For a concrete classical solution of (1.2), p. 1826, set \(c_0=1\), \(\ell_t=1\) and \(u(x,t)=2(1-x)\) on \(0\leq x\leq1\), zero for \(x\geq1\), with this stationary initial density. It has mass one, zero second derivative in the interior and both specified flux values equal to one. If the density is extended by zero to \(x<0\), then \[U(1,t)=\int_{-\infty}^1u(x,t)\,dx=1,\qquad
\beta\{U>0\}\big|_{\mathbb R\times[0,T]}=T>0.\] Hence the proposed pair fails (2.10)(ii).

Counterevidence and repair. Equation (1.2) deliberately removes the rightmost particle and includes reflection at the origin; the main model removes the leftmost particle on the full line. A corresponding weak formulation must use the right tail and the appropriate half-line boundary flux, or explicitly transform the orientation and domain. Merely extending the half-line density by zero also introduces a distributional issue at the reflecting endpoint. The general idea of a complementary weak formulation remains useful; this direct identification with (1.2) is the incorrect statement.

Problem 4 (Minor: Theorem 2.5(ii)’s density formula must exclude time zero).  On p. 1833, the theorem sets \(\xi_t(dx)=u(x,t)\,dx\) and asserts continuity on \(\mathbb R_+=[0,\infty)\), although arbitrary probability initial measures are allowed.

Choose \(b=0,c=1,\kappa=0,\alpha=0,J=0,\xi_0=\delta_0\). These are admissible data, with mass identically one, and can be approximated by particles initially at zero and no injections or removals on each fixed finite horizon. At positive times the density is the heat kernel. At time zero there is no Lebesgue density of \(\delta_0\), whatever version of \(u\) is chosen. In particular, an absolutely continuous measure assigns zero to \(\{0\}\), whereas \(\delta_0\{0\}=1\).

Counterevidence and repair. The measure-valued path is nevertheless weakly continuous at zero. The proof of Lemma 4.3 on p. 1851 already makes the correct distinction: \(\xi_t=u(\cdot,t)\,dx\) for \(t>0\), and \(\xi_0\) is specified separately. Incorporating that convention in Theorem 2.5(ii) repairs the endpoint wording. This is not a counterexample to hydrodynamic convergence.

# The particle construction and martingale identities

Problem 5 (Major: the stated coupling does not establish the required pathwise domination).  The no-removal comparison on p. 1847 is said to use exactly the same stochastic primitives, changing only the removal times to infinity. With the chronological family labels prescribed on p. 1831, this does not imply \(\xi_t^N\leq\zeta_t^N\) as measures.

A finite genealogy showing the obstruction. Consider one root, with Brownian motion, positive branching rate and no injections before the comparison time. There is a positive-probability event on which the root first has child \(1\), that child is to its left at a deterministic removal time and is removed, and no other particle is removed before the comparison time. In the no-removal system, child \(1\) survives and has a child before the root’s next birth. That extra child receives chronological label \(2\).

At the root’s next birth, the original system gives the newborn label \(2\), whereas the no-removal system gives the corresponding newborn label \(3\). The first uses \(B^2\), while its genealogically corresponding counterpart uses \(B^3\). Moreover, the no-removal particle already labelled \(2\) started earlier at a different space-time location. At a later time before further events, with positive probability the original particle \(2\) occupies a point occupied by none of the finitely many no-removal particles. Atomic measure domination therefore fails. The required event is obtained by prescribing finitely many disjoint Poisson event windows and open Brownian path constraints. It can also be embedded in an \(N\)-particle configuration. ◻

Counterevidence and repair. A valid domination coupling does exist: construct a common unremoved genealogical forest, with drivers indexed by root, parent and child number, then prune the selected system without reassigning drivers on surviving vertices. Chronological numbers can subsequently be used as bookkeeping. Nonanticipation in (S.7) and independent fresh drivers preserve the required marginal law. This explicit coupling, rather than literal reuse of the chronological array, supports the domination used in Lemmas 4.2–4.3 and the many-to-one estimates. The finding concerns the stated construction, not a failure of stochastic domination under all couplings. The invalid comparison is used directly in the compact-containment estimate (4.8) and in the passage from (4.18) to absolute continuity of every limit of the selected system. Those are core steps of Lemmas 4.2–4.3 and Theorem 2.5. A replacement coupling with the correct marginal laws is therefore needed to complete that derivation. This core proof defect is Major even though the genealogical repair supports the intended result.

Problem 6 (Minor: three transcription errors in Lemma 4.1’s martingale derivation).  The formulas on pp. 1846–1847 require an indicator reversal, removal of an extra branching-rate factor, and retention of the terminal configuration term before setting \(t=T\).

The definition after (4.3) prints \(\mathbf1_{\{\sigma^i\geq t\}}\int_{\sigma^i}^{t\wedge\tau^i}c\,\partial_x\phi\,dB^i\). For an initial particle \(\sigma^i=0\), this is zero for every \(t>0\), whereas the preceding Itô integral is generally nonzero. For instance, take standard Brownian motion, no events on a short interval and a compactly supported test function with nonzero spatial derivative near the initial point. The correct indicator is \(\mathbf1_{\{t\geq\sigma^i\}}\).

Next, the printed definition \(M_t^{\#,N}=\sum_i\int_{\sigma^i}^{t\wedge\tau^i}d\widehat\pi_s^i\) gives \[Y_t^N=\kappa\int_0^t m_s^N\,ds+M_t^{\#,N},\] not (4.5)’s expression with \(\kappa M_t^{\#,N}\). With one initial particle, \(\kappa=2\), and no births or removals up to \(t\), the defined martingale equals \(-2t\). The printed right side is then \(2t-4t=-2t\), while \(Y_t^N=0\).

Finally, the finite-time sum at the bottom of p. 1846 requires \[\int_{\mathbb R\times[0,t]}\phi\,d\bar\beta^N
=\langle\phi_0,\bar\xi_0^N\rangle
+\int_{\mathbb R\times[0,t]}\phi\,d\bar\alpha^N
+\int_0^t\langle\partial_s\phi+L\phi,\bar\xi_s^N\rangle ds
+\bar M_t^N-\langle\phi_t,\bar\xi_t^N\rangle.\] The last term is omitted in the displayed expression.

Counterevidence and repair. The next page explicitly sets \(t=T\), where \(\phi_T=0\), and the quadratic-variation argument already uses the correct lifetime orientation. Correcting these three formulas restores the usual decomposition and its order \(N^{-1}\) quadratic variation. No failure of Lemma 4.1’s vanishing-noise conclusion is established.

# The cited linear PDE theory

Problem 7 (Major: the imported linear well-posedness argument lacks its required hypotheses).  The passage following (2.9), p. 1833, transforms the forward operator using \(\bar b=-b+a'\) and \(\bar\kappa=\kappa-b'\), then invokes Amann’s Theorems 1 and 13 and Remarks 1 and 2(b). The paper’s assumptions do not imply the explicit coefficient bounds in that reference.

The acquired original [amann], p. 105, assumes bounded uniformly smooth coefficients in its general elliptic operator; Remark 2(b), p. 116, explicitly permits a bounded measurable zeroth-order coefficient. But \(b(x)=\sin(x^2)\), \(c=1\) satisfy Assumption 2.1, while \[\bar\kappa(x)=\kappa-2x\cos(x^2)\] is unbounded. Thus the stated transformation does not by itself verify the cited sufficient conditions. The reference also mentions further relaxations; those should not be silently identified with the assumptions here.

A direct repair under the original assumptions. The underlying linear statement can instead be justified without a bound on \(b'\). Let \(h=|c|\), set \[y=F(x)=\int_0^x h(r)^{-1}\,dr,\qquad
w(y,t)=h(x)u(x,t),\quad x=F^{-1}(y).\] This is a global bi-Lipschitz change of variable, preserving the \(L^1_tL^q_x\) class for every finite \(q>1\). Applying the chain rule in the weak formulation, justified by local smooth approximation of tests, gives \[\partial_tw=\tfrac12\partial_y^2w-\partial_y(Bw)+\kappa w+\nu,
\quad
B(y)=\frac{b(x)}{h(x)}-\frac12h'(x),\] where \(B\) is bounded and \(\nu\) is the pushforward of the driving measure. Include the pushed initial measure at time zero in a measure \(\eta\). With \(G\) the standard heat kernel, the equation has the distributional Duhamel form \[w=G*\eta-(\partial_yG)*(Bw)+\kappa G*w.\] The measure term belongs to \(L^1([0,T];L^q)\), since \(\|G_t\|_q=C_qt^{-(q-1)/(2q)}\) and the exponent is less than one. The remaining linear operator is a contraction in the equivalent norm \(\int_0^T e^{-\lambda t}\|w_t\|_qdt\) for sufficiently large \(\lambda\), because \[\int_0^T e^{-\lambda t}\|\partial_yG_t\|_1dt
\leq C\lambda^{-1/2},\qquad
\int_0^T e^{-\lambda t}\|G_t\|_1dt\leq\lambda^{-1}.\] Heat-equation duality gives the same Duhamel identity for any distributional solution in this class. The contraction therefore supplies existence and uniqueness there; transforming back gives the needed linear result. Compatibility across \(q\) follows by performing the construction simultaneously in intersections of these spaces.

Dependency and severity. This repair addresses the imported linear uniqueness used to identify the mild version in Lemma 3.3 and the limit equation in Section 4. It explains why a citation-hypothesis mismatch should not be presented as a counterexample to the main theorem. A precise applicable reference or the direct argument should replace the unsupported application. The displayed invocation does not establish linear well-posedness for the stated coefficient class; Lemma 3.3 then uses this unsupported uniqueness to identify the mild representative, on which the barrier proof and limit identification depend. This is a Major gap in a core derivation. The direct argument above supplies a repair under the original assumptions; it does not turn the original invocation into a valid one or establish that the final theorem is false.

# Positive checks, coverage, and version limits

The six mass-transport inequalities of Lemma 3.5 were checked against their total-mass restrictions. For a density of mass \(m\) with tail \(H(r)\), the two cut operators have tails \[(C_d u)[r,\infty)=\min\{H(r),m-d\},\] \[(C_{D,d}u)[r,\infty)
=H(r)-\min\{d,\max\{0,H(r)-(m-D-d)\}\}.\] These identities give direct checks of the order and modulus calculations. An independent exact-rational program checked 149,529 admissible scalar cases of parts (ii), (iv) and (vi), with no failures; finite checks supplement the algebra and do not prove the general assertions.

The lower-barrier proof was followed through the separation of the removal support, the Gaussian tail \(e^{-c\Delta^4/\delta}\), its replacement by \(e^{-\Delta^5/\delta}\) for fixed small \(\Delta\) and sufficiently small \(\delta\), the quantile separation using the intended \(L^\infty\) bound, and the error recurrence. The final bound \[e^{\kappa t}\Delta+
e^{\kappa(t+\delta)}
\bigl(J_{t_0+\delta}+e^{-\Delta^5/\delta}J_t\bigr)\] does tend to zero in the stated order \(\delta\downarrow0\), then \(\Delta\downarrow0\), then \(t_0\downarrow0\). Zero-removal intervals are treated separately by \(j_n=0\); positive total mass guarantees the cut domains.

In the convergence argument, the stopped population estimate, first and second moments of the dominating branching system, compact containment and the continuity modulus were traced. Density identification is a positive-time assertion. The complementarity step uses countably many rational space-time tests, absence of spatial atoms in the limit, and continuity of its measure-valued path. At the switch from \([r,\infty)\) to \((r,\infty)\) on p. 1854, retaining a strictly smaller rational threshold from the preceding approximation avoids any loss of a possible spatial atom of \(\beta\); this is an explicit intended reading, not an assumption that \(\beta\) has no spatial atoms.

The independent primary-authored calculations verify the traveling profile’s mass, interior PDE and boundary flux, the numerical quantile violation, and the cut-operator cases.
