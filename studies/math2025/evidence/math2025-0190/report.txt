Abstract: The acquired author version contains a false effective square-representation assertion: \(441\) satisfies every relevant hypothesis of Theorem 2.6 for the numerator orbit starting at \((2,25)\), but its only possible matrix rows are nonprimitive. A family shows that replacing \(8xy\) by any universal constant times \(xy\) cannot repair this square bound. The proof of the near-full-dimension thin-semigroup theorem also uses uniform contraction and distortion properties that fail for its parabolic generators. These are Major findings with separate, explicitly limited effects. Local corrections concern the effective root branch, a continued-fraction proof’s enlargement of the semigroup, a finite endpoint equality, and the denominator path in the supplied code. Positive checks include the reciprocity identities, square exclusion, strong approximation, an independent exact certificate \(\delta_{\{4,8,\ldots,128\}}>0.5001\), and a reviewer-authored verification of all \(109\,120\,000\) integers in the finite interval used for Theorem 2.8. Neither the principal reciprocity phenomenon nor the continued-fraction counterexample is refuted by the findings. Scientific adjudication remains pending.

If \(x=0\) in the coprime odd-denominator case, then \(y=1\) and the claim is the row/column identity of Lemma 3.1. If \(y=0\), then \(x=1\), and the corresponding identity follows directly. # Confirmed findings

Problem 1 (Major: false square saturation and loss of primitivity).  Location. Theorem 2.6, PDF p. 5 and its proof on p. 19; Lemmas 4.3–4.6, pp. 13–17, especially (4.0.5) and the replacement of the square-denominator symbol by a constant. Source TeX lines 245–259, 686–946 and 1060–1087.

Take \((x,y)=(2,25)\) and \(n=441=21^2\). The starting coordinates are positive and coprime, \(y\) is odd, \(\left(\frac{2}{25}\right)=1\), and \(441\geq8xy=400\). Table 1 places no congruence or reciprocity obstruction on the numerator. Yet a putative matrix’s top row must satisfy \[2a+25b=441,\qquad a\equiv1\pmod4,\qquad a,b\geq0.\] All solutions are \[(a,b)=(33+100t,15-8t),\qquad t=0,1.\] Their gcds are respectively three and seven. Neither row can occur in any integral determinant-one matrix. Consequently \(441\) is absent even from the larger \(\Gamma_1(4)^{\geq0}\) orbit. This is a counterexample to the stated effective conclusion, with every hypothesis checked.

The common algebraic error is to replace \(\left(\frac{q}{m^2}\right)\) by one, or \(\left(\frac{q}{2m^2}\right)\) by \(\left(\frac{q}{2}\right)\), without retaining the condition \(\gcd(q,m)=1\). In the proof’s determinant-one change of coordinates, \[\gcd(cu+dv,n)=\gcd(u,v).\] Choosing \(d\) coprime to \(u\) does not make \((u,v)\) primitive. The exact factors are instead \[\left(\frac{q}{m^2}\right)=\mathbf1_{\gcd(q,m)=1},\qquad
 \left(\frac{q}{2m^2}\right)=\left(\frac{q}{2}\right)\,\mathbf1_{\gcd(q,m)=1}
 \quad(q\ \text{odd}).
 \tag{3.1}\] A denominator example is \((x,y,n)=(3,13,225)\): \(225\geq4xy\), \(\left(\frac{3}{13}\right)=1\), and the only permitted lower row is \((u,v)=(36,9)\). This refutes Lemma 4.4, though it is below the main theorem’s \(8xy\) threshold.

The twice-square numerator branch also fails: \((x,y,n)=(1,7,98)\) satisfies \(98\geq56\) and every congruence hypothesis of Lemma 4.5. All permitted rows are \[(21,11),\ (49,7),\ (77,3),
 \qquad\text{with symbols }-1,\ 0,\ -1.\] Equation (2.1) excludes \(98\) from this orbit. This small example does not refute the much larger nonsquare cutoff of Theorem 2.6; it refutes the intermediate lemma used to justify its twice-square branch.

A stronger repair boundary. No universal constant \(C\) can replace \(8\) in a square bound \(n\geq Cxy\). Choose an integer \(K\) with \(4K>C\) and put \[m=2t\prod_{j=0}^{K-1}(4j+3),\qquad n=m^2,\qquad x=1.\] For sufficiently large \(t\), the interval \((n/(4K+1),n/(4K))\) has length exceeding four. Choose \(y\equiv1\pmod4\) in that interval. Then \(n>Cxy\) and \(\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)=1\). Every nonnegative top row has \(a=n-yb\equiv1\pmod4\), hence \[b\in\{3,7,\ldots,4K-1\}.\] Each such \(b\) divides \(m\), so \(\gcd(a,b)=b>1\). This family varies the starting vector; it does not prove infinitely many exceptions for one fixed orbit.

Impact and counterevidence. The explicit central square bound is false, and its proof requires a primitive-point counting argument. It is therefore Major even though exact row criteria remain available. The square-exclusion theorem, the congruence classification and the finite-alphabet counterexample are unaffected. Qualitative eventual saturation for each fixed starting vector is not disproved by this family. The non-square, non-twice-square character-sign branch retains the zero values and does not suffer this particular cancellation.

Repair. Keep the indicators in (3.1), prove existence of a primitive row within the relevant sign class, and replace the effective square threshold by a justified bound with different dependence on the starting vector or additional arithmetic information. A pointwise demand for a primitive row is not a proof of the published uniform bound. The exact orbit in Theorem 2.8 has a separate verification described below; that verification does not repair the uniform statement. Author adoption is unknown.

# Unscored clarifications and verification limits

Theorem 2.6 calls \(r\) the unique positive root of \[H(r)=\frac{\sqrt r}{\log r\log\log r}=7.542795xy.
 \tag{4.1}\] For positive integer \(xy\) there are two roots. Positive values of \(H\) require \(r>e\). With \(z=\log r\), \[\frac{d\log H}{dz}=\frac12-\frac1z-\frac1{z\log z}.\] This derivative increases strictly from a negative value to a positive limit. The function \(H\) tends to infinity at both ends of \((e,\infty)\) and has one minimum. Since \(H(e^2)=e/(2\log2)<3<7.542795xy\), it crosses the required level twice. The proof on p. 19 uses the increasing branch. Define \(r=r_+\) to be the larger root, equivalently the unique root beyond \(e^4\), and separate \(xy=0\) before using (4.1). For \(xy=6\), the computed upper root is about \(3\,404\,737.102082\), below \(3\,410\,000\). The reversed inequality on p. 20 is consistent with a local sign error; the conservative cutoff actually used is correct.

These facts specify the intended range, but no second core counterexample is inferred from the root wording. Because the proof itself supplies the large-root interpretation, this is recorded as an unscored scope clarification. Likewise, the odd part of zero and the positive-integer interpretation of Corollary 4.2 are retained as clarifications with direct endpoint checks.

The static continued-fraction routines also seed the value three as represented, whereas the displayed family with a positive tail parameter has smallest denominator five. This is a bounded source-level mismatch, not a certification that the original Table 2 aggregates were produced by these exact files or are incorrect. No rerun to \(2\times10^{13}\), CPU-hour replication, or verification of the terminal missing denominators was performed. The conjecture based on that experiment remains a conjecture.

# Independent verification of the explicit orbit

## The entire stated finite interval

Theorem 2.8 concerns the numerator orbit of \((2,3)\). A reviewer-authored exact sieve, using no author functions, checked every integer \[1\leq n\leq N=109\,120\,000.\] It uses the criterion \(2u+3v=n\), \(u\equiv1\pmod4\), \(\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)=1\).

For \(n\equiv2\pmod3\), the row \((1,(n-2)/3)\) always works. For \(n\equiv0\pmod3\), the sieve considers \(u=9,21,33,\ldots\); for \(n\equiv1\pmod3\), it considers \(u=5,17,29,\ldots\). A candidate is removed only when \(n\geq2u\) and the exact Jacobi symbol \(\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)\) is one. Since \(u\equiv1\pmod4\), reciprocity makes this exactly the required Kronecker condition. For a fixed \(u\), the lookup index \[\left(\frac{n\bmod(3u)+u}{3}\right)\bmod u
       =\left(\frac{n-2u}{3}\right)\bmod u\] is integral in the appropriate residue class. All intermediates fit in the specified unsigned integer type; no floating point is used for representation decisions.

The residue-zero class required forty-two rows through \(u=501\); the residue-one class required thirty rows through \(u=353\). The only survivors were the \(10\,446\) squares at most \(N\) and \[3,\ 6,\ 7,\ 10,\ 12,\ 15,\ 18,\ 19,\ 27,\ 31,\ 34,\ 55,\ 63,\ 99,\ 115.
 \tag{5.1}\] Every nonsquare survivor was then checked against all nonnegative congruence-compatible rows, with no positive symbol. The square exclusion also follows analytically from Theorem 2.5, because \(\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)=-1\). The saved execution result therefore reproduces the finite exception list by a different algorithm. It establishes a finite statement, not by itself the infinite theorem.

## A primitive-row repair for the large twice-squares

For \(n=2m^2\) in this particular orbit, write the eligible rows as \[u=u_0+12k,\qquad v=v_0-8k,\qquad
 0\leq k\leq n/24-u_0/12,\quad 0<u_0<12.\] Choose the auxiliary determinant-one change of coordinates used in Section 4 of the source, and let \(Q=cu+dv\). The correct character expression is \[\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)=s\left(\frac{Q}{2}\right)\,\mathbf1_{\gcd(Q,m)=1},
 \qquad s\in\{1,-1\},
 \tag{5.2}\] with \(s\) fixed. Here \(Q\) is odd, and the source’s \(\mu_2\) factor is one. Increasing \(k\) changes \(Q\) by \(-4\), so \(\left(\frac{Q}{2}\right)\) changes sign. Restrict to the parity \(k=\epsilon+2j\) that makes the sign in (5.2) positive. The allowed \(j\) form an interval of at least \[\ell=\left\lfloor m^2/24\right\rfloor\] consecutive integers: the amount subtracted from its upper endpoint is \((u_0/12+\epsilon)/2<1\).

As \(j\) increases, \(Q\) changes by \(-8\). Each odd prime divisor of \(m\) therefore forbids exactly one residue class of \(j\). If \(\omega\) is the number of these primes, CRT and inclusion–exclusion give at least \[\ell\prod_{p\mid m,\ p\ {\rm odd}}(1-1/p)-2^\omega
 \ \geq\ \ell/2^\omega-2^\omega\] primitive choices. Since the primes are distinct, \(4^\omega\leq(4/3)\operatorname{rad}(m_{\rm odd})\leq4m/3\). For \(m\geq34\), \[\left\lfloor m^2/24\right\rfloor
       >m^2/24-1>4m/3.\] The final inequality follows from \(m^2-32m-24>0\), already true at \(34\) and increasing thereafter. Thus every \(2m^2\geq2312\) in this orbit is represented. This repairs the large twice-square step needed here; it does not restore the general \(8xy\) assertion.
