Abstract: This review concerns the 49-page arXiv version 2402.00767v2 of the paper by Pierre Perruchaud and Isao Sauzedde. A missing heat-kernel factor makes the displayed Symanzik identity in Theorem 6.8 false even for a trivial line bundle on a unit flat torus. An explicit spectral calculation establishes the discrepancy and verifies the natural correction. A separate step in the proof of Theorem 7.1 substitutes a squared scalar trace defect for a matrix-norm estimate. An admissible winding-bridge example disproves the corresponding displayed inequality. The correct matrix estimate repairs that part of the argument. Neither issue is a counterexample to the determinant representation in Theorem 2.1. The assessment is an initial review of the identified version, not an independent acceptance decision.

# The missing transition density in Theorem 6.8

Problem 1 (Major: a false displayed Symanzik formula).  In Theorem 6.8, page 37, both the real and complex displayed identities omit \(p_t(x,y)\) from each integral representing a covariance. The required factor is present in Lemma 6.5, page 35, and in the Feynman–Kac representation of Theorem 3.3, page 13. The bridge notation in Section 2.2, page 10, does not absorb this factor.

## Hypotheses and the exact assertion under examination

Section 6 assumes a compact connected smooth Riemannian manifold of dimension \(d\in\{2,3\}\), without boundary, with smooth nonnegative mass \(m\) that is not identically zero. The real or complex metric vector bundle has finite rank, and the connections are smooth and compatible with the bundle metric. A connection has an arbitrary probability law \(P\). Definition 6.7 weights that law by the positive loop-soup expectation \[w(\nabla)=\mathbb E^{\mathcal L}_{n/2}[Z_{\nabla,\mathcal L}],
 \qquad
 Z_{\nabla,\mathcal L}=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal L}\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Hol}^\nabla(\ell).\] Here the trace is normalized, as explicitly defined on page 7. Theorem 6.8 concerns moments of the resulting mixture of Gaussian free fields, smooth test sections, and a measurable functional \(f\) of the connection. The counterexample below uses \(f=1\), so no integrability ambiguity is involved.

Equation reference: eq:printed, eq:correct. For the real case with \(k=1\), the covariance integral printed in Theorem 6.8 is \[
 \widetilde C_\nabla(s_1,s_2)
 =\int_0^\infty\!\int_{M^2}
 \mathbb E^m_{t,x,y}\!
 \left[\left\langle s_1(x),\operatorname{Hol}^\nabla_{t,0}(W)s_2(y)\right\rangle\right]
 \,dx\,dy\,dt .\] By contrast, Lemma 6.5 identifies the actual covariance as \[
 C_\nabla(s_1,s_2)
 =\int_0^\infty\!\int_{M^2}p_t(x,y)
 \mathbb E^m_{t,x,y}\!
 \left[\left\langle s_1(x),\operatorname{Hol}^\nabla_{t,0}(W)s_2(y)\right\rangle\right]
 \,dx\,dy\,dt .\] The bridge expectation is the usual normalized bridge expectation, multiplied by the mass/killing weight. The density \(p_t\) is a separate factor. The original PDF images on pages 10, 35, and 37 were inspected to exclude a text-extraction omission.

## An admissible exact counterexample

Let \(M=\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2\) with its standard flat metric and volume one. Let \(E=M\times\mathbb R\), let \(\nabla=d\), and set \(m\equiv1\). Take \(P=\delta_d\), \(f\equiv1\), \(k=1\), and \[s_1(x)=s_2(x)=s(x)=\cos(2\pi x_1).\] The manifold is smooth, compact, connected, closed, and two-dimensional; the bundle and connection are smooth and metric-compatible; the mass is strictly positive; and the sections are smooth and periodic. Thus all operative assumptions for this instance are satisfied.

Equation reference: eq:variance, eq:zero. Holonomy equals the identity for every path. Every loop-product cutoff equals one, so \(Z_{\nabla,\mathcal L}=w(\nabla)=1\), and the normalization in Definition 6.7 is also one. Equation (3), page 7, gives \[L=-\tfrac12(\partial_1^2+\partial_2^2)+1,\qquad
 Ls=(1+2\pi^2)s,\qquad
 \int_M s\,dx=0,\qquad \|s\|_{L^2(M)}^2=\tfrac12.\] Consequently the actual Gaussian variance is \[
 \mathbb E[\Phi(s)^2]=\langle s,L^{-1}s\rangle
 =\frac{1}{2(1+2\pi^2)}>0.\] For constant mass one, \(\mathbb E^m_{t,x,y}[1]=e^{-t}\). The right-hand side of the printed formula instead becomes \[
 \widetilde C_d(s,s)
 =\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dt
   \left(\int_M s(x)\,dx\right)^2=0.\] Equations [eq:variance] (#eq:variance) and [eq:zero] (#eq:zero) contradict the displayed assertion of Theorem 6.8.

This calculation is exact. An independently written SymPy check verified the eigenfunction equation, both integrals of \(s\), the zero printed answer, and the positive variance \(0.024108923574146834\ldots\). It also verified that inserting the missing heat kernel yields \[\int_0^\infty \tfrac12 e^{-(1+2\pi^2)t}\,dt
 =\frac{1}{2(1+2\pi^2)}.\] No author code was executed.

## Alternative readings, repair, and impact

An implicit unit-volume convention cannot explain the discrepancy: the counterexample already has volume one. A convention putting the transition density inside \(\mathbb E^m_{t,x,y}\) would conflict with the explicit definition on page 10 and with Lemma 6.5. A determinant weight or random connection effect cannot correct it: both are trivial in this example.

Equation reference: eq:wick. There is strong internal evidence for the intended repair. Theorem 3.3, Lemma 6.5, and the one-paragraph Wick argument following Theorem 6.8 all require [eq:correct] (#eq:correct). For bounded measurable \(f\), replace every occurrence of [eq:printed] (#eq:printed) by [eq:correct] (#eq:correct). The corrected real formula is \[
 \mathbb E^{\mathrm{GFF}}_P
 \!\left[f(\nabla)\prod_{i=1}^{2k}\Phi(s_i)\right]
 =
 \frac{\displaystyle
 \mathbb E_P\!\left[f(\nabla)w(\nabla)
   \sum_{\pi}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\pi}C_\nabla(s_i,s_j)\right]}
 {\mathbb E_P[w(\nabla)]},\] where \(\pi\) ranges over pairings.

Verification of the repair. Condition on \(\nabla\) in Definition 6.7. The conditional field is centered Gaussian with covariance [eq:correct] (#eq:correct); Wick’s formula therefore gives the sum over pairings in [eq:wick] (#eq:wick). Integrating against \(w(\nabla)dP/\mathbb E_Pw\) gives the result. The scalar massive Green bound used in Lemma 6.5 bounds the covariance uniformly in the metric connection for fixed smooth sections. The loop products have absolute value at most one; their positive expectations give \(0<w\leq1\) and \(\mathbb E_Pw>0\). Thus bounded \(f\) makes these finite moments integrable. The complex pairing formula requires the same missing density in each covariance, with the conjugation convention of the paper. ◻

For an arbitrary unbounded measurable \(f\), suitable integrability is still needed to interpret signed expectations; the bounded-\(f\) repair does not certify an unrestricted formula. The demonstrated failure is of the displayed Symanzik identity in this version. It does not invalidate the construction of the conditional Gaussian field, the loop representation of determinants, or the corrected covariance formula.

# A matrix estimate replaced by a scalar trace square

Problem 2 (Major: a false cutoff inequality in a core proof).  In the proof of Theorem 7.1, page 40, the first estimate of \(-\log\mathbb EX_\delta\) cites equation (20), page 32, and replaces the trace defect by \(\tfrac12|1-\operatorname{tr}U|^2\), where \(U\) is holonomy. Equation (20) controls the matrix \(I-U\); it does not provide this scalar replacement for a normalized trace in rank greater than one.

## Assumptions and a decisive algebraic check

Theorem 7.1, page 38, works in dimension two with a smooth metric bundle of arbitrary finite rank and a compatible smooth connection. The Riemannian metric, conformal factor, and nonnegative mass are smooth, and either the boundary is nonempty or the mass is not identically zero. Its proof compares products with different duration cutoffs after a conformal time change. The relevant event in the first moment is invariant under time reversal.

Equation reference: eq:traceid. For a unitary or orthogonal \(U\), set \(z=\operatorname{tr}U\), with normalized trace. Then \(|z|\leq1\), and \[
 2\operatorname{Re}(1-z)
 =|1-z|^2+1-|z|^2.\] Hence the pointwise inequality \(\operatorname{Re}(1-\operatorname{tr}U)\leq\tfrac12|1-\operatorname{tr}U|^2\), which would justify the displayed substitution, has the wrong direction whenever \(|\operatorname{tr}U|<1\).

For example, take the real orthogonal matrix \[U=J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.\] Then \(\operatorname{tr}U=0\), so the two sides are \(1\) and \(1/2\). Moreover \((I-U)^*(I-U)=2I\), which confirms that the matrix estimate itself is consistent. This is not an inadmissible class of holonomies: on the trivial rank-two real bundle over the flat two-torus, the smooth metric connection \(d-(\pi/2)J\,dx_1\) has holonomy \(J\) along a winding-one loop. All matrix equalities were also checked independently in SymPy.

This finite-dimensional example disproves the proposed pointwise matrix-to-trace-square substitution. By itself it is not a counterexample to the averaged cutoff inequality for a specified Brownian event, and it is not a counterexample to the final conformal-invariance theorem. The cited estimate does not justify the displayed scalar bound. The following construction also disproves the averaged inequality itself.

## An admissible counterexample to the integral inequality

Retain the flat unit torus and rank-two connection just described, and set \(m=1\). Choose the smooth conformal factor \[f(x)=\tfrac12\log a(x),\qquad a(x)=2+\cos(2\pi x_1).\] Since \(1\leq a\leq3\), this is globally smooth and \(\|e^{-2f}\|_\infty=1\). Formula (22), page 39, gives \(\widehat T=\int_0^t a(W_s)\,ds\). Thus the event in the first moment on page 40 is \(A=\{\widehat T>\delta\}\). The manifold is closed and \(m=1\) is nonzero; all assumptions of Theorem 7.1 are satisfied.

Equation reference: eq:Ldelta, eq:Rdelta. Let \(L_\delta\) and \(R_\delta\) denote respectively the two sides of the first inequality following the exact Campbell integral on page 40: \[\begin{aligned}
 L_\delta&=\int_0^\delta\!\int_M\frac{p_t(x,x)}t
 \mathbb E^1_{t,x,x}\bigl[\mathbf1_A(1-\operatorname{tr}U)\bigr]\,dx\,dt,
 \\
 R_\delta&=\frac12\int_0^\delta\!\int_M\frac{p_t(x,x)}t
 \mathbb E^1_{t,x,x}\bigl[|1-\operatorname{tr}U|^2\bigr]\,dx\,dt.
 \end{aligned}\] The asserted inequality is \(L_\delta\leq R_\delta\).

The lifted torus bridge has winding \(k=(k_1,k_2)\in\mathbb Z^2\), with unnormalized density \[g_t(k)=(2\pi t)^{-1}e^{-|k|^2/(2t)}.\] Indeed, the torus transition density is the sum of the Euclidean Gaussian densities, and conditioning on a lift divides each by that sum. Multiplication by \(p_t(x,x)\) in [eq:Ldelta] (#eq:Ldelta)–[eq:Rdelta] (#eq:Rdelta) cancels this normalizer. The mass weight is \(e^{-t}\), and the normalized holonomy trace is \[z_k=\cos(\pi k_1/2).\] In particular, the two windings \(k=(1,0),(-1,0)\) both have \(1-z_k=1\).

Equation reference: eq:Llower. For either of these two windings, the first lifted coordinate at time \(tu\) has the form \(x_1\pm u+\sqrt t\,b(u)\), where \(b\) is a standard unit Brownian bridge. Since \(a\) is \(2\pi\)-Lipschitz and \(\int_0^1 a(x_1\pm u)\,du=2\), \[\left|\frac{\widehat T}{t}-2\right|
 \leq2\pi\sqrt t\int_0^1|b(u)|\,du.\] Writing \(b(u)=B(u)-uB(1)\) gives \(\mathbb Eb(u)^2=u(1-u)\) and hence \[\mathbb E\int_0^1|b(u)|\,du
 \leq\left(\int_0^1u(1-u)\,du\right)^{1/2}
 =\frac1{\sqrt6}.\] Set \(\delta=1/100\). Uniformly in \(x\) and \(3\delta/4\leq t\leq\delta\), Markov’s inequality gives \[\mathbb P(\widehat T\leq\delta\mid k)
 \leq\frac{3\pi\sqrt\delta}{\sqrt6}
 <\frac{11}{28},\] where \(\pi<22/7\) and \(\sqrt6>12/5\) suffice for the last bound. Thus the probability of \(A\) is at least \(17/28\). All terms of \(L_\delta\) are nonnegative, so retaining these two windings yields \[
 L_\delta\geq\frac{17}{28}\frac{99}{100}\frac1\pi
 \int_{3\delta/4}^{\delta}t^{-2}e^{-1/(2t)}\,dt.\]

Equation reference: eq:strictcounterexample. For the other side, put \(q_t=e^{-1/(2t)}\). For \(0<q\leq1/1000\), the elementary Gaussian-lattice bound \[\begin{aligned}
 \sum_{\substack{k\in\mathbb Z^2\\|k|^2\geq2}}q^{|k|^2}
 &\leq \left(1+\frac{2q}{1-q^3}\right)^2-1-4q\\
 &=\frac{4q^4}{1-q^3}+\frac{4q^2}{(1-q^3)^2}
 \leq5q^2\end{aligned}\] follows from \(n^2\geq1+3(n-1)\) for integers \(n\geq1\). The final scalar bound can be checked by setting \(q=1/1000\) in the increasing coefficient after division by \(q^2\). Because \(0\leq1-z_k\leq2\), and the remaining unit windings have \(z_k=1\), dropping \(e^{-t}\leq1\) gives \[R_\delta\leq\frac1{4\pi}\int_0^\delta
 t^{-2}(2q_t+20q_t^2)\,dt
 =\frac{e^{-50}}{\pi}\bigl(1+5e^{-50}\bigr).\] The integrals are elementary: \[\int_0^\delta t^{-2}e^{-1/(2t)}\,dt=2e^{-50},
 \qquad
 \int_{3\delta/4}^{\delta}t^{-2}e^{-1/(2t)}\,dt
 =2e^{-50}(1-e^{-50/3}).\] Using \(e^{-50}<1/1000\), \(e^{-50/3}<1/1000\), and [eq:Llower] (#eq:Llower), we obtain the strict contradiction \[
 L_\delta>
 \frac{1681317}{1400000}\frac{e^{-50}}{\pi}
 >
 \frac{201}{200}\frac{e^{-50}}{\pi}
 > R_\delta.\] All quantities are positive; their small absolute scale plays no role in this exact inequality. Independent symbolic calculations verified the integrals, the lattice coefficient bound, and the positive rational gap \(274317/1400000\). This is an analytic probability bound, not a simulation. It disproves the displayed intermediate inequality while leaving the final conformal-invariance assertion open to the repair below.

## A valid local repair

Equation reference: eq:matrixrepair, eq:cutoffrepair. The valid identity and bound are \[
 \operatorname{Re}(1-\operatorname{tr}U)
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\bigl((I-U)^*(I-U)\bigr)
 \leq\tfrac12\|I-U\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2.\] For fixed rank, equivalent matrix norms only change a constant. Let \(A\) denote the cutoff event occurring in the first moment on page 40. Reversing the bridge sends \(U\) to \(U^*\) and preserves \(A\) and the mass weight. Its expectation is therefore real. Taking real parts before dropping the indicator gives \[\begin{aligned}
 0&\leq \mathbb E^m_{t,x,x}
       [\mathbf1_A(1-\operatorname{tr}U)]\notag\\
  &\leq\tfrac12\mathbb E^m_{t,x,x}\|I-U\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2
   \leq Ct^2. \end{aligned}\] The last bound is Proposition 3.1, page 13, with exponent two. In dimension two, the diagonal heat kernel is \(O(t^{-1})\) at small time, so \[\int_0^\delta\!\int_M
       \frac{p_t(x,x)}{t}\,Ct^2\,dx\,dt=O(\delta).\] This proves the required first-moment convergence by the matrix estimate, without the false scalar substitution.

For the second moment the inequality printed on page 40, \(1-|z|^2\leq2\operatorname{Re}(1-z)\), is valid for \(|z|\leq1\). Using [eq:matrixrepair] (#eq:matrixrepair) gives the same \(O(\delta)\) control. Thus \(\mathbb EX_\delta\to1\) and \(\mathbb E|X_\delta|^2\to1\), whence \(\mathbb E|X_\delta-1|^2\to0\). The remaining comparison uses the already established bound \(|Q_\delta|\leq1\).

## Counterevidence and boundaries

For a rank-one unitary holonomy, \(|\operatorname{tr}U|=1\), and [eq:traceid] (#eq:traceid) is an equality without the additional term. That special case explains why a scalar formula can look plausible, but Theorem 7.1 allows arbitrary rank. Equation (20) and the matrix estimates elsewhere in the paper provide the needed repair. The original images on pages 13, 32, 38–40 were inspected; the issue is present in the PDF and is not a conversion artifact. The repair addresses this cutoff step under the theorem’s hypotheses, conditional on the preceding loop-measure and holonomy results. It does not independently reprove every external ingredient of the conformal-invariance theorem.

# Coverage, dependencies, and limits of this review

The complete extracted text of all 49 source pages was read in this scientific conversation, including both appendices and the reference list. The source identity and all four manifest-listed payload files were hash-checked. Text reading, file parsing, visual inspection, and independent calculation are recorded separately.

The principal dependency chain examined was the following. The covariant Feynman–Kac formula identifies the heat kernel. Proposition 3.1 provides an \(O(t)\) holonomy fluctuation and an \(O(t^2)\) mean defect, the latter using reversal and the isometry property. Together with \(p_t(x,x)=O(t^{-d/2})\), these give the integrable small-loop power \(t^{1-d/2}\) for \(d<4\). The normalized Poisson products are controlled in even moments, leading to the determinant representation. The normalized kernel-dimension equality in the first part of Theorem 2.1 must be retained; the stronger nonzero-mass or nonempty-boundary condition is used for the loop-product assertion and the large-time behavior. The Gaussian covariance and mixture then lead to the Symanzik identities. The conformal result requires an additional cutoff comparison because the products are not absolutely convergent.

The boundary cases checked include trivial holonomy, deterministic connection laws, a constant positive mass, nonconstant mean-zero test sections, normalized versus unnormalized traces, rank one versus rank two, and the distinction between zero-mode assumptions and the stronger mass/boundary condition. In particular, the counterexample in Problem 1 lies wholly inside the assumptions; it does not rely on a zero-mass limit, a boundary singularity, or an unbounded test functional.

No further substantive defect is asserted. Reading the full text does not amount to independent certification of every stochastic-analysis estimate or cited theorem. The small-time rough-path arguments and the external heat-kernel and loop-measure results were examined as dependencies, but their original external proofs were not all reconstructed. The report’s decisive evidence is the two explicit counterexamples and the local repair arguments above. The reviewed arXiv PDF has not been established to be identical to the final journal article.
