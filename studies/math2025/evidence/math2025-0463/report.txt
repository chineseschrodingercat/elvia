Abstract: The acquired author manuscript is reviewed with emphasis on its expected characteristic polynomials, iterative selection and root bounds. A wrong coefficient index in the supplied polynomial-computation formula is a Major defect in that computational derivation: an exact two-dimensional example produces a different polynomial whose largest root cannot certify any feasible residual. The correct index is derived explicitly. Four Minor issues concern a zero-direction guard, a factorial normalization and two degenerate parameter ranges. The example does not refute the interlacing existence theorem with the correctly defined polynomial. All conclusions are limited to arXiv:2312.01715v2. Scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Main constructions and checks

For \(A\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}\), \(B\in\mathbb R^{n\times d_B}\) and \(C\in\mathbb R^{n_C\times d}\), define \[Q_S=I_n-B_{:,S}B_{:,S}^{\dagger},\qquad
 P_R=I_d-C_{R,:}^{\dagger}C_{R,:}.\] The residual characteristic polynomial is \(p_{S,R}(x)=\det(xI_d-P_RA^TQ_SAP_R)\). The polynomial \(P_{k,r}\) on p. 4 is the sum of these polynomials weighted by \(\det(B_{:,S}^TB_{:,S})\det(C_{R,:}C_{R,:}^T)\). When \(k\leq\operatorname{rank}B\) and \(r\leq\operatorname{rank}C\), some such weights are positive; rank-deficient subsets have zero weight. Thus a zero-volume subset must not be mistaken for a positive-weight interlacing candidate.

The block determinant in Proposition 3.3 has positive semidefinite variable coefficients. Subject to the stated stability-preserving operations and nonvanishing, its derivative/specialization representation gives real-rootedness. Once real-rootedness is known, nonnegative weights and the common sign of the summands on \(x<0\) rule out negative roots. In Lemma 4.1, replacing a column projection changes the associated block Gram matrix by a rank-one term; the convex combination is represented by \((1+\partial_t)\) applied to a determinant with a positive semidefinite \(t\) coefficient. This supports the common-interlacing argument for nonzero projected directions. The successive largest-root comparisons then terminate in the squared spectral norm of the residual.

The error accumulation argument of Theorem 1.2 also has a direct interpretation: minimizing numbers that are each within \(\eta\) of the true largest roots incurs at most \(2\eta\) relative to the best child at each selection. Summing over \(k+r\) steps gives the stated additive \(2(k+r)\eta\), provided the polynomials and candidate set are correct. Problem 1 concerns a supplied route for computing those polynomials, rather than this elementary accumulation inequality.

Exact direct enumeration was compared with formula (3.5b) and the corrected coefficient extraction for six choices \((k,r)\in\{0,1,2\}\times\{0,1\}\) using \[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
 B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
 C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}.\] All agreed exactly, including dependent source columns and rows. Four additional exact instances checked the mixed derivative identity (3.9), using a nonsymmetric \(3\times3\) integer target and \(B=C=I_3\). These finite checks support the inspected derivations but do not substitute for their general proofs.

# Problems

Problem 1 (Major: the wrong coefficient index in (A.16)).  After differentiating \(\widehat k\) times with respect to \(y\) and setting \(y=0\), equation (A.16) on p. 38 uses \(\mu_{j,c_1,\widehat r}\) in place of \(\mu_{j,c_1,\widehat k}\). This computes a different polynomial when the two indices differ.

The preceding definition (A.15) is \[g(x,y,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n_C+n}
 \sum_{c_1=0}^{j}\sum_{c_2=0}^{j-c_1}
 \mu_{j,c_1,c_2}\,x^{n_C+n-j}z^{c_1}y^{c_2}.\] The identity \(\left.\partial_y^{\widehat k}y^{c_2}\right|_{y=0}
=\widehat k!\,\mathbf 1_{\{c_2=\widehat k\}}\) therefore fixes the last index unambiguously. The corrected version of the final expression is \[\begin{aligned}
 &\frac{x^{d-n+\widehat k}}{(n_C-\widehat r)!}
 \sum_{j=\widehat k+n_C-\widehat r}^{n_C+n}
 \sum_{c_1=n_C-\widehat r}^{j-\widehat k}
 \frac{(-1)^{c_1-n_C}c_1!}{(c_1-n_C+\widehat r)!}\\[-2pt]
 &\hspace{35mm}\cdot
 \mu_{j,c_1,\widehat k}\,
 x^{n-j+c_1+\widehat r}.\end{aligned}\] The derivative factorial cancels the \(\widehat k!\) in (A.14). The sign follows by evaluating the remaining \(z\) power at \(z=-x\). No change of notation in (A.15) accounts for the printed \(\widehat r\) index.

For an exact admissible example take \[n=d=d_B=2,\quad n_C=1,\quad
 A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix},\quad B=I_2,\quad C=0_{1\times2},
 \quad k=1,\quad r=0,\quad S=R=\varnothing.\] Here \(\operatorname{rank}B=2\), \(\operatorname{rank}C=0\), so all rank conditions hold. The empty row selection uses the explicitly stated empty-determinant convention. Directly from Definition 1.1, \[P_{1,0}(x)=x(x-4)+x(x-1)=2x^2-5x.\] Both column weights are one. In Appendix A.6 the same instance gives \[V=I_2,\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}(1,4,0),\qquad
 g(x,y,z)=(x+z)(x+y-1)(x+y-4).\] The printed final formula instead selects \(\mu_{2,1,0}=-5\) and \(\mu_{3,1,0}=4\), giving \[P_{\mathrm{printed}}(x)=-5x^2+4x.\] Its largest root is \(4/5\), whereas the correct polynomial has largest root \(5/2\). The two feasible residual squared norms are \(4\) and \(1\). Thus \(4/5\) cannot certify any feasible choice in this example. The leading coefficient also changes from the positive total volume weight \(2\) to \(-5\); multiplying by a nonzero scalar cannot repair the wrong root location.

This is a Major defect in the explicit computation and certificate derivation used for Lemma 4.2 and the polynomial evaluation in Algorithm 1. It is locally repairable by the index correction above, which follows for all coefficient arrays and was additionally checked on the six stated configurations. The abstract Algorithm 1 calls for the correctly defined \(P\); no full trajectory violating its guarantee with correctly computed polynomials is claimed. Theorem 1.1 and determinant formula (3.5b) are not refuted by this example.

Problem 2 (Minor: zero directions must be excluded before normalization).  Algorithm 1, p. 21, normalizes every remaining candidate in lines 3 and 10, but excludes zero projected directions only later in lines 5 and 12.

For example, let \(A=I_2\), \(B=[e_1,0,e_2]\), \(C=0_{1\times2}\), \(k=1\) and \(r=0\). The rank conditions hold, yet line 3 visits the second column and requests \(q_2=0/0\). The Moore–Penrose convention elsewhere does not define this normalization.

Restrict candidate preparation and polynomial evaluation to \(\{i:Q_Sb_i\neq0\}\) and \(\{i:P_Rc_i\neq0\}\) before dividing. This is exactly the admissible-set restriction already present in Lemma 4.1. Until the desired rank is reached, the rank assumptions guarantee that an admissible direction remains. The omission is Minor because the proof supplies the correct domain and this guard restores its intended implementation without changing the interlacing argument. No floating-point tolerance rule is prescribed or verified here.

Problem 3 (Minor: the factorial in Lemma A.2 is reversed).  The displayed identity on p. 35 places \(1/k!\) in front of the sum of principal-submatrix characteristic polynomials and equates it to the \(k\)th derivative. The correct identity is \[\sum_{\substack{S\subset[d]\\|S|=d-k}}
 \det(xI_{d-k}-M_{S,S})
 =\frac1{k!}\,\partial_x^k\det(xI_d-M).\]

Take \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)\) and \(k=2\). The sum is \(3x-6\), so the printed left side is \(3x/2-3\), whereas the derivative is \(6x-12\). Each deletion set is counted \(k!\) times by repeated differentiation, giving the corrected factor.

The following proof of Proposition 3.5 already uses the correct normalization, as seen in the denominator \((k-|S|)!\) immediately below (A.9). Moreover, an overall positive scalar does not change roots. This is therefore a Minor displayed-identity error, not an established failure of Proposition 3.5 or the submatrix bound.

Problem 4 (Minor: the definition of \(\alpha\) needs a projected-zero case).  Theorem 1.3 on p. 6 and Lemma 5.2 on p. 24 define \(\alpha\) using \(U^TAA^TU/\|U^TAA^TU\|_2\) without separately excluding a zero denominator.

For \(A=e_3\in\mathbb R^3\) and \(B=[e_1,e_2]\), one may take \(U=B\), \(m=2\) and \(k=1\). The target is nonzero but \(U^TAA^TU=0\), so the displayed normalized matrix is undefined. It is consequently inappropriate to evaluate the condition involving \(\alpha\) for this input without a convention.

The case is harmless once stated correctly. If \(BB^\dagger A=0\), then every selected-column projection also annihilates \(A\), and the residual is exactly \(A\). Handle this case first, and define \(\alpha\) only when the normalization denominator is positive. This repairs the domain statement; it does not furnish a counterexample to the nondegenerate reconstruction inequality.

Problem 5 (Minor: Lemma 6.1 needs an upper bound on the differentiation order).  On p. 27 the integer \(k\) is required only to exceed \(\beta d/(1+\beta)\). For \(k\geq d\), the iterated Laguerre derivative is constant or zero, so its largest root is not defined by the paper’s stated conventions.

For \[d=2,\qquad p(x)=(x-\tfrac14)(x-\tfrac34),\qquad \beta=\tfrac12,
 \qquad L=\partial_x\,x\,\partial_x,\] the choice \(k=2\) satisfies \(k>2/3\), but \(L^2p=4\). At \(k=3\), \(L^3p=0\), and the radical in the displayed right side is also outside its real range. The usual nonconstant statement should specify \[\frac{\beta d}{1+\beta}<k<d,\] or explicitly handle the exhausted-degree cases with separate conventions.

The application to Theorem 1.4 on p. 29 uses deletion order \(d-k\) with positive retained size \(k\), so it remains below \(d\). This domain omission does not refute that theorem. At \(\beta=0\), the minimizing \(b\) in the proof is the endpoint \(b=1\), which is outside the open domain \(b>1\); the infimum is obtained by the one-sided limit. That endpoint can be treated directly because all original roots are zero.

# Root bounds and assessment limits

The inspected GCSS argument separates the target into its part in \(\operatorname{range}B\) and its orthogonal complement. The cross terms vanish in the residual Gram matrix, and the operator-norm triangle inequality gives Lemma 5.1. The multiaffine expression in Proposition 5.1 uses complement-indexed coefficients and substitution \(z_i=x/2\); the factor of two from differentiating a squared variable explains this substitution. The barrier result then applies conditional on its cited hypotheses, with the positive normalization addressed in Problem 4.

For the square-submatrix result, the derivative order counts deleted rows and columns, whereas Theorem 1.4 counts retained indices. With roots in \([0,1]\), the paired roots of \(p(x^2)\) have mean zero and second moment \(\beta\). The stated barrier estimate yields \[f(b)=(1-\beta)(1-k/d)
       +(1-k/d)(b+\beta-1)
       +\frac{(k/d)\beta}{b+\beta-1}.\] Minimizing the last two terms gives the displayed root bound in the nonconstant range, with the \(\beta=0\) endpoint treated by a limit. These checks do not independently reprove the cited barrier theorem itself.

The report assessment is Major because (A.16), as printed, fails to compute the polynomial on which a central algorithmic certificate relies. The explicit correction restores that coefficient extraction. Problems 2–5 are separately calibrated Minor: the demonstrated effects are local domain or normalization defects, with the relevant repaired reasoning stated above. No counterexample to the correctly formulated interlacing existence theorem, the nondegenerate GCSS inequality or the retained-submatrix bound has been established.
