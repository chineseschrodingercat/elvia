Abstract: This initial review identifies one supported Minor issue: an auxiliary mass-vanishing assertion in the proof of Proposition 1.4 does not follow from the stated hypotheses, which control the volume of the supporting manifolds but do not control current multiplicities. A connected, smooth, bounded-mass example satisfies every explicit hypothesis and contradicts that assertion. It does not contradict the proposition’s conclusion or either main theorem. The main applications have additional mass control, and a local repair is available. The review also checks the sharp barycenter bound independently and follows the compactness, intrinsic-distance, and entropy arguments. No Major defect was established. The scientific decision remains pending; this initial review is not independent acceptance.

# Claims, hypotheses, and proof structure

Theorem 0.1 concerns a connected closed oriented real hyperbolic \(n\)-manifold \((M,g_0)\), \(n\geq3\), and smooth Riemannian metrics \(g_i\) with \(\operatorname{Vol}(M,g_i)=\operatorname{Vol}(M,g_0)\) and \(h(g_i)\to n-1\). It asserts the existence of smooth exceptional regions \(Z_i\) with both volume and boundary area tending to zero, such that the complements converge in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense to \((M,g_0)\). The metric on a complement is its intrinsic path metric. The theorem does not assert convergence of the original, unmodified ambient metric spaces.

Theorem 0.2, made precise in Definition 2.7 and Theorem 2.8 (pp. 894–898), identifies every spherical Plateau limit up to intrinsic isomorphism with \[\left(M,g'\right),\qquad
 g'=\frac{(n-1)^2}{4n}g_0 .\] An isometry for the induced length metric, preserving the current, is weaker than an isometry for the original metric on the current space. The paper expressly leaves the stronger uniqueness question open. The admissible homotopy class is the marked class inducing the identity on the fundamental group, as specified on p. 891; it is not an arbitrary automorphism class.

The essential chain is as follows. The regular representation gives the spherical quotient; a degree-one barycenter map gives the sharp spherical-volume lower bound. Near equality yields almost isometry on regions of almost full volume. Proposition 1.4 turns suitable flat limits and maps into a bi-Lipschitz identification, with a one-sided intrinsic length bound. The stronger neighborhood estimate gives intrinsic uniqueness in Theorem 2.8. For the entropy theorem, Theorem 3.2 additionally removes thin regions to obtain genuine Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of the intrinsic complements. Theorem 3.5 excludes every strict distance shortcut in the limit by producing a uniform entropy increase. Finally Theorem 3.7 and Lemma 3.8 give the asserted measured convergence.

Theorem 3.5 itself is stated for \(n\geq2\), with a bi-Lipschitz, 1-Lipschitz bijection from the hyperbolic metric to the limiting metric, and, for every sufficiently small error and all sufficiently large indices, a global homotopy equivalence whose restriction to an open set is an error-isometry for its intrinsic metric. Those quantifiers and the global homotopy condition are needed to make the entropy gap uniform and to preserve distinct loop classes.

# Problem 1: missing multiplicity control in the auxiliary mass claim

Severity: Minor. Location: Assumption 1.3(a)–(e), printed p. 885 (physical p. 9); equation (3) and the displayed chain immediately following it, p. 887 (physical p. 11); the conversion of small geometric volume into small current mass in the subsequent curve-lifting argument, p. 888.

Assumption 1.3 describes the Riemannian geometry of \(N_i=\operatorname{spt}S_i\) and the volume of its good and bad regions. It does not say that \(S_i=[\![N_i]\!]\), or impose convergence of \(\mathbf M(S_i)\) to \(\operatorname{Vol}(N,h)\). For an integer rectifiable current on a Riemannian support, the mass includes the absolute integer multiplicity. This is explicit in the intrinsic representation and mass formulas of Ambrosio–Kirchheim [Retained latex: \cite[Theorems 9.1 and 9.5]{AK}]. Small unweighted volume therefore does not imply small mass.

Equation (3) claims, with \(O_\eta\) the \(\eta\)-neighborhood of \(\operatorname{spt}S_\infty\) in the complete ambient space, \[\lim_i\|S_i\|(E\setminus O_\eta)=0
 \quad\hbox{for every fixed }\eta>0.\] Its displayed justification identifies \(\lim_i\mathbf M(S_i)\) with \(\operatorname{Vol}(N,h)\). Both claims fail under all of the literal hypotheses, as the following example shows.

## A connected example satisfying every stated hypothesis

Fix a connected closed oriented Riemannian three-manifold \(N\); the round three-sphere is a concrete choice. The construction also works with a closed oriented hyperbolic three-manifold. Put \[E=N\times\mathbb R^4,\qquad S_\infty=[\![N\times\{0\}]\!],
 \qquad r_i=i^{-1},\quad \epsilon_i=i^{-5},\quad m_i=i^3
 \quad(i\geq4),\] and use the product Riemannian distance on \(E\). Choose \(p\in N\). In the vertical fiber \(\{p\}\times\mathbb R^4\), place a round three-sphere of radius \(r_i\), centered at a vector of norm one. Join it to \(N\times\{0\}\) by a smooth three-dimensional neck of cross-sectional radius \(\epsilon_i\) and bounded length, removing caps of radius comparable to \(\epsilon_i\) at the two ends. Denote the resulting oriented connected closed embedded three-manifold by \(N_i\).

Here the neck may be constructed as the side boundary of a thin four-dimensional handle along an embedded arc. Its three-dimensional volume is \(O(\epsilon_i^2)\), and its four-dimensional filling volume is \(O(\epsilon_i^3)\). Normal frames can be rotated along the arc to join the horizontal cap to the vertical cap; the product ambient dimension is seven. Smoothing the two junctions changes these estimates only by uniform constants. The modification is identical to \(N\times\{0\}\) outside an \(O(\epsilon_i)\)-ball about \(p\).

Let \(B_i\) be the spherical part of \(N_i\) with the small attaching cap removed. It lies entirely in the vertical fiber. Its orientation agrees with that of \(N_i\), and \[\operatorname{Vol}(B_i)=2\pi^2 r_i^3-O(\epsilon_i^3),\qquad
 \operatorname{Area}(\partial B_i)=O(\epsilon_i^2).\] Set \[S_i=[\![N_i]\!]+(m_i-1)[\![B_i]\!].\] Thus the multiplicity is one on the neck and the main component and \(m_i\) on \(B_i\). These are integral currents, with \[\operatorname{spt}S_i=N_i,\qquad
 \mathbf M(\partial S_i)=O(m_i\epsilon_i^2)=O(i^{-7})\longrightarrow0.\] In particular, even a uniform mass and boundary-mass bound would not exclude the example.

The natural currents \([\![N_i]\!]\) converge flatly to \([\![N\times\{0\}]\!]\). Indeed the difference is filled by the thin handle and the four-ball bounded by the spherical component, with total filling mass \(O(\epsilon_i^3+r_i^4)\). If \(Q_i\) is the deleted spherical cap, then \[[\![B_i]\!]=\partial[\![\mathbb B^4_{r_i}]\!]-[\![Q_i]\!],
 \qquad \mathbf M(Q_i)=O(\epsilon_i^3).\] Consequently \[\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal F_E(S_i-S_\infty)
 &\leq C(\epsilon_i^3+r_i^4)
       +C m_i(r_i^4+\epsilon_i^3)\\
 &\longrightarrow0 ,\end{aligned}\] because \(m_ir_i^4=i^{-1}\) and \(m_i\epsilon_i^3=i^{-12}\).

Let \(\varphi_i\) be the restriction of the product projection \(E\to N\). It is smooth and 1-Lipschitz for the restricted ambient metric, as required in Assumption 1.3(c). The oriented manifold \(N_i\) is homologous in \(E\) to \(N\times\{0\}\), while the projection is constant on \(B_i\). It follows exactly, not merely in the limit, that \[(\varphi_i)_\# S_i=[\![N]\!] .\] Let \(R_i\) be the unchanged part of \(N\times\{0\}\) outside a slightly larger cap neighborhood. The metric is smooth there and \(\varphi_i\) is exactly a Riemannian isometry. Moreover, \[\operatorname{Vol}(R_i)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Vol}(N),\qquad
 \operatorname{Vol}(N_i\setminus R_i)=O(r_i^3+\epsilon_i^2)\longrightarrow0.\] This verifies (d) and (e). The induced intrinsic metric on \(N_i\) is its smooth Riemannian metric, verifying (a), and \(\partial N_i=\varnothing\), verifying (b). The complete ambient space, the flat convergence, the orientation, and the connectedness requirements have all been retained.

For the fixed value \(\eta=1/4\), all of \(B_i\) lies outside \(O_\eta\) for sufficiently large \(i\). Therefore \[\liminf_i\|S_i\|(E\setminus O_{1/4})
 \ \geq\ \lim_i m_i\operatorname{Vol}(B_i)=2\pi^2>0 .\] In fact \[\mathbf M(S_i)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Vol}(N)+2\pi^2 ,\] which also directly contradicts the mass identification used below equation (3). The supporting manifolds still have volumes converging to \(\operatorname{Vol}(N)\).

## Impact and repair boundary

The example disproves the stated auxiliary mass claim and its displayed derivation. It does not disprove the conclusion of Proposition 1.4: its limiting projection is an isometry. Nor is it a counterexample to Theorems 0.1 or 0.2.

A clean repair is to state explicitly the intended unit-multiplicity condition \(S_i=[\![N_i]\!]\), with the compatible orientation, and carry it through the proof. An alternative is to impose appropriate mass convergence and formulate each estimate with multiplicities; that alternative requires auditing the area and curve-lifting arguments, not simply replacing the term volume once.

The principal applications already have the relevant extra control. In Theorem 3.2, \(D_i=[\![N_i]\!]\) is explicitly defined on p. 901, and the volumes converge to the target value. In Theorem 2.8, pp. 896–897, the minimizing sequence has \(\mathbf M(C_i)\to\operatorname{SphereVol}(M)\), and equation (20) gives a discarded part of vanishing mass before restriction to \(D_i\). These application-specific facts should be stated at the point where Proposition 1.4 is invoked. The demonstrated impact is a repairable overstatement in the auxiliary hypotheses and proof; no failure of the main entropy or intrinsic-uniqueness conclusion has been established. This is the reason for the Minor classification.

# Independent checks of the barycenter estimates

## The modified distance and differential inequality

The smooth modified distance on p. 893 can be checked explicitly: for curvature minus one and \(r=\operatorname{dist}(w,\cdot)\), take \(\rho=\log\cosh r\). Away from \(w\), \[\rho'=\tanh r,\qquad \rho''=\operatorname{sech}^2r,\qquad
 \operatorname{Hess}r=\coth r\,(g_0-dr\otimes dr).\] Consequently \[\operatorname{Hess}\rho
   =g_0-d\rho\otimes d\rho .\] The even radial expansion makes \(\rho\) smooth at \(w\), where the same identity extends. Thus the claimed lower bound holds already for this choice of the parameter. Strict convexity and properness of the finite positive weighted sum give its unique barycenter.

With the notation of [Retained latex: \cite[pp.~16--19]{Song}], set \(H=\sum_\gamma f(\gamma)^2\,d\rho_{\gamma o}\otimes
d\rho_{\gamma o}\) and \(K=\sum_\gamma f(\gamma)^2\operatorname{Hess}\rho_{\gamma o}\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}H<1\) and \(K\geq I-H>0\). Differentiating the barycenter equation, followed by Cauchy–Schwarz, gives \[\left|\operatorname{Jac}\operatorname{Bar}\right|
 \leq 2^n\frac{\sqrt{\det H}}{\det K}
 \leq 2^n\frac{\sqrt{\det H}}{\det(I-H)}.\] The finite-dimensional tangent plane is the relevant domain; no determinant on the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is being taken.

## A direct proof of the sharp matrix bound

For \(n\geq3\), let the nonnegative numbers \(\mu_j\) satisfy \(\sum_{j=1}^n\mu_j\leq1\), and set \[F(\mu)=\prod_{j=1}^n\frac{\sqrt{\mu_j}}{1-\mu_j}.\] The sharp bound is \[F(\mu)\leq\frac{n^{n/2}}{(n-1)^n}.
 \tag{*}\] Each positive factor increases in its argument, so an interior maximum must have trace one. At an interior stationary point on that face, every eigenvalue satisfies \[\frac{1+\mu_j}{2\mu_j(1-\mu_j)}=\lambda .\] There can be at most two distinct values \(a<b\). Equality of these derivatives gives \[(1+a)b(1-b)-(1+b)a(1-a)
       =(b-a)(1-a-b-ab)=0 .\] Hence \(b=(1-a)/(1+a)\). Two occurrences of \(b\), together with one \(a\), are impossible because \[2b+a-1=\frac{(1-a)^2}{1+a}>0.\] If \(b\) occurs once, the trace condition is \((n-1)a+b=1\), which reduces to \[(n-1)a^2+(n-3)a=0.\] This has no positive solution for \(n\geq3\). Thus the only interior stationary point has all eigenvalues equal to \(1/n\).

On a boundary face with a zero eigenvalue and all eigenvalues bounded away from one, the limiting product is zero. At a vertex where \(\mu_1\to1\), write \(\delta=1-\mu_1\), so that \(\sum_{j>1}\mu_j\leq\delta\). The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives \[F(\mu)\leq
 \frac{(\delta/(n-1))^{(n-1)/2}}
      {\delta(1-\delta)^{n-1}}.\] Its upper limit is zero for \(n>3\) and at most \(1/2\) for \(n=3\). In the latter case the isotropic value is \(\sqrt{27}/8>1/2\), since \(27/64>1/4\). This proves (*) and the uniqueness of its maximum, including the boundary cases which require \(n\geq3\).

It also proves the qualitative stability needed here: near-maximal \(F\) forces the eigenvalues to approach \(1/n\). Since \[\|d\operatorname{Bar}\|\leq
 \frac{2\sqrt{\mu_{\max}}}{1-\mu_{\max}},
 \qquad B=\frac{2\sqrt n}{n-1},\] near equality in the Jacobian bound gives an upper bound \(B+o(1)\) for every singular value and a product \(B^n-o(1)\). Each singular value is therefore also at least \(B-o(1)\). The variation estimate for \(H\) and a fixed spectral gap propagate the upper bound to short neighboring curves, as checked in [Retained latex: \cite[pp.~17--20]{Song}]. This verifies both parts of Lemma 2.6, with the exponent \(1/2\) in the length estimate. A typographical \(n/2\) in an intermediate line of the acquired reference’s proof is not present in the statement used by the reviewed paper and is not reported as a defect of this paper.

# Proof-level coverage and counterchecks

## Currents, compactness, and the almost-isometry proposition

Sections 1.1–1.2 were checked with particular attention to support versus positive-density set, orientation, integer multiplicity, restricted ambient distance, induced intrinsic distance, and flat versus weak convergence. Sormani’s Theorem 6.1 [Retained latex: \cite[pp.~30--32]{Sormani}] supplies the limiting Lipschitz map for a fixed compact target. The proof of Basso–Creutz–Soultanis, Lemma 7.3 [Retained latex: \cite[pp.~20--21]{BCS}], gives compatibility with pushforwards. The paper explains its extension through an injective \(L^\infty\) ambient space. The fixed-target current and its boundary have finite mass; the linear homotopy between bounded extensions controls the difference by integrals of their pointwise displacement against those two mass measures. This gives the required limiting pushforward without assuming Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

Wenger’s original compactness statement [Retained latex: \cite[Theorem 1.2, p.~2; proof, pp.~17--19]{Wenger}] requires uniform mass, boundary mass, and diameter bounds and allows nonzero boundaries. The boundaryless formulation in the paper’s Theorem 1.1 is a special case; the applications with vanishing boundary mass use this general original statement. The slicing formulas and preservation of integer multiplicities were checked in [Retained latex: \cite[Theorems 5.6--5.7 and 9.7]{AK}].

With the intended multiplicity control, the proof of Proposition 1.4 uses the area formula to select a region of essentially single-sheeted coverage, coarea to lift short curves while avoiding the boundary and the small bad region, and compactness to obtain a bijective limit with a 1-Lipschitz inverse for intrinsic lengths. For a precise choice of endpoints, one may also discard the preimage of the image of the bad region; its image has vanishing volume by the uniform Lipschitz bound. Injectivity merely on the retained region would not by itself rule out another preimage in the discarded region. The stronger local Lipschitz hypothesis in part (2) is essential: the shortcut example in Remark 1.5 rules out silently replacing ambient distance by intrinsic length.

## Spherical volume and intrinsic uniqueness

For the torsion-free fundamental group, the regular action on the unit sphere is free and proper. Matrix coefficients of the regular representation vanish at infinity, and a vector fixed by an infinite-order translation cannot be square summable. The quotient diameter bound and the current boundary condition give the required compactness.

The finite-support and polyhedral approximations were checked against [Retained latex: \cite[Lemma 1.6, pp.~13--14]{Song}]. In applying them to admissible maps, one retains the marked homotopy class and makes a generic perturbation in the available high codimension; arbitrary flat approximation alone would not establish those additional properties. The barycenter has degree one, so its Jacobian bound forces the spherical-volume lower bound. The opposite inequality comes from the entropy maps, and the two constants match after rescaling by \((n-1)^2/(4n)\); see [Retained latex: \cite[Theorem 4.1, pp.~27--29]{Song}].

In Theorem 2.8, the mass lower bound and minimizing hypothesis force near equality of the barycenter Jacobian on regions of asymptotically full mass. A coarea choice can be made with collar radii tending to zero slowly enough that discarded mass divided by the radius still tends to zero. This supplies vanishing boundary mass and the local almost-1-Lipschitz estimate needed by Proposition 1.4(2). A short-curve estimate only controls such sufficiently small collars; there is no need to use an unjustified uniform assertion for every collar radius below one. The current-preserving limit then proves intrinsic isomorphism, with no promotion to equality of the original metric spaces.

## Entropy maps and construction of the convergent regions

For \(c>h(g)\), the exponential kernel defining \(\mathcal P_c\) is square integrable on the universal cover. Normalization is an orthogonal projection in the differential calculation, giving the Hilbert–Schmidt bound \(\sum_j|d\mathcal P_c(e_j)|^2\leq c^2/4\). The map sending a function to its \(L^2\)-norm on each fundamental domain translate is 1-Lipschitz by the reverse triangle inequality. Equivariance and the a.e. distance-gradient identity therefore give Lemma 3.1. These checks do not assume differentiability of distance at its cut locus.

After rescaling, \(c_i\to2\sqrt n\). The trace bound and arithmetic–geometric mean give the volume upper bound in (28); the spherical-volume lower bound then forces the singular values to approach one on a region of almost full volume. The same argument is applied to the barycenter composition. Coarea selects a small smooth boundary.

The metric \(\widehat d_i=\min\{\operatorname{dist}_{h_i},6\operatorname{diam}(M,g')\}\) is finite and locally unchanged, even when a region has several components. Thus it permits Wenger compactness. The inverse limit map bounds the limit diameter; on sufficiently small neighborhoods of that limit the cutoff is inactive. Slicing and the degree/Jacobian lower bound show that the remaining volume tends to the full target volume. This proves return to the original intrinsic metric in Step 3.

Step 4 is necessary: intrinsic flat convergence alone does not give Hausdorff convergence of all supporting sets. Nets approximating the limit and small neighborhoods of those nets supply a noncollapsing sequence after another coarea choice. For the intrinsic-ball lower bound, paths can be assembled along a finite chain of nearby net points approximating a limit geodesic. Starting from a point within distance \(t\) of the net, use the margin \(s>2t\) and points in a thinner inner neighborhood to keep the connectors inside the chosen region. The mass outside each such fixed inner neighborhood tends to zero. This gives the positive ball-volume lower bound needed for (36), without requiring a uniform inclusion of every ambient ball up to the outer boundary. Reapplying the limit construction can change the limiting metric space, as the paper correctly notes on p. 904. The homotopy equivalences then become error-isometries for the final intrinsic regions.

## Equidistribution, uniform entropy gap, and measured convergence

The original equidistribution hypotheses were checked in [Retained latex: \cite[Theorem 1.2, pp.~2--3, and pp.~5--6, 16--17]{EM}]. For a closed real hyperbolic manifold the lattice is cocompact, the relevant maximal compact subgroup has finite volume, and the semisimple group satisfies the required irreducibility condition. Pushing the compact-orbit averages to the unit tangent bundle gives Theorem 3.3. For the open set in Corollary 3.4, a continuous nonnegative bump supported in it justifies the positive lower bound; convergence for continuous functions is not being applied without justification to an arbitrary indicator.

If a random visitation fraction \(X\in[0,1]\) has \(\mathbb E X\geq c>0\), then \[\mathbb P(X\geq c/2)\geq\frac{c}{2-c}.\] This supplies a positive fraction of directions with a positive frequency of visits. Finitely many initial time blocks and the last incomplete block have vanishing relative contribution. The direction sphere is \(S^{n-1}\); the \(S^2\) notation on p. 906 is a harmless dimension-specific notation slip in an otherwise dimension-independent argument.

A strict shortcut for the limiting 1-Lipschitz map has a fixed positive length gap. Small-error isometries of the intrinsic regions lift sufficiently fine geodesic subdivisions, with homotopy controlled below the injectivity radius. The maps need not themselves be Lipschitz: the additive distortion bound controls the images of sufficiently short subpaths. Approximate endpoints and almost-minimizing paths suffice for open regions; the strict shortcut margin absorbs these errors. Bounded hyperbolic length leaves only finitely many relevant relative homotopy classes, so the shortcut constants may be taken uniformly after subselection, or minimized over that finite collection.

Positive-frequency use of the shortcut reduces the length of a positive-area family of long geodesics by a fixed proportion. A bounded fundamental domain converts this family into exponentially many distinct orbit points; the global homotopy equivalence preserves distinct loop classes. With a fixed \(\theta>0\), the resulting comparison has the form \[\#\{\hbox{classes of }g_i\hbox{-length at most }(1-\theta)L+O(1)\}
 \ \geq\ c_0 e^{(n-1)L}.\] It implies \(h(g_i)\geq(n-1)/(1-\theta)>n-1\) uniformly for large \(i\), giving Theorem 3.5.

Every subsequence then has a further subsequence with the same hyperbolic limit. Choosing exceptional regions by an error functional tending to zero upgrades the subsequential statement to Theorem 3.7 for the full sequence. The remaining regions have mass converging to the target mass. Portegies’s mass-measure lemma [Retained latex: \cite[Lemma 2.7, p.~6 in the acquired version]{Portegies}], cited as Lemma 2.1 in the paper’s published reference, applies with precisely this extra mass hypothesis. Together with the Hausdorff convergence already established, it gives the measured conclusion.
