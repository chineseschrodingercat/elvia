Abstract: Five Major proof issues are established in the bound first preprint: the Thue-inequality lemma omits a necessary finite-type restriction; the cubic-surface upper argument loses logarithmic factors; its lower argument can select only excluded zero points; the partial-two-torsion argument uses a field that need not be Galois; and the moment proof does not respect the stated range of its exponential-moment input. Each issue is supported by an explicit example or a quantified calculation. Three additional local errors are recorded. These findings do not constitute counterexamples to the principal density theorems. Some repairs can be carried through algebraically, while others require a replacement estimate or a new argument.

The family is the fixed integral short Weierstrass model \[E_D:\quad y^2=x^3+AD^2x+BD^3,\qquad D\text{ square-free},\] with fixed integers \(A,B\) and \(4A^3+27B^2\neq0\). The counting statements exclude the affine two-torsion points; their presence is not a counterexample. The model \(Dy^2=x^3+Ax+B\) has a different set of integral points. The irreducible cubic case is unconditional. Full rational two-torsion is also treated unconditionally. The report preserves these distinctions.

# The core Thue bound

Problem 1 (Major: Lemma 5.5 is false, including on the constructed image).  Page 15, Lemma 5.5 and its proof, assert a uniform finite bound for all integral pairs satisfying \(|F(X,Y)|\leq m\) when \(F\) is an integral binary form with nonzero discriminant. These assumptions allow infinitely many such pairs.

Take the nonsingular curve \(E:y^2=x^3+2x+1\) and its nontorsion-or-torsion unrestricted integral point \(P=(1,2)\); in particular \(y(P)\neq0\), so this point is not two-torsion. In Lemma 5.1 take \(D=M=1\) and \(k=0\), which satisfies the congruence modulo one. Equations (5.3)–(5.4) give \[F_P(X,Y)=X^4-6X^2Y^2+16XY^3-11Y^4
 =(X-Y)(X^3+X^2Y-5XY^2+11Y^3).\] Its invariants are \(I=-8\), \(J=-4\), and \(I^3-27J^2=-944\neq0\); its ordinary polynomial discriminant is \(-241664\). Nevertheless \(F_P(t,t)=0\) for every \(t\in\mathbb Z\). For every \(m\geq1\), the set counted in Lemma 5.5 is infinite. All assumptions hold, and the example occurs in the precise quartic construction used subsequently.

Thunder’s Theorem 2 requires finite type, which includes finiteness on nontrivial rational subspaces, not only finiteness of the two-dimensional area [thunder]. Here the line \(X=Y\) has infinite restricted measure. Thus the citation does not justify the assertion that checking the area alone suffices.

Equation (5.17), Lemma 5.6, and the final small-gcd estimates invoke this lemma. This is a Major defect in that proof chain. The points actually needed there have \(F(\alpha,\beta)=M\neq0\), and the transformed vector is primitive. These facts suggest a narrower replacement statement, but neither fact makes Lemma 5.5 true as printed. In particular, restricting to forms without rational linear factors would discard the example above from the image. A repair must prove or cite a suitable uniform bound for the required nonzero values, with all reducible quartics that arise included. Such a replacement was not established by this review; the density theorem itself has not been disproved.

# The cubic-surface count

Problem 2 (Major: logarithmic factors are lost in Lemma 3.5).  Page 9, the first displayed estimate in the proof of Lemma 3.5, claims \(\sum_{U\leq|u|<2U}f(|u|)\ll U\) from Lemma 3.2. For the allowed reducible cubics this is false.

Choose \[C(x_1,x_2)=x_1(x_1-x_2)(x_1+x_2).\] It is separable, \(C(1,0)=1\), and \(\Delta(C)=4\). For every odd prime \(p\) and \(v\geq1\), the function in (3.12) has \(f(p^v)=3\). Thus for odd \(n\), \(f(n)=3^{\omega(n)}\). The three-factor case of (3.6), equivalently its Euler-product mean-value calculation, gives \(\sum_{n\leq T}f(n)\asymp T(\log T)^2\). If the printed dyadic bound held for all \(U\), summing dyadically would instead give \(O(T)\), a contradiction. This is an asymptotic contradiction for one fixed allowed cubic, not a numerical estimate of an unknown constant.

The appropriate general bound at this step is \(U(\log(2U))^{\lambda-1}\). There is a second bookkeeping point: the next displayed estimate contains \(f(h_2)B/(h_1h_2)\), so its subsequent sum over \(h_1\) also contributes a harmonic logarithm. Retaining these factors in the written argument yields the conservative upper bound \[N^\circ(B)\ll B(\log(2B))^{2\lambda},\qquad \lambda=1,2,3.\] Indeed the first term has exponent \((\lambda-1)+1+\lambda=2\lambda\), while the second has exponent \((\lambda-1)+2=\lambda+1\leq2\lambda\). This does not prove the sharper stated exponent \(\max\{\lambda,2\}\) in Theorem 1.4.

The effect on the main density strategy can be bounded. Using this weaker cubic estimate in the remainder of Lemma 5.6 would give logarithmic exponents \(3,6,7\) for \(\lambda=1,2,3\), respectively, after including the stated \(r=0\) exceptions. For full two-torsion one could then choose the large-gcd cutoff parameter \(15\) in place of \(13\), giving \(7-15/2=-1/2\). This shows a possible route to preserve the main logarithmic density saving, conditional on repairing the other inputs. It does not repair Theorem 1.4 as stated or certify the exact exponent \(6\) in the full-torsion version of Lemma 5.6.

Problem 3 (Major: the lower-bound construction can lie entirely on the deleted lines).  Page 9, Lemma 3.6, passes from a nonzero short lattice vector obtained by Minkowski’s theorem to a contribution to \(N^\circ(B)\). Nonzero vectors need not have \(C(x_1,x_2)\neq0\), and the required coprimality is not supplied either.

Use the same separable cubic and an odd integer \(q=x_3\). The allowed root \(\alpha=0\bmod q^2\) produces the lattice \[\Lambda_q=\mathbb Z(q^2,0)+\mathbb Z(0,1).\] For any fixed constant \(c>0\) and \(q>c\), a vector in \(\Lambda_q\) with \(\max(|x_1|,|x_2|)\leq cq\) must have \(x_1=0\). Hence \(C(x_1,x_2)=0\) and the resulting \(x_4=0\), which is excluded by Theorem 1.4. For prime \(q>2c\), the other two roots \(1,-1\) similarly force \(x_1=x_2\) or \(x_1=-x_2\) throughout that box. Thus none of the three root lattices at such a prime supplies the admissible point claimed by the construction.

The exact finite check \(q=101\), \(c=10\) illustrates the first case; the argument above proves the obstruction for arbitrarily large \(q\). Minkowski’s theorem is not being contradicted: the short nonzero vectors exist, but lie on excluded lines. The lower estimate in Theorem 1.4 requires a new admissible construction, including coprimality and multiplicity control. The lower estimate for the elliptic-twist density has a separate proof in Lemma 5.8 using square-free values, and is not disproved by this issue.

# The partial-two-torsion field

Problem 4 (Major: the fields in Section 8 do not meet the Galois hypothesis).  Page 33 defines \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{A^2-4B})\), \(\alpha=(-A+\sqrt{A^2-4B})/2\), and \(L=K(\sqrt\alpha)\). The Artin-symbol description uses \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\) and \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K(\sqrt B))\). Page 35 then applies Theorem 6.1 with \(K_{21}=K\) and \(\alpha_{21}=\alpha R_{21}\). The claimed Galois setup need not exist.

In the model (8.1), take \(A=0\), \(B=-2\). The curve is \(y^2=x(x^2-2)\), and \(A^2-4B=8\) is not a rational square, as required. Then \[K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\qquad \alpha=\sqrt2,\qquad
 L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]2).\] The polynomial \(T^4-2\) is Eisenstein at \(2\). Its real root generates a real field that omits the conjugates \(\pm i\sqrt[4]2\); hence \(L/\mathbb Q\) is not Galois. Moreover \(\sqrt B=\sqrt{-2}\notin L\), so \(K(\sqrt B)\) is not even a subfield of \(L\). The displayed relative Galois group is consequently undefined.

The failure also reaches the actual theorem invocation. The admissible fixed parameter choice \(\delta=\gamma_1=\gamma_2=\widetilde D=1\) has \(R_{21}=1\). Then \(K_{21}(\sqrt{\alpha_{21}})=L\) violates the explicit Galois assumption of Theorem 6.1. This concerns an allowed partial-two-torsion curve, including a subcase intended to be unconditional.

A natural repair starts from \(K'=K(\sqrt B)\) and \(L'=K'(\sqrt\alpha)\), which contains both conjugate square roots and is a splitting field over \(\mathbb Q\). The character must be defined on the primes splitting in \(K'\), with its support, discriminant bound, and parameters in Theorem 6.1 checked again. The non-square-root cases need not be declared false; rather, the written Galois/character argument does not apply as specified. This repair was identified, but the full revised character-sum proof was not independently completed.

# Moment quantifiers

Problem 5 (Major: the range of Theorem 2.5 is not respected).  Page 5, the proof of Theorem 2.4 sets \(m=qk\sigma\log c\) and then \(q=1/\varepsilon\). It uses \(m\ll\log\log\log N\) as if this implied \(m<C'\log\log\log N\), the actual hypothesis on page 4. These are different quantified bounds.

Put \(a=\sigma\log c>0\) and \(L=\log\log\log N\). For any fixed constants \(a,C'>0\), choose \[\varepsilon=\min\{1/2,a/(4C')\},\qquad
 k=\lfloor L\rfloor,\qquad L\geq2.\] These choices obey the claimed range \(k\leq L\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), but \[m=\frac{ak}{\varepsilon}
 \geq \frac{aL}{2\varepsilon}\geq2C'L>C'L.\] Thus the invocation fails for allowed endpoint moments. Increasing \(N\) cannot remove a fixed excessive multiplier of \(L\). The cited Smith theorem retains a fixed small multiplier of the triple logarithm [smith]; that passage does not supply an arbitrary multiplier.

This is a Major gap in the stated uniform moment conclusion, not a counterexample to its truth. The same calculation allows the argument on a smaller range such as \[k\leq \frac{C'\varepsilon}{2a}\log\log\log N,\] subject also to a valid normalization of the moment input. A stronger input or a separate argument is required to recover coefficient one for every prescribed loss. The density theorems do not depend on this later moment-upgrading argument.

Problem 6 (Minor: the asserted small-\(m\) normalization is impossible).  Page 4, Theorem 2.5 asserts the exponential-moment bound with right side \(\exp(Cm^2)\) for every \(m>0\) in its range. For a family with any positive-rank member, this fails as \(m\downarrow0\).

Take \(E:y^2=x^3-2\). Its point \((3,5)\) is not torsion: doubling gives \(x(2P)=129/100\), whereas rational torsion points on an integral short Weierstrass equation have integral coordinates by the Nagell–Lutz theorem. For fixed sufficiently large \(N\), let \(M=\#\mathcal D(N)\). Since \(D=1\) is present, the mean in Theorem 2.5 is at least \[1+\frac{e^m-1}{M}.\] The derivative of this expression at zero is \(1/M>0\); the derivative of \(e^{Cm^2}\) is zero. The inequality therefore fails for all sufficiently small positive \(m\), which are within the required upper range. The same small-\(m\) wording occurs in the consulted cited theorem; it is not used here to invalidate all large-\(m\) moment information.

An input with a fixed prefactor, an exponent \(C(m+m^2)\), or an explicit lower restriction on \(m\) avoids this particular obstruction. The application’s \(m\) is bounded away from zero, so this local normalization error is separate from the Major endpoint-range issue above.

# Two further local corrections

Problem 7 (Minor: the numerical choice of \(\varepsilon\) in Lemma 8.4).  Page 35 chooses \(\varepsilon=5/8\) to change an error exponent \(-1/2+\varepsilon\) to \(-1/8\). The actual value is \(+1/8\).

The exact correction \(\varepsilon=3/8\) gives \(-1/8\). Since the preceding estimate allows any positive \(\varepsilon\), this correction restores the intended numerical step, conditional on the corrected field setup. No separate deterioration of the intended final exponent is forced by this arithmetic error.

Problem 8 (Minor: the displayed short Weierstrass model in Section 8.2).  Page 37, the proof of Theorem 8.1, omits twist powers and uses the old variable in the equation following \((X,Y)=(9x+3AD,27y)\).

Direct expansion gives the correct identity \[Y^2=X^3+27(3B-A^2)D^2X+
             27A(2A^2-9B)D^3.\] For the allowed example \(A=-4\), \(B=2\), \(D=2\) and \((x,y)=(1,1)\), one has \(A^2-4B=8\) and \((X,Y)=(-15,27)\). The corrected right side is \(729=Y^2\). The printed right side is \(-5157\) if its \(x\) is taken literally, or \(-837\) if read as \(X\). This is an actual integral point satisfying the original hypotheses.

The corrected expression has fixed integral coefficients multiplying \(D^2\) and \(D^3\), and \(\gcd(X,D)\mid9\gcd(x,D)\) still holds. Thus the intended reduction to the same fixed twist-family setting is recovered by the explicit algebraic correction; this local display error is not an additional obstruction to that reduction.

# Other checks and limits

The independent script verifies the full quartic seminvariant identity \[4H^3-Ia^2H-Ja^3-R^2=0\] using the definitions on page 11. The Hensel argument in Lemma 5.1 correctly needs odd \(M\) and invertible \(x_0\); these follow from \(\gcd(M,2x_0)=1\). In the large-gcd irreducible case, square-freeness gives \(g^2\mid y\) from \(g^3\mid y^2\), and \(\gcd(g,\widetilde D)=1\) makes the root congruence valid. The fixed-point proportions \(2/3\) for \(S_3\) and \(1/3\) for \(C_3\) give the asserted prime-density upper bound.

The linked-index analysis distinguishes double-oscillation estimates from the small-conductor Hecke-character step. An independent exact graph enumeration verifies maximum unlinked size four for the twelve indices in Section 7.1, and size eight for the thirty-two indices in Section 7.3. All size-eight sets in the latter computation use at most two first-index rows, as required for containment in one of the specified excluded sets. This checks the combinatorics, not the full analytic estimates.

The Pell reductions keep the square-free product and positivity restrictions; \(cd>ab\) excludes the simultaneous square case in the cited step. In Section 8 the exceptional positive, nonsquare-\(B\) points are controlled only under the displayed height restriction. Applying a uniform weak Hall–Lang bound makes that restriction automatic eventually, since \(N^{1-\eta}=o(N/(\log N)^c)\) for fixed \(\eta,c>0\). No unproved Hall–Lang assertion is treated as an unconditional theorem.
