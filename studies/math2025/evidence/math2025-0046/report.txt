# M01: the proposed approximants have divergent mass

## Location and assertion

Equation reference: badapprox. The last displayed initial-data formula on printed p. 371 (PDF 57), with the stated cutoff directly below it, is \[
 f^\varepsilon(0,v)
 =(f_0(v)+\varepsilon)\eta(\varepsilon v)
 +(1-\eta(\varepsilon v))e^{-|v|^2},
 \qquad
 0\leq\eta\leq1,\quad
 \eta|_{B_1}=1,\quad
 \operatorname{supp}\eta\subset B_2.\] The first paragraph of p. 372 (PDF 58) then asserts convergence of the solutions, including weighted \(L^{\infty,k}\) convergence for \(f\), for any \(k\), and \(L^\infty([0,T],H^1(\mathbb R^3))\) convergence for their square roots. It uses this to transfer monotonicity of Fisher information to the original solution. The approximation is explicitly connected to the proof of Theorem 1.1 on p. 364 and to the justification of the integral manipulations on p. 368.

## Complete obstruction, including a unit-mass example

Choose the admissible, nonzero, smooth unit-mass Maxwellian \[f_0(v)=\pi^{-3/2}e^{-|v|^2}.\] It has every polynomial moment, finite Fisher information, and the upper bound required by Theorem 1.2. It is a stationary solution for every applicable power-law kernel: the difference of logarithmic gradients is \(-2(v-w)\), which is annihilated by \(a(v-w)\).

Equation reference: mass, energy. On \(B_{1/\varepsilon}\), equation [badapprox] (#badapprox) gives \(f^\varepsilon(0,v)=f_0(v)+\varepsilon\). Therefore \[\begin{aligned}
 m_\varepsilon:=\int_{\mathbb R^3}f^\varepsilon(0,v)\,\mathrm dv
 &\geq\varepsilon |B_{1/\varepsilon}|
   =\frac{4\pi}{3}\varepsilon^{-2}\longrightarrow\infty,\\
 \int_{\mathbb R^3}|v|^2f^\varepsilon(0,v)\,\mathrm dv
 &\geq\varepsilon\int_{B_{1/\varepsilon}}|v|^2\,\mathrm dv
   =\frac{4\pi}{5}\varepsilon^{-4}\longrightarrow\infty.\end{aligned}\] These bounds hold for every cutoff permitted in the paper, independently of its transition behavior.

For each \(\varepsilon>0\), the approximant is integrable and has a Gaussian tail, so the objection is not that an individual three-dimensional datum is inadmissible. It is the absence of convergence and of uniform hydrodynamic bounds. At time zero, \[\|\sqrt{f^\varepsilon(0)}-\sqrt{f_0}\|_{L^2}
 \geq \sqrt{m_\varepsilon}-1\longrightarrow\infty.\] Mass conservation, recalled on p. 324, gives the same lower bound at every common time of existence of \(f^\varepsilon\) and the stationary \(f_0\). Thus this also rules out the asserted essential-supremum-in-time \(H^1\) convergence; the problem cannot be avoided by discarding the time-zero endpoint.

There is a second elementary obstruction in the exact norm used for the initial data. At \(v_\varepsilon=(1/(2\varepsilon),0,0)\), the cutoff is one and the difference is exactly \(\varepsilon\), whence \[\|f^\varepsilon(0)-f_0\|_{L^\infty_k}
 \geq \varepsilon\langle v_\varepsilon\rangle^k
 \geq 2^{-k}\varepsilon^{1-k}.\] It diverges for every \(k>1\), in particular for the weights used by Theorem 2.1. The energy divergence in [energy] (#energy) also prevents using the cited mass/energy-dependent estimates uniformly by simply invoking that theorem.

## The related six-dimensional formula

Printed p. 364 (PDF 50) proposes \[F_\varepsilon(v,w)=(F(v,w)+\varepsilon)\eta(\varepsilon v,\varepsilon w)
 +(1-\eta(\varepsilon v,\varepsilon w))e^{-|v|^2}.\] As printed, the tail has no \(w\)-decay. For fixed \(v\) and all sufficiently large \(w\), it equals \(e^{-|v|^2}\), and hence is not integrable on \(\mathbb R^6\); it need not be symmetric under \(v\leftrightarrow w\), either. These facts prevent its direct use in Proposition 10.2. Inserting the missing Gaussian factor in \(w\) alone still leaves the constant floor on an expanding six-dimensional ball, of mass at least \(\omega_6\varepsilon^{-5}\). The substantive issue is the unproved passage to a limit with these actual approximants, not the isolated missing factor. Both formulas belong to the same issue M01.

## Counterevidence and precise impact

The argument through Lemma 10.1 and Proposition 10.2 is a valid formal dissipation argument for sufficiently controlled, positive, symmetric inputs, subject to the analytic integrations being justified. Sections 3–10 do not require the divergent plateau to produce the constants. The later continuation argument in Section 11 is coherent once the monotonicity estimate is available for its solution class. A Gaussian stationary solution itself is not a counterexample to either global existence or Fisher monotonicity.

Moreover, Lemma 3.3 on p. 333 already uses an integrable symmetric Gaussian perturbation for a different limiting argument. That is useful evidence that an appropriate repair is plausible. However, it does not prove the solution convergence asserted on p. 372 for [badapprox] (#badapprox). Renormalizing [badapprox] (#badapprox) alone is not a remedy: its mass tends to infinity while it tends pointwise to \(f_0\), so division by its mass sends the initial data locally to zero.

The issue is rated Major because the defective passage is presented as the justification for extending the main Fisher-information theorem beyond the specially controlled class and for applying that estimate in the global theorem. The demonstrated result is a failure of the supplied proof step. It is not a claim that Theorems 1.1 or 1.2 are false, nor that no alternative proof exists. Its repair must supply convergence of the relevant solutions and Fisher information, rather than merely alter a displayed symbol.

## A checked initial-data repair and its remaining obligation

For the Gaussian-upper-bound class of Theorem 1.2, choose a positive Gaussian \(M\) with \(f_0\leq C M\), and set \[g^\varepsilon_0=f_0+\varepsilon M.\] Then \(\varepsilon M\leq g^\varepsilon_0\leq(C+\varepsilon)M\), and every finite polynomial moment of \(g^\varepsilon_0-f_0\) tends to zero. When \(\sqrt{f_0}\in H^1\), this also repairs initial square-root \(H^1\) convergence. Indeed, \[\|\sqrt{g^\varepsilon_0}-\sqrt{f_0}\|_2^2
 \leq\varepsilon\|M\|_1,\qquad
 i(f_0+\varepsilon M)\leq i(f_0)+\varepsilon i(M).\] The latter inequality is pointwise Cauchy–Schwarz applied to \(|\nabla(f_0+\varepsilon M)|^2/(f_0+\varepsilon M)\). Weak compactness and lower semicontinuity, combined with the upper bound, give convergence of the gradient norms and then strong \(H^1\) convergence.

This proves an initial-data replacement in the stated finite-Fisher class. It does not by itself prove convergence of the corresponding Landau solutions in \(L^\infty_tH^1\), or justify passage of the dissipation inequality at arbitrary positive times. Those stability and time-shift arguments must be supplied with uniform hypotheses. The more general polynomial-tail statement at the end of Appendix B requires further construction and appropriately restricted weights; it is not repaired here. No claim of a fully repaired main theorem is made.

# Independent checks supporting the unaffected calculations

## Equation scope and the lifting argument

Theorems 1.1–1.2 concern the three-dimensional space-homogeneous equation. Theorem 1.1 is an a priori estimate for classical solutions with adequate decay, under \(\sup_{r>0}r|\alpha'(r)|/\alpha(r)\leq\sqrt{19}\). For \(\alpha(r)=r^\gamma\), this ratio is \(|\gamma|\); the physical interval \([-3,1]\) is included. Theorem 1.2 assumes a Gaussian upper bound on the initial datum. Rough data are interpreted through positive-time smoothness and weak attainment at zero, as explained in Theorem 2.1. The unit-mass convention on p. 324 is respected by the counterexample above; zero initial mass is not used to manufacture a separate issue.

For unit-mass \(f\), direct differentiation of the tensor product gives \(I(f\otimes f)=2i(f)\). For symmetric \(F(v,w)\), Cauchy–Schwarz in \(w\) yields \[\frac{|\nabla\pi F(v)|^2}{\pi F(v)}
 \leq \int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{|\nabla_vF(v,w)|^2}{F(v,w)}\,\mathrm dw.\] Integration in \(v\) and symmetry give \(i(\pi F)\leq I(F)/2\). At \(F(0)=f\otimes f\), equality holds. The right derivative at zero of the nonpositive difference \(i(\pi F)-I(F)/2\) is nonpositive, proving the direction of Lemma 3.4. This suffices; the optional two-sided-curve discussion in Remark 3.5 is not needed.

## Rotations, radial error and signs

Writing \(B_k=\widetilde b_k\cdot\nabla\), the identities \(\sum_k b_k\otimes b_k=|v-w|^2 I-(v-w)\otimes(v-w)\), \(B_k\alpha=0\), and \(\operatorname{div}\widetilde b_k=0\) give \(Q=\sum_k\alpha B_k^2\). The fields are rotations on the fixed center-of-mass and relative-radius layers. The parallel Fisher information and the spherical Fisher information are separately invariant under the weighted rotations; the two nonzero commutator contributions cancel in the sum.

For \(n\) as in (4.7), \(n\cdot\nabla\alpha=\sqrt2\,\alpha'\) and \([n,\widetilde b_k]=0\). Setting \(u=\log F\), \(h=B_k u\), the integrand before the final square completion in Lemma 7.1 is \[-2\alpha F(nh)^2-2(n\alpha)Fh(nh).\] It equals \[-2F\bigl(n(\sqrt\alpha\,h)\bigr)^2+
 \frac{(n\alpha)^2}{2\alpha}Fh^2
 =
 -2F\bigl(n(\sqrt\alpha\,h)\bigr)^2+
 \frac{(\alpha')^2}{\alpha}Fh^2.\] Thus the sign and coefficient of the radial error are correct.

The intermediate displays (8.2) on p. 348 and the first display on p. 349 omit a minus sign before the respective Fisher Hessian term. The negative signs follow directly from Lemma 7.1 and (8.1), and the lemma statement and final expanded expression have the correct sign. These are recorded as excluded local transcription errors, not additional substantive findings: the required identity has been reconstructed without changing the argument.

## The \(19/4\) sphere constant

Put \(B_i=b_i\cdot\nabla_\sigma\), \(u=\sqrt f\), \(A_g=(B_iB_jg)_{ij}\), and let \(M_g,N_g\) be its symmetric and antisymmetric parts. The commutators give \[\|N_g\|^2=\frac12\sum_i(B_i g)^2.\] For a divergence-free rotational field \(B\), integration by parts gives the one-direction identity \[\int f(B^2\log f)^2
 =4\int\left((B^2u)^2+\frac{(Bu)^4}{3u^2}\right)
 \geq4\int(B^2u)^2.\] Averaging over \(B=b_e\cdot\nabla\), with fourth spherical moments \(\int e_1^2e_2^2=4\pi/15\) and \(\int e_1^4=4\pi/5\), gives Lemma 9.8.

The pointwise relation \(\sum_i\sigma_i B_i=0\) implies \(\sigma^T A_g=0\), so \(A_g\) has rank at most two. In a basis with a unit kernel vector first, its first diagonal entry is zero. The squared Frobenius norm of its symmetric part dominates the sum of squares of the other two diagonal entries, giving \(2\|M_g\|^2\geq|\operatorname{tr}M_g|^2\). This verifies the rank step of Lemmas 9.9–9.10.

The Casimir \(\Delta_\sigma=\sum_i B_i^2\) commutes with each rotation, and integration by parts yields \[\sum_{i,j}\int(B_iB_j u)^2=\int(\Delta_\sigma u)^2,\qquad
 \int\|M_u\|^2=\int(\Delta_\sigma u)^2-\frac12\int|\nabla_\sigma u|^2.\] Consequently Lemma 9.10 implies \[\int f\|M_{\log f}\|^2
 \geq 3\int(\Delta_\sigma u)^2-\int|\nabla_\sigma u|^2.\] If \(f\) is even, then \(u\) and each \(B_j u\) are even. Each \(B_j u\) has integral zero because \(B_j\) is divergence free. The first allowed nonconstant spherical harmonic degree is two, of eigenvalue six. Hence \(\sum_{i,j}\int(B_iB_j u)^2\geq6\sum_j\int(B_j u)^2\). The last lower bound is therefore \(17\int|\nabla_\sigma u|^2
=(17/4)\int f|\nabla_\sigma\log f|^2\). Adding the antisymmetric contribution \(1/2\) gives \(19/4\). Dropping evenness would invalidate this improved spectral-gap step.

## Transfer and continuation

With \(v=z+r\sigma,\ w=z-r\sigma\), the six-dimensional Jacobian is \(8r^2\), and \(\widetilde b_k\cdot\nabla=2B_k\). The factors in (8.3)–(8.4) give \(16\) and \(8\) times the corresponding sphere integrals. Thus \(D_{\rm spherical}\geq(19/2)R_{\rm spherical}\), exactly compensating the error coefficient \(\sup r^2(\alpha')^2/(2\alpha^2)\). Remark 10.3’s \(Q=4\alpha(2r)\Delta_\sigma\) also follows. The earlier informal geometric normalization is not needed for these explicit computations.

For Section 11, finite Fisher information at \(t_0>0\) controls \(L^3\) by the Sobolev estimate for \(\sqrt f\). The weighted interpolation can be made explicit: \[\|f\|_{L^2_k}
 \leq\|f\|_{L^1_{4k}}^{1/4}\|f\|_{L^3}^{3/4}.\] For \(\gamma\in[-3,-2)\), \(2>3/(5+\gamma)\), so this meets the displayed conditional criterion of Theorem 2.2. Moment bounds may depend on the fixed finite proposed blow-up time; they need not be uniform as time tends to infinity. The Gaussian upper estimate then controls every finite \(L^\infty_k\) weight and closes the stated continuation criterion, conditional on the Fisher estimate and the cited analytic inputs. Constants must depend on the actual positive-time Fisher bound and the moments used; finiteness of \(i(f_0)\) is not silently assumed for every rough datum in Theorem 1.2.

For Appendix A, even functions on \(S^1\) exclude odd Fourier modes, giving the first positive allowed eigenvalue four. The same one-direction identity gives the displayed constant four and hence the lifted factor eight. This does not independently establish every higher-dimensional suggestion in that appendix.
