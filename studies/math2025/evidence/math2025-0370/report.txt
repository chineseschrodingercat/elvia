Abstract: This review concerns the exact 33-page preprint arXiv:2302.09058v2. Its combinatorial extraction, Baire category exclusion, matroid coloring, and geometric lower-bound constructions were examined with their different quantifiers retained. Three local errors are confirmed: the concluding planar chromatic minimum is misstated, a preliminary collinear dichotomy is false, and an entropy display omits a factor. Explicit arguments repair these points without an identified loss of the main results. The supported assessment is Minor; no Major issue is established. Reviewer-authored exact finite calculations corroborate several counting steps, with their finite scope stated below. All source pages were read as text and 24 source pages were actually inspected as images. Independent scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Identity, scope, and principal claims

The reviewed work is by Noga Alon, Matija Bucić, and Lisa Sauermann [ABS], with frozen annual identity M25-0370. The exact source is arXiv:2302.09058v2, submitted on 7 November 2024; its actual PDF has 33 pages. The associated journal identifier is DOI https://doi.org/10.1007/s00039-025-00698-x. The publisher records online publication on 24 January 2025 and pagination 1–42 in volume 35. The journal record and the acquired preprint are distinct bindings. No equality of their texts is assumed, and the frozen annual identity is not changed by the preprint date. Locations below are one-based PDF pages, agreeing with the printed numbers.

For a norm on \(\mathbb R^d\), \(U(n)\) maximizes the number of unordered unit-distance pairs among \(n\) distinct points, whereas \(D(n)\) minimizes the number of distinct distances between different points. The space \(\mathcal B_d\) consists of closed, bounded, centrally symmetric convex bodies with the origin in their interior, with the Hausdorff metric. A typical norm means a norm outside a meagre subset of this space. It is not a probabilistic assertion about a randomly chosen norm.

Theorem 1.1 states, for fixed \(d\ge2\), that a residual collection of norms satisfies \[U(n)\le \frac d2 n\log_2 n\] for every \(n\) and every corresponding configuration. Theorem 1.2 instead applies to every norm and gives, as \(n\to\infty\) with \(d\) fixed, \[U(n)\ge \frac{d-1-o(1)}2 n\log_2 n.\] Theorem 1.3 gives \(D(n)=(1-o(1))n\) for typical norms. Theorem 1.4 gives planar unit-distance chromatic number four for typical norms; Theorem 6.1 proves the stronger upper bound \(2^d\) for the odd-integer-distance graph. These quantifiers are essential: the lower-bound construction does not require a typical norm, while the generic exclusion must hold simultaneously for all finite configurations.

# Examination of the main proof mechanisms

## Independent directions, forests, and weighted extraction

Lemma 4.3, pages 10–12, assumes linearly independent directions over an arbitrary field and separately requires the edges in each direction to form a forest. The latter hypothesis cannot be dropped: even collinear point sets can otherwise give dense graphs. In the connected case, the proof fixes one direction and partitions the vertices by its coefficient in the independent-coordinate representation. Non-red edges stay within coefficient fibers. Distinct vertices in one fiber cannot belong to the same red-tree component, since that would place their nonzero difference in two disjoint linear spans. Consequently, the red forest has at least as many components as the largest fiber.

Writing the fiber sizes as \(n_1\ge\cdots\ge n_\ell\), this yields \[e(G)\le n-n_1+\frac12\sum_i n_i\log_2 n_i
       \le \frac12 n\log_2 n.\] The last inequality is the correctly stated Lemma 4.4. Problem 3 below concerns a defective intermediate display in its proof; the preceding binary-entropy computation already supplies the missing factor. The forest argument itself retains the correct expression.

Lemma 4.5, page 13, uses the closure bound \[|\{j:u_j\in\operatorname{span}_F(u_i:i\in I)\}|\le d|I|+m\] for every index set \(I\), and weights \(0\le\lambda_j\le M\). Sorting weights and greedily selecting independent vectors gives indices \[j_s\le (s-1)d+m+1,\qquad k\le dr+m.\] Grouping the sorted weights into blocks of size \(d\), after the first \(m\) weights, then gives \[d\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_j\ge\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_j-mM.\] The proof has two local \(m/r\) index slips noted below, but the greedy definition establishes independence for all \(r\) selected vectors. No new structural hypothesis is needed.

For Lemma 4.1, the field is \(\mathbb Q\), and a fixed unit direction generates disjoint paths in a finite point configuration. Combining the weighted extraction with the forest bound therefore gives the claimed excess-closure alternative. Lemma 4.2 uses a subfield \(F\subset\mathbb R\), non-collinearity over \(\mathbb R\), and sufficiently large \(n\). Its use of the direction theorem is confined to the finite-dimensional real span of the finite configuration; the ambient vector space need not be finite-dimensional.

The collapse procedure in Lemma 4.2 leaves a set of size \(h>\mu n/(3d)\), unless the alternative already contradicts the assumed closure bound. In the remaining case the direction forests have weights \(\lambda_i\le3d/\mu\), and pair coverage gives \[\sum_i\lambda_i\ge \frac{\mu}{24d}h^2.\] For sufficiently large \(n\), weighted extraction produces an independent-direction graph with at least \(\mu h^2/(48d^2)\) edges. This contradicts \(e(G)\le h\log_2 h/2\). The dependence on \(d,\mu\), the exclusion of the collinear case, and the rounding of \((1-\mu)n\) were retained in checking this argument.

## Nowhere density and the universal configuration quantifier

Section 5, pages 17–23, encodes a bad finite configuration by a rational matrix in the unit-distance case, or by rational functions over \(\mathbb Q\) in the distinct-distance case. The latter functions are evaluated only where their denominators are nonzero. The distance field need not be purely transcendental: every element of the field generated by the actual distances has a rational-function representative with a denominator nonzero at those distances.

For fixed matrix and a positive rational angular separation, approximate a unit ball by a centrally symmetric polytope with small facets. A hypothetical separated family of unit directions must use different pairs of opposite facets. For a fixed assignment and signs, the \(d\ell+m+1\) selected support parameters are rational functions of only \(d\ell+m\) variables. Their image therefore satisfies a nonzero polynomial equation. There are only finitely many facet assignments and sign choices. Their union lies in a proper closed algebraic subset of the support-parameter space.

This closed algebraic enclosure is important. It permits choosing a small box of support parameters disjoint from all achievable tuples, even when a possible bad configuration could have unbounded auxiliary coordinates or nearly vanishing denominators. Small Hausdorff changes of the resulting polytope preserve the facet-pair separation, and the nearby unit vectors would create another achievable tuple in the forbidden box. The proof thus excludes a neighborhood, which is the needed nowhere-density conclusion.

The countability step involves integer configuration sizes, matrix sizes, rational angular thresholds, and coefficients in a countable rational-function field. For the asymptotic distinct-distance statement, one also takes a countable sequence \(\mu\downarrow0\). These countable operations preserve the common residual collection needed for all configurations. The two preliminary approximation lemmas and the boundary Hausdorff argument were followed in this use. Local stability of the small-facet bound is sufficient; the proof does not require a global assertion about continuity of every exposed face across all polytope degenerations.

## The coloring argument and its external input

For the finite set of unit directions arising from a finite odd-distance graph, the absence of an excess closure implies the matroid rank criterion. Indeed, for any subset \(A\) of the direction indices, choose a basis \(I\subset A\). Then \[|A|\le|\operatorname{cl}(I)|\le d|I|=d\,\operatorname{rk}(A).\] The finite vector matroid can therefore be partitioned into \(d\) independent sets by Edmonds’ theorem. Its original statement and vector-matroid setting were checked in [Edmonds], PDF pages 1 and 3, printed pages 67 and 69.

Each resulting graph is bipartite: an odd cycle would give a relation among independent directions, and at least one direction would have an odd integer coefficient, hence a nonzero coefficient. Combining the \(d\) two-colorings gives \(2^d\) colors. The finite-subgraph reduction uses the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem. No claim of an explicit or measurable coloring follows from this argument.

Although Section 5 begins with \(d\ge2\), the \(d=1\) endpoint of Theorem 6.1 can also be checked directly. Write the norm as \(c|x|\). Within each coset of \(c^{-1}\mathbb Z\), parity of the integer coordinate gives a two-coloring of odd-distance edges; different cosets have no such edges. The planar equality four additionally uses the cited universal lower bound of Chilakamarri. That external lower-bound proof was not independently reconstructed in this review.

## Lower bounds for every norm

For a strictly convex planar norm, Lemma 7.2 constructs unit triangles successively while avoiding collisions between all their sums. The finite forbidden-choice argument uses that two distinct translates of the strictly convex unit circle have at most two common points. This can also be understood by taking the translation horizontal: intersection endpoints correspond to a fixed horizontal chord length, and the strictly concave chord-width function attains that positive length at at most two heights. The construction only needs the Cartesian power of \(K_3\) as a subgraph; extra unit edges would improve the lower bound.

The resulting \(3^k\) points have at least \(k3^k\) unit edges. The ternary expansion of an arbitrary \(n\), with separately translated blocks, gives at least \[\sum_i a_i i3^i\ge \lfloor\log_3 n\rfloor n-\frac32 n.\] For a norm that is not strictly convex, the existing boundary segment supplies the stronger quadratic construction. Thus this planar lower bound does not impose strict convexity on the final proposition.

For the general-dimensional construction, the compact set \(U'\) of Lemma 7.4 contains, through its product parametrization, a closed set of dimension at least \[d+(d-1)^2=d(d-1)+1.\] The projection target has dimension \(d(d-1)\). Compactness follows from boundedness of the \(x_i\) and \(\|x\|\le\|x_1\|+1\); closedness follows from continuity. The Hurewicz dimension-lowering theorem therefore yields a fiber of positive dimension and hence an infinite set of common unit neighbors. The unit boundary has the needed local dimension because radial projection identifies it with a sphere.

The grid in Lemma 7.5 remains injective after a sufficiently small perturbation of its finitely many generators. The infinite common-neighbor set then allows each further binary generator to avoid the finitely many collisions with earlier sums. This establishes distinctness of all \[n'=k^{d-1}2^m\] points before their edges are counted.

For fixed \(i,j\), the counted edge decreases grid coordinate \(i\) by one and increases binary coordinate \(j\) from zero to one. Its difference is \(z_j-x_i\), a unit vector. The two endpoint labels uniquely identify these changed coordinates, so the counted unordered pairs for different \((i,j)\) are disjoint. The count is therefore \[(d-1)m(k-1)k^{d-2}2^{m-1}.\] This justification uses injectivity of the labels, not an unstated assumption that all geometric difference vectors are distinct. With the manuscript’s choices of \(m\) and \(k\), one has \(m=\log_2 n-O(\log\log n)\), \(k\to\infty\), and \(n'=(1-o(1))n\). Padding by extra points completes the all-\(n\) conclusion without losing edges.

# Confirmed local findings

Problem 1 (The planar chromatic minimum is misstated).  In Section 8, page 29, the paragraph beginning A related problem says that the minimum possible chromatic number of a planar norm’s unit-distance graph is two. This is false for every planar norm.

Fix any planar norm and a point \(x\) on its unit circle. The unit circle is a continuous radial image of the Euclidean circle, so there is a continuous arc from \(x\) to \(-x\). The continuous function \(y\mapsto\|y-x\|\) has endpoint values zero and two on that arc. It therefore takes the value one at some \(y\). The three distinct points \(0,x,y\) have all three mutual distances equal to one. Hence every planar norm’s unit-distance graph contains \(K_3\) and cannot be two-colored.

This universal argument is needed: merely exhibiting a Euclidean unit triangle would not by itself refute a minimum taken over all planar norms. It applies without strict convexity or genericity. The intended value is four, consistently with the universal lower bound cited on pages 3 and 23 and the paper’s typical-norm upper bound. The proof of Theorem 1.4 already uses four correctly. The error is a local concluding misstatement, assessed as Minor; it does not invalidate that theorem.

Problem 2 (The collinear dichotomy omits a boundary/interior pair).  The preliminary strict-convexity discussion on page 7 asserts that distinct collinear points \(a,b\) of the unit ball either have \(\max\{\|a\|,\|b\|\}<1\) or satisfy \(a=-b\).

In the Euclidean unit disk, take \[a=(1,0),\qquad b=(1/2,0).\] They are nonzero, distinct, collinear, and belong to the ball. Their maximum norm is one, and they are not antipodal. Thus all relevant hypotheses hold and the stated alternative fails. At \(\alpha=1/2\), however, the midpoint has norm \(3/4\), consistent with the desired strict-convexity conclusion.

Replacing \(\max\) by \(\min\) repairs the alternative. If one norm is strictly below one, the convex-combination norm is strictly below one. If both norms equal one and the points are distinct and collinear, they are antipodal, giving norm \(|2\alpha-1|<1\) for \(0<\alpha<1\). Equivalently, write \(a=tu,b=su\) with \(\|u\|=1\), \(s,t\in[-1,1]\), and \(s\ne t\). A strict convex combination cannot be an endpoint of that interval. The stated criterion for strict convexity survives. This is a Minor error in its local explanation.

Problem 3 (A missing factor in the entropy induction display).  In the final display of the proof of Lemma 4.4, page 11, the middle expression subtracts \(\sum_{i=2}^{\ell-1}n_i\log_2 n_i\), where a factor \(1/2\) is required.

Take \(\ell=3\) and \(n_1=n_2=n_3=2\), so \(n=6\) and \(n'_1=4\). These satisfy every hypothesis of the lemma. The three displayed quantities, as printed, become \[-1,\qquad -2,\qquad 6-3\log_2 6.\] The asserted second inequality is false, since \[-2<6-3\log_2 6
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 6^3<2^8,\] and \(216<256\). Thus this is not merely an uncertainty about numerical rounding.

The corrected induction chain is \[\begin{split}
 n_1-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}n_i\log_2 n_i
 &\ge n'_1-\frac12n'_1\log_2 n'_1
       -\frac12\sum_{i=2}^{\ell-1}n_i\log_2 n_i\\
 &\ge n-\frac12n\log_2 n.
\end{split}\] The preceding two-variable computation on the same page proves exactly the first corrected inequality using \(H(t)\ge2-2t\) for \(1/2\le t\le1\). The induction hypothesis gives the second. For the example, the corrected middle value is \(-1\).

The conclusion of Lemma 4.4 is unchanged, and its use in the forest proof already has the correct factor. The existing preceding calculation establishes the repaired general step, with no missing case or additional theorem required. Accordingly, no substantive loss of the core combinatorial argument is identified from this transcription error; its assessment is Minor.

## Additional editorial corrections

There are several further precise local slips. On page 9, the point \(s_1\) chosen within \(\eta/2\) of \(b_1\) is on the same side of the hyperplane as \(b_1\), not \(b_2\): the distance of \(b_1\) from that hyperplane is at least \(\eta\). In Lemma 4.5, page 13, the independence range and the contradiction range should use \(r\), the number of greedy choices, instead of \(m\), the closure allowance. The greedy definition and the final choice \(J=\{j_1,\ldots,j_r\}\) make the correction unambiguous. On page 21, the algebraic exceptional set \(H\) lies in \(\mathbb R^h\), the support-parameter space, rather than the printed \(\mathbb R^d\).

The Petty-conjecture reformulation on page 29 should say \(2\le n\le d+1\) when asserting \(D(n)=1\): a singleton determines no distance between different points, so \(D(1)=0\). The equivalent formulation using only \(n=d+1\) is unaffected. Finally, the description on page 25 should require that the unit-distance graph contains the Cartesian triangle power, rather than suggesting that all its edges have been characterized. The proof and lower bound use only containment. These observations have separate retained location and repair records; none is evidence of a false principal conclusion.
