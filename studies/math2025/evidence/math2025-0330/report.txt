# The higher-differentiability parameter obstruction

Equation reference: impossible. Problem 1 (Major: Lemma 4.6, page 42, equation (4.21)).  For every \(\sigma\in(0,2s_0-1)\), the proof asks for \(\beta\in(0,1)\) such that \[
 2s_0\beta-1>\frac{\sigma+1}{2}>\sigma.\] This selection is not possible throughout the stated range.

Take \[s=s_0=\frac35,\qquad \sigma=\frac1{10}.\] Then \(0<\sigma<2s_0-1=1/5\), exactly as required. Nevertheless, every \(\beta<1\) satisfies \[2s_0\beta-1<\frac15<\frac{11}{20}
       =\frac{\sigma+1}{2}.\] Indeed, [impossible] (#impossible) would require \(\beta>31/24\). These are realizable PDE parameters: \(n=2\), \(\Phi(t)=t\), \(\Lambda=1\), \(u=0\), \(\mu=0\), and \(R=1\) satisfy all assumptions of Lemma 4.6. The contradiction concerns the claimed parameter existence, not a failure of that particular solution to be regular.

In general, the first strict inequality in [impossible] (#impossible) can hold with \(\beta<1\) only if \[\sigma<4s_0-3.\] For \(s_0\leq3/4\) it cannot hold for any positive \(\sigma\). For every \(3/4<s_0<1\), it still excludes a nonempty part of the advertised interval \((0,2s_0-1)\).

The condition is used again on pages 44–45 to select the \(\sigma_j\) and remove the minimum in the recurrence. Lemma 4.6 is then used in the proof of Theorem 1.9 on page 47 and in the gradient comparison of Corollary 5.2 on page 48. It is therefore a core proof obstruction for the claimed optimal differentiability and subsequent potential-estimate chain. The homogeneous Theorem 1.5 does not depend on Lemma 4.6. No counterexample to the main regularity conclusions is claimed.

## A concrete repair of the parameter arithmetic

The obstruction can be removed at the level of exponent selection. For fixed \(1/2<s_0<1\) and \(0<\sigma<2s_0-1\), set \[a=\frac{\sigma+2s_0-1}{2},\qquad
 \beta=\frac12\left(1+\frac{a+1}{2s_0}\right),\qquad
 K=2s_0\beta-1,\qquad T=\frac{s_0(1+\sigma)}2.\] Then \(0<\beta<1\), \(\sigma<a<K\), and \[T-\sigma=(1-s_0)^2+
       \frac{(2-s_0)(2s_0-1-\sigma)}2>0.\] Starting from \(b_0=0\), the capped recurrence \[b_{j+1}=\min\{a,\ \beta b_j+T(1-\beta)\}\] increases to \(\min\{a,T\}>\sigma\) and crosses \(\sigma\) after finitely many steps. It retains strict margins below the data exponent \(K\) and below the available gain \(\beta b_j+s(1-\beta)\), since \(T<s_0\leq s\). For the explicit example above, \(a=3/20\), \(\beta=47/48\), \(K=7/40\), and \(T=33/100\); exact rational iteration first crosses \(\sigma=1/10\) at step 18.

This gives a viable replacement for the impossible arithmetic, rather than an unproved conjectural repair. It does not constitute a complete revised proof of all the covering, tail, localization, and constant estimates. Those estimates must be written with the new exponents and strict margins. Also, the standing lower bound \(s_0\) in Sections 4–5 must be taken above \(1/2\) whenever \(2s_0\beta-1>0\) is required. Merely correcting that standing interval does not fix [impossible] (#impossible), since the counterexample already has \(s_0>1/2\).

# Normalization and scale corrections

The following three changes of variables use smooth admissible functions and exact powers. The counterexamples to scale-dependent displays are families, so an unspecified constant depending only on the listed fixed parameters cannot absorb the discrepancy.

Problem 2 (Minor: formal operator versus weak form, pages 1, 2, and 5).  The formal operator (1.2) has prefactor \(1-s\), whereas the double integral in (1.15) also has prefactor \(1-s\). With the stated oddness, the latter represents twice the formal operator.

Let \(\mathcal L_{\rm pv}\) denote exactly (1.2). For smooth compactly supported functions, symmetrization gives \[(1-s)\iint
 \Phi\left(\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^s}\right)
 \frac{\psi(x)-\psi(y)}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\,dx\,dy
 =2\int(\mathcal L_{\rm pv}u)\psi\,dx.\] The identity follows first with a symmetric exclusion \(|x-y|>\varepsilon\) and then by passage to the limit. For a decisive nonzero example take \(\Phi(t)=t\) and \(\psi=u\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^n)\) nonconstant. The Dirichlet integral is positive, so the factor cannot disappear. The measure \((\mathcal L_{\rm pv}u)\,dx\) has finite total variation, since the density is bounded locally and decays as \(|x|^{-n-2s}\) outside a compact set.

The variation of energy (1.4) agrees with the printed weak form, hence with \(2\mathcal L_{\rm pv}u=\mu\). One may change the formal prefactor to \(2(1-s)\) while preserving the weak form and energy, or halve both their integral prefactors while preserving (1.2). The estimates have unspecified constants and the later localization calculation consistently uses the weak form, so this is a normalization correction rather than a refutation of those qualitative results.

Problem 3 (Minor: Lemma 2.3, page 14).  The left side is an averaged Sobolev norm, but the right side is the unnormalized seminorm without its required scale factor.

Take \(n=2\), \(q=2\), \(\gamma=1/2\), and \(g_R(x)=x_1/R\) on \(B_R\). Then \((g_R)_{B_R}=0\) and \[\left(\frac{1}{|B_R|}\int_{B_R}|g_R|^4\,dx\right)^{1/4}=8^{-1/4},\qquad
 [g_R]_{W^{1/2,2}(B_R)}
   =R^{1/2}[x_1]_{W^{1/2,2}(B_1)}.\] The seminorm on the unit ball is finite and positive. Letting \(R\downarrow0\) contradicts the printed constant \(c(n,\gamma,q)\). The required factor is \(R^{\gamma-n/q}\) on the right. The invocation on page 40 occurs after \(R=1\) normalization, on radii between \(1/4\) and \(1/2\), where this factor is uniformly bounded. That application does not inherit the false unrestricted radius dependence.

Problem 4 (Minor: equation (4.5), page 35).  With the chosen differentiability index \(h=2s-1\), the power \(R^{-s/2+2s-n}\) is inconsistent with scaling. The corresponding power before the measure mass is \(R^{1-n}\).

Take \(n=2\), \(s=3/4\), \(\Phi(t)=t\), and a nonzero smooth bump \(\phi\) supported in \(B_{1/2}\). Put \(u_R(x)=R^{2s}\phi(x/R)\). Its homogeneous replacement on \(B_R\) with the same exterior data is \(v_R=0\). Define \(\mu_R\) by the printed weak formulation, so that its density is a fixed smooth bounded function evaluated at \(x/R\) and \(|\mu_R|(B_R)=R^nM\) with a fixed \(M>0\). All assumptions of the comparison step hold.

The normalized double integral on the left of (4.5) is \(R A\) for a fixed \(A>0\). The printed right side is \(cR^{3s/2}M=cR^{9/8}M\). Their ratio diverges as \(R\downarrow0\). In the cited comparison lemma [kms], the generic factor is \(r^{-h}r^{2s-n}\), which indeed becomes \(r^{1-n}\) here. The displayed stability prefactor itself is consistent: \[\frac{(1-h)^2}{(1-s)(s-h)}=4,\qquad h=2s-1.\] The normalized \(R=1\) estimate used later is not refuted by this scale test. Correcting the power and restoring the corresponding Poincare factor yields the stated \(R^{2s-n}\) degree in the zero-order comparison. Uniform constants still require the normalization and dependency estimates to be retained.

Problem 5 (Minor: Lemma 3.6, page 31).  The nonhomogeneous term lacks the factor \(R^{2s}\) present in Lemma 2.13. Its printed radius-uniform estimate is false.

For an explicit admissible family choose \(\Phi(t)=t\), \(n=2\), \(s=3/4\), and \(\phi\in C_c^\infty(B_1)\) with \(\phi(x)=x_1\) on \(B_{1/2}\). Such a function is obtained by multiplying \(x_1\) by a smooth cutoff equal to one on \(B_{1/2}\). For \(L>3\), set \[w_L(x)=L\phi(x/L),\qquad R=L^2,\qquad h=e_1,\qquad\beta=1.\] Use the weak-form forcing \(f_L\) for \(w_L\). If \(F\) is that forcing for \(\phi\), homogeneity in this linear test case gives \(f_L(x)=L^{1-2s}F(x/L)\). Smoothness of \(\phi\) implies \(\|\nabla F\|_\infty<\infty\).

At the origin \(\delta_hw_L(0)=1\), so the left side of the claimed estimate is at least one. Yet \[\frac{1}{|B_R|}\int_{B_R}|\delta_hw_L|\,dx\leq CL^{-n},\qquad
 \operatorname{Tail}(\delta_hw_L;B_R)=0,\qquad
 \|\delta_hf_L\|_\infty\leq CL^{-2s}.\] The tail vanishes because the difference is supported in \(B_{L+1}\subset B_R\). Thus the entire printed right side tends to zero. The functions are smooth energy functions with finite tails, \(f_L\) is bounded on \(B_{2R}\), and \(|h|<R/8\). Restoring \(R^{2s}\|\delta_hf/|h|^\beta\|_\infty\) gives the scaling supplied by Lemma 2.13. The later nonhomogeneous bootstrap uses Lemma 3.6 after unit-ball rescaling; Lemma 3.10 uses \(f=0\). These actual uses do not depend on the missing factor.

# Two further local corrections

Problem 6 (Minor: equation (1.19), page 8).  For arbitrary \(\beta\in(0,n)\), the comparison of truncated and classical Riesz potentials cannot have a constant depending only on \(n\).

Take \(n=2\), \(\mu=\delta_{e_1}\), \(x_0=0\), \(\beta=2-\varepsilon\), and \(R=\exp(1/\varepsilon)\). The classical potential equals one, whereas \[I_\beta^{|\mu|}(0,R)=
 \int_1^{\exp(1/\varepsilon)}t^{-1-\varepsilon}\,dt
 =\frac{1-e^{-1}}{\varepsilon}.\] This is unbounded as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). Tonelli’s theorem gives the correct factor \(1/(n-\beta)\). In the actual gradient applications \(\beta=2s-1<1\) and \(n\geq2\), so \(n-\beta>1\) and a dimension-only upper constant is available. The correction concerns the overly general uniformity assertion, not those applications.

Problem 7 (Minor: difference-quotient displays in Lemmas 2.9–2.10).  Two local displays have the wrong power or difference order. The preceding product calculation and the intended conclusions identify the corresponding corrections.

On page 19, in the displayed application of Lemma 2.8 after (2.15), the left side is a \(q\)th-power integral, while the right side still has a \(1/q\) power. For \(q=2\), take a nonzero smooth cutoff product \(H=G\xi\) obtained, for example, from \(g(x)=x_1^2\). Both seminorms are finite and positive. Scaling \(g\) by an amplitude \(A\) scales the left side by \(A^2\) but the printed right side by \(A\). No amplitude-independent constant makes that display valid. Remove the \(1/q\) power after raising Lemma 2.8 to the \(q\)th power; the following bound already has \(M^q\).

On page 20, the proof of Lemma 2.10 instead displays \[\sup_{0<|h|<\infty}
       \int_{\mathbb R^n}\frac{|\delta_h(g\xi)|}{|h|^{1+\gamma}}\,dx
       <\infty.\] It must involve \(\delta_h^2(g\xi)\). Indeed, take \(n=2\), \(R=1\), \(h_0=1/10\), \(\gamma=1/2\), and \(g(x)=x_1\) on the required larger ball. Condition (2.16) holds: \(\delta_h^2g=0\) and the first-difference contribution is bounded by \(|B_{7/5}|h_0^{-1/2}\). For a cutoff \(\xi\) equal to one on \(B_{21/20}\) and small \(h=te_1\), however, the displayed integral is at least \[|B_1|\,t^{-1/2}=\pi t^{-1/2}\longrightarrow\infty.\] The second-difference product estimate used immediately beforehand supplies the corrected expression. These are errors in the intermediate displays, not counterexamples to the two embedding lemmas after those corrections.

# Coverage of the proof chain

The tail space, excess functionals, weak form, and all seven SOLA conditions were checked. In particular, Definition 1.7(6) uses \(\limsup_j|\mu_j|(B)\leq|\mu|(\overline B)\), with a closed ball on the right. The fractional embedding condition \(\gamma-n/q\leq\widetilde\gamma-n\) in Lemma 2.4 has the correct direction. The proof of Lemma 2.6 uses the fundamental theorem of calculus and tail integration, accounting for its \(s^{-1}\) factor. The covering arguments require overlap bounds for enlarged balls; those factors must be combined with the volume factors before summing fractional seminorms.

In Section 3, the localization forcing is supported by interactions outside the inner ball. Lipschitz continuity of \(\Phi\) controls its size by the tail and transfers the local Holder exponent without differentiating \(\Phi\). Difference quotients have a uniformly elliptic secant coefficient, so the De Giorgi argument does not require \(\Phi\) to be \(C^1\). The nonlinear rescaling in Lemma 3.8, \[w_R(x)=R^{-s}w(Rx+x_0),\qquad f_R(x)=R^sf(Rx+x_0),\] preserves the argument of \(\Phi\) exactly. It is not arbitrary amplitude homogeneity. The bootstrap exponents may be decreased slightly to avoid the excluded equality \(\beta+\gamma=1\) in Lemma 3.7. The final use of subtracting a constant to replace \(\widetilde E\) by \(E\) is legitimate. The unrestricted homogeneous regularity conclusion was not refuted by the checks above.

For the comparison input, rewriting \(u\) with its own kernel does not by itself make its nonlinear replacement \(v\) solve the equation with that same frozen kernel. The appropriate direct verification uses the secant between the two differences: \[A(x,y)=
 \frac{\Phi(a_u)-\Phi(a_v)}{a_u-a_v},\qquad
 a_w=\frac{w(x)-w(y)}{|x-y|^s}.\] For \(a_u\ne a_v\), Assumption 1.1 gives \(\Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda\). On the zero-difference set one may choose any value in this interval. Oddness gives symmetry. Consequently \(u-v\), which vanishes outside the comparison ball, satisfies a linear uniformly elliptic weak equation with that secant kernel. This verifies the structural route to the comparison estimate, without assuming that the two individual frozen kernels coincide.

The applications of the \(q\)-independent differentiability exponent in Lemma 4.3 and the embedding bootstrap in Lemma 4.4 were followed. One may decrease the free exponent to avoid landing exactly on an infinite Sobolev conjugate. The obstruction in Lemma 4.6 is different: its impossible choice occurs for fixed admissible \(s_0,\sigma\), and requires the replacement recurrence described above. The initial gradient-integrability step in Lemma 4.7 refers to the iterative argument of [kns]. The relevant source passages were read, but a full independent reconstruction of every localization constant in that adaptation is not claimed.

Theorem 1.9 uses a strict intermediate exponent \(\widetilde\sigma\) with \(\widetilde\sigma-n\geq\sigma-n/q\), possible precisely because the target \(q\) lies below its displayed endpoint. Compactness of gradients gives local \(L^1\) convergence along a subsequence; the lower semicontinuity needed for the final nonnegative seminorm bound is supplied by Fatou’s lemma. No pointwise dominating function for that seminorm is furnished merely by a uniform Sobolev bound.

In Section 5, the interpolation exponent \(\theta=1/(1+\kappa)\), the Young-inequality absorption, and the convergence of the geometric tail sum with \(\alpha_1<1<2s\) were checked. For the discrete potential iteration, the outermost radius needs care: a measure concentrated in a thin shell near \(R\) shows that \(|\mu|(B_R)/R^{n-\beta}\) is not bounded by a fixed multiple of \(I_\beta^{|\mu|}(x_0,R)\) alone. One can start the summation one scale inward, controlling the first excess by the initial excess, or use a larger potential radius and subsequently shrink the working ball. This is a required bookkeeping clarification, not a counterexample here to Proposition 5.4.

For passage from the approximating data to the SOLA, a safe order is to retain finite sums of ball masses, pass to the limit using the closed-ball limsup condition at continuity radii, and then increase the number of scales. Weak convergence of measures alone does not justify pointwise dominated convergence of their full potentials. Finally, for the global energy solution, the computed decay \[E(u/R;B_R(x_0))\leq C R^{-n/2-1}\|u\|_{L^2(\mathbb R^n)}
       \longrightarrow0\] has the correct power. The gradient-potential order \(2s-1\) and the Lorentz exponents in Corollary 1.14 agree with the classical potential scaling.
