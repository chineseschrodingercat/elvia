Abstract: The incidence-dimension method supports the principal transversality thresholds after the allowed sign or phase ambiguity is handled explicitly. The assertion that second-moment identifiability always makes growth faster than the fourth power of the noise level necessary is false: a nontrivial compact-group model satisfying the stated dimension conditions admits recovery from its first moment. An explicit estimator establishes the contradiction in mean square. This application-level failure supports a Major assessment. Several local proof, orbit, arithmetic, and citation corrections are also identified, without inferring failure of the main transversality conclusions.

Author: AI-assisted technical review

Title: Transversality, residual ambiguities, and the order of moments needed for recovery

# Source and the actual transversality statements

We review T. Bendory, N. Dym, D. Edidin, and A. Suresh, A Transversality Theorem for Semi-Algebraic Sets with Application to Signal Recovery from the Second Moment and Cryo-EM, Foundations of Computational Mathematics 26 (2026), 2765–2792, DOI 10.1007/s10208-025-09736-4 [paper]. The acquired 28-page publisher file states online publication on 29 September 2025. The review concerns these exact pages. PDF page \(p\) corresponds to journal page \(2764+p\). The complete text was read; the claims below received targeted proof or calculation checks. This does not certify every external dependency or every assertion in the paper.

Write \(D=\dim_{\mathbb R}V\), \(k(H)=\max_x\dim_{\mathbb R}Hx\), and \(K=D-k(H)\). Definition 2.1, PDF p. 5, makes genericity relative to the chosen transformation group and a fixed semi-algebraic prior. The exceptional transformations must have smaller dimension. Theorem 2.2, p. 6, uses \(K>M\) for generic points and \(K>2M\) for every point. Real linear transformations leave a permitted sign ambiguity; real affine transformations give equality. The orthogonal thresholds on p. 7 are \(K>M+2\) and \(K>2M+2\), improved by one for connected orbits.

The proof’s basic calculation, pp. 18–20, is \[\dim\mathcal B\le 2M+\dim\Gamma-\kappa,\] where \(\kappa\) is a lower bound on the codimension of each relevant collision fiber. If \(\kappa>2M\), projection excludes all bad collisions for generic transformations. If \(\kappa>M\), the generic fiber has dimension less than \(M\), so its projection excludes only a lower-dimensional set of prior points. The finite stratification argument provides the required generic-fiber statement. The relation defining relevant collisions needs the correction in Section 3.

The application caveat on p. 8 is substantive: a theorem about generic transformed priors is not a guarantee for a fixed sparsity basis, a particular learned subspace, or a particular trained network. The review retains this distinction.

# A counterexample to the claimed necessary sample growth

Problem 1 (Major: Corollary 4.3).  The conclusion on PDF pp. 16–17 that the minimal necessary sample growth is \(n/\sigma^4\to\infty\) does not follow from the stated second-moment identifiability conditions.

Use precisely the Gaussian-noise MRA model (4.5). Let \(G=SO(2)\) act on \[V=\mathbb R^2\oplus\mathbb R^2,\qquad
 g\cdot(z,w)=(z,R_\theta w).\] This is a nontrivial orthogonal representation of a compact group, with Haar-uniform rotations. The first block is two copies of the trivial representation, and the second is one standard two-dimensional representation. In the notation of (3.4), the block sizes are \((N_1,R_1)=(1,2)\) and \((N_2,R_2)=(2,1)\). Thus \(k=0+1=1\), \(D=4\), and \(K=3\).

Take a known affine-line prior \(\mathcal M=\{p+tu:t\in\mathbb R\}\). Its dimension is \(M=1\), so \(K>2M\), as required by the all-point affine branch of Corollary 3.1, p. 10. Write \(p_0,u_0\in\mathbb R^2\) for the fixed-coordinate components. The condition \(\det(p_0,u_0)\ne0\) is open and generic among affine lines. It implies \(u_0\ne0\).

Both the second moment and the first moment identify \(t\) on these lines. The fixed block of the noise-free second moment is \[(p_0+tu_0)(p_0+tu_0)^T.\] Equality at \(t_1,t_2\) implies \(p_0+t_1u_0=\pm(p_0+t_2u_0)\). The positive sign gives \(t_1=t_2\); the negative sign would make \(p_0,u_0\) dependent. Thus the example meets the actual identifiability conclusion, in addition to its dimension bound.

But the mean is already \((p_0+tu_0,0)\). With \(\overline y_0\) the sample mean of the first two coordinates, define \[\widehat t=\frac{u_0^T(\overline y_0-p_0)}{\|u_0\|^2},
 \qquad \widehat x=p+\widehat t\,u.\] The group action contributes no randomness in these coordinates. Independence and covariance \(\sigma^2 I\) give exactly \[\mathbb E(\widehat t-t)^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\|u_0\|^2},
 \qquad
 \mathbb E\|\widehat x-x\|^2
   =\frac{\|u\|^2\sigma^2}{n\|u_0\|^2}.\] Choose \(n=\lceil\sigma^3\rceil\). Recovery is consistent in mean square, although \(n/\sigma^4\to0\). For the concrete line \(x_t=(t,1,t,1)\), the error is \(2\sigma^2/n\). Genericity does not remove the obstruction, since the same argument holds whenever \(\det(p_0,u_0)\ne0\).

The failed dependency is the inference from a sufficient identifying moment to the lowest identifying moment. It affects an advertised information-theoretic application, hence the Major assessment. A corrected statement should give second-moment sufficiency, or impose and prove appropriate first-moment nonidentifiability and lower-bound hypotheses. This example does not refute deterministic transversality, Corollary 3.1, or the sufficiency wording of the cryo-EM Corollary 4.2. It is not a cryo-EM projection counterexample.

# Two repairs to the general proof framework

Problem 2 (Minor: the equivalence used in the fiber lemma).  The relation defined on PDF p. 18 is not always the sign or phase relation used later in the proof.

The definition is \[x\sim y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Gamma(x,y)=\Gamma,\qquad
 \Gamma(x,y)=\{\theta:\theta x\in H(\theta y)\}.\] For \(V=\mathbb R^2\), choose \[H=\{I,\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)\},\quad
 \Gamma=GL_2(\mathbb R),\quad x=e_1,\quad y=-e_1.\] Here \(K=2\). A collision occurs exactly when the first column of the invertible matrix lies on \(\mathbb R e_1\). Consequently \(\dim\Gamma(x,y)=3\), whereas \(\dim\Gamma-K=2\). The pair is not equivalent under the given definition, since many first columns have two nonzero coordinates. The assertion on p. 21 that the parallel case is empty unless the pair is equivalent therefore does not establish the fiber-lemma hypothesis.

There is a direct repair appropriate to the theorem’s conclusion: define the bad incidence using \(y\ne\pm x\) for real linear and orthogonal transformations. Repeat the incidence argument with that invariant relation. In the complex linear and unitary cases, exclude \(y=\lambda x\), \(|\lambda|=1\). For affine transformations retain equality as the allowed relation. The counterexample above is precisely a pair the real theorem permits; it is not a counterexample to its conclusion.

The remaining column counts on pp. 21–25 check with these exclusions. Independent real vectors give collision codimension at least \(D-k(H)=K\) for \(GL(V)\); for \(O(D)\), the second-column sphere has dimension \(D-2\), giving \(K-2\). For a complex \(n\)-dimensional space, that sphere has real dimension \(2n-3\), giving \(K-3\). In the real affine opposite-vector case, the translation is unique; in the complex affine phase case, it has at most one real parameter. These yield the stated affine offsets.

The connected-orbit improvement can be seen without a special orthogonal-pair exception. Normalize a nonempty collision to \(\|x\|=\|y\|=1\), write \(y=ae_1+be_2\), and put \(w_1=Ae_1\). For independent vectors, \(|a|<1\). Orthogonality of the second column requires \(\operatorname{Re}\langle w_1,hw_1\rangle=\operatorname{Re}a\). At \(hw_1=w_1\) this fails. It therefore cuts a proper algebraic subset of the connected irreducible orbit, lowering dimension by at least one, as required.

Problem 3 (Minor: joint semi-algebraicity).  The pointwise action definition on p. 5 is insufficient for the joint semi-algebraic incidence asserted on pp. 18–19.

Let \(\Gamma=(\mathbb R,+)\) act by \(\theta\cdot(u,v)=(e^\theta u,v)\), let \(H\) consist of identity and coordinate interchange, and take \(\mathcal M=\{e_1\}\cup\{(0,t):t>0\}\). Each fixed-\(\theta\) action is an invertible linear semi-algebraic map, but its collision branch between \(e_1\) and \((0,t)\) is \(t=e^\theta\), which is not semi-algebraic. Requiring the joint map \(\Gamma\times V\to V\) to be semi-algebraic repairs this issue. The actual linear, affine, orthogonal, and unitary actions already satisfy that requirement.

# Orbit dimensions and the cryo-EM formulas

Problem 4 (Minor: connected orbits require a rank condition).  The sentence after (3.4), PDF p. 10, asserts that the orthogonal-product orbits are connected exactly when all irreducible dimensions exceed one. This ignores multiplicity.

For an \(N\times R\) matrix of rank \(r\), the orbit under left multiplication is \(O(N)/O(N-r)\). When \(r<N\), the stabilizer contains a determinant-negative element and the orbit is connected. When \(r=N\), the stabilizer is trivial and the orbit has the two components of \(O(N)\). In particular, \(N=R=2\), \(X=I_2\) gives the disconnected orbit \(O(2)\), although \(N>1\). All orbits in a nonzero block are connected precisely when \(R<N\). For products, apply the condition to every block.

The correction does not invalidate the displayed bounds of Corollary 3.1: that corollary uses the conservative orthogonal offset \(+2\), not the connected-orbit improvement. Products of unitary groups in the complex application are connected, so the real example does not contradict Corollary 7.3.

The stabilizer dimension in (3.4) itself checks. At maximal rank \(r=\min(N,R)\), \[k=\frac{N(N-1)}2-\frac{(N-r)(N-r-1)}2,\qquad
 K=\begin{cases}
 R(R+1)/2,&R\le N,\\
 NR-N(N-1)/2,&R\ge N.
 \end{cases}\] For real Fourier phase retrieval, p. 12, this gives \(K=\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1\). The integer thresholds \(N\ge2M\), \(N\ge4M\), \(N\ge2M+4\), and \(N\ge4M+4\) imply their respective strict inequalities.

Problem 5 (Minor: two displayed cryo-EM simplifications).  The approximation in Theorem 4.1, PDF p. 14, has the wrong sign, and the numerator of (4.4), p. 15, contains an extra factor \(L+1\).

For \(R\ge2L+1\), the exact dimension formula on p. 14 is \[D=R(L+1)^2,\quad
 k=\sum_{\ell=0}^L\binom{2\ell+1}{2}
   =\frac{L(L+1)(4L+5)}6,\quad K=D-k.\] Thus the leading expression is \(L^2(R-2L/3)\); the printed approximation uses a plus sign. The correct ratio is \[\frac KD=
 \frac{R(L+1)^2-L(L+1)(4L+5)/6}{R(L+1)^2}.\] In (4.4), the subtracted term is instead printed as \((L+1)\bigl((L+1)L(4L+5)/6\bigr)\). For \(L=1,R=3\), the exact values are \(D=12,k=3,K=9\), so \(K/D=3/4\); the printed numerator gives \(1/2\).

The exact formula in Theorem 4.1 is correct. It gives \(K/D\to2/3\) when \(R\sim2L\), agreeing with the stated limiting interpretation on p. 15. The resulting generic-point and all-point prior-dimension fractions are \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), subject to the strict inequalities. These independent checks support a local correction of the two displays, not a failure of the exact cryo-EM threshold.

# Sharpness illustration, references, and limits

Problem 6 (Minor: singular parameters in the parabola example).  The numerical witness on PDF p. 8 does not belong to the claimed group \(GL_2\).

For \(ax+by-(cx+dy)^2=0\), the selected coefficients \((1,2,2,4)\) have \(ad-bc=0\). The equation is \((x+2y)-4(x+2y)^2=0\), the union of two parallel lines.

A nonsingular witness is \((a,b,c,d)=(1,5/2,1,1)\), with determinant \(-3/2\). Its parabola contains both \((1,1/2)\) and \((-1,1/2)\). For nonzero \(c,d\), symmetric intersections satisfy \[y=\frac{a}{2cd},\qquad
 x^2=\frac{by-d^2y^2}{c^2}.\] At the corrected coefficients \(y=1/2\) and \(x^2=1>0\); nonzero determinant and strict positivity persist on an open neighborhood. Thus the intended positive-measure sharpness argument survives.

Problem 7 (Minor: citation-to-reference bindings).  Several central body references do not identify their intended sources in the acquired publication.

The following are literal comparisons with the printed bibliography, PDF pp. 26–28. On p. 4 the named Kleiman paper is cited as source reference [17], but [17] is Kam’s electron-micrograph reconstruction paper; the exact named Kleiman title occurs as [36]. On p. 20, Nash stratification and semi-algebraic Sard are cited as [57, Proposition 2.9.10] and [57, Theorem 9.6.2], whereas [57] is Tu’s Manifolds chapter. The pre-image theorem is cited there as [58, Theorem 9.9], but [58] is a paper on sparse multi-reference alignment. The moment-order assertion on pp. 13 and 16 cites [40], which is Deng et al.’s Learning to synthesize: robust phase retrieval at low photon counts.

The acquired publisher HTML reproduces the latter number-to-title bindings, including the proof anchors [57] and [58]. The bibliography contains Bochnak–Coste–Roy’s Real algebraic geometry as [38], but this observation does not verify any replacement theorem locator. The references and links need a systematic correction. These binding failures impede checking proof dependencies; they alone do not establish that the underlying mathematical results are false. The external works were not retrieved or read here.

Finally, the cryo-EM model uses a finite-dimensional truncation, uniform rotations, the stated radial multiplicities, and Gaussian noise. The footnote on p. 14 expressly excludes nonuniform viewing distributions. The discussion on pp. 15–16 does not prove identification of a distribution of continuously varying conformations. Likewise, moment injectivity by itself provides no uniform conditioning constant or reconstruction algorithm.

The permutation application on pp. 17–18 has finite ambiguity group and the consistent count \(nd>2M\). Its warning that arbitrary affine changes need not preserve permutation structure is necessary and is retained. No author code was executed, and no later correction of the acquired source was established.
