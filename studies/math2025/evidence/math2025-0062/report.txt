Abstract: A clopen two-move example disproves the assertion in Lemma 72 that the explicitly defined tree \(Q\) is a quasi-strategy for Player I. The tree restricts Player II and can change a game won by II into one won by I, preventing the selection of the required auxiliary strategy. This is a Major defect in the written proof of the second separation reduction theorem. A replacement using actual winning positions and an additional escape phase repairs this local strategy argument. No counterexample to the determinacy theorem itself is established. Independent scientific acceptance remains pending.

# Problem 1: the tree in Lemma 72 can restrict the wrong player

## Exact source claim

Equation reference: eq:Wi. In §5.2.1, p. 363 (PDF 51), write \[W=\bigcup_i(A_i\cap C_i),\qquad C_i=\bigcup_n C_{i,n},\] where the \(C_i\) are disjoint \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^0_2\) sets and \(C_{i,n}\) are closed. The paper defines \[\begin{aligned}
 W_{i,n}(X)&=\{x:\exists k\ (x\upharpoonright k\in X)\}
                 \cup(A_i\cap C_{i,n}),\\
 \Phi_{i,n}(X)&=\{p:\text{I wins }G(W_{i,n}(X))\text{ from }p\},\\
 \Phi(X)&=\bigcup_{i,n}\Phi_{i,n}(X).\end{aligned}\] Finite sequences are identified with their natural-number codes. Let \(P=\Phi^\infty\).

Equation reference: eq:badQ. Lemma 72 starts on p. 364 (PDF 52) with \(p\notin P\). For a fixed pair \((i,n)\) it considers \[H_{i,n}=\{x:\exists k\ (x\upharpoonright k\in P)\}\cup C_{i,n}.\] In Case II on p. 365 (PDF 53), I wins \(H_{i,n}\). The paper explicitly defines \(Q\) by \[
 q\in Q\ \Longleftrightarrow\
   \bigl(\exists r\preccurlyeq q\ (r\in P)\bigr)\ \lor\ q\in T(C_{i,n}),\] where \(T(C_{i,n})\) is the tree of finite prefixes of the closed set, using the paper’s convention. It then asserts that \(Q\) is a quasi-strategy for I, so an I-winning strategy in \(G(W_{i,n}(P),Q)\) would also win the unrestricted game. Since I does not win the unrestricted game, the proof selects a II-winning strategy in the restricted game. The original PDF image confirms this exact formula and inference.

## Parameters and verification of all local hypotheses

Choose the paired pointclasses \[\Gamma_{2l}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^0_2,\qquad \Gamma_{2l+1}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}^0_2
 \quad(l\in\mathbb N).\] They are definable, effectively treeable Wadge classes: their codes are uniformly closed under union with an open set and intersection with a closed set, and the two classes are dual. Work with the ordinary full interpretation of Baire-space games. In the usual monotone-induction interpretation, strategic replacement and the required fixed points are available in ZFC. The particular fixed point needed below is, more strongly, exhibited directly.

Equation reference: eq:example. Set \[
\begin{aligned}
 A_0&=[1],& C_0&=[0]\cup[1,0],& C_{0,n}&=C_0\quad(n\in\mathbb N),\\
 A_i&=\varnothing,&C_i&=\varnothing,&C_{i,n}&=\varnothing\quad(i>0).
\end{aligned}\] All sets are recursive clopen sets, hence lie in the required classes. The layer family is pairwise disjoint, its closed decomposition is increasing (constant in \(n\)), and \[W=A_0\cap C_0=[1,0].\] Thus layered compatibility also holds. Take the initial position \(p=\varnothing\) and the pair \((i,n)=(0,0)\).

Equation reference: eq:P, eq:fixed. Let \[
 P=\{q\in\mathbb N^{<\mathbb N}:(1,0)\preccurlyeq q\}.\] We verify the fixed point rather than assuming it. From a position extending \((1,0)\), the payoff \(W\) is already guaranteed. From the empty position, II can answer a first move \(1\) with \(1\); from position \((1)\), II can again choose \(1\); and every other position outside \(P\) either still allows this response or has already made \(W\) impossible. Hence \(\Phi(\varnothing)=P\). Reaching \(P\) is equivalent to the final play lying in \([1,0]\), so [eq:Wi] (#eq:Wi) with \(X=P\) again has precisely \(P\) as its I-winning positions (also for the empty layers). Therefore \[
 \Phi(\varnothing)=P=\Phi(P)=\Phi^\infty,\qquad
 \varnothing\notin P.\] This particular induction has one nonempty stage. Even the printed stage-comparison convention can be satisfied for this example by the single-equivalence-class relation \(P\times P\); no transfinite-order ambiguity is needed for the counterexample.

## The claimed and actual outcomes

Because reaching \(P\) is just \([1,0]\), we have \[H_{0,0}=[1,0]\cup C_0=C_0.\] I wins \(H_{0,0}\) by choosing \(0\) initially. Thus the example is in Case II, exactly as required.

Every extension of a position in \(P\) is already a prefix of a real in \(C_0\). Formula [eq:badQ] (#eq:badQ) therefore gives \[Q=T(C_0).\] The node \((1)\) lies in \(Q\), while \((1,1)\) does not. At \((1)\) it is II’s turn. Consequently \(Q\) restricts II’s move \(1\) and is not a quasi-strategy for I. This tree is pruned and has no terminal nodes; dead-end coding is not the explanation.

The unrestricted game \(G(W_{0,0}(P))=G([1,0])\) is won by II: answer the initial move \(1\) with \(1\), and otherwise the payoff is already lost by I. But in the restricted game \(G([1,0],Q)\), I wins by choosing \(1\). If II chooses \(0\), the play lies in \([1,0]\); if II chooses any other number, II immediately loses by moving outside \(Q\). Thus \[\text{unrestricted winner}=\mathrm{II},\qquad
 \text{restricted winner}=\mathrm{I}.\] The II-winning strategy required by the displayed proof does not exist for this example.

The independent script `clopen_game_check.py` checked the nine pairs of move types \(0,1,\geq2\). This is an exact finite quotient of the above cylinder games: all integers at least \(2\) have identical membership and legality effects, and subsequent moves are irrelevant. It confirms, rather than replaces, the analytic verification.

## Counterevidence and alternative readings

There certainly exists a genuine I quasi-strategy for \(H_{0,0}\): I may choose \(0\) and leave every II move free. This does not make the explicitly defined \(Q\) a quasi-strategy. Replacing the displayed set by actual winning positions changes the construction and also requires checking the transition when I leaves the new tree.

The terminology canonical non-losing cannot override the explicit membership formula. Nor does restricting to the component reachable under some other fixed I strategy justify deleting the offending node \((1)\) from the displayed \(Q\). The source’s own game-tree convention makes illegal moves immediate losses, and the example uses that convention exactly.

The final statement of Lemma 72 survives this example: II really does win \(W\). The example refutes the construction and strategy-transfer inference used to prove that statement, not determinacy or the second separation reduction theorem as a statement.

## Core impact

The finding is Major. Lemma 72 completes the implication (3)\(\Rightarrow\)(1) of Theorem 69 and is explicitly used with Lemma 71 on p. 366 (PDF 54) to obtain the comprehension assertion (5.2). These are core parts of the second separation reduction theorem. Conclusions that rely on that proof need a corrected local argument. The evidence does not invalidate every theorem, the independent first reduction argument, or the entire classification program. Repairability does not remove the demonstrated core dependency.

# A precise repair of the local strategy construction

The following replacement addresses both the wrong-player restriction and the exit transition. It is given at the level of the game’s mathematical construction; the entire paper has not been mechanically formalized over \(\mathsf{RCA}_0\).

Fix \(i,n\), put \(C=C_{i,n}\) and \(H=H_{i,n}\), and let \[D=\{q:\text{I has a winning strategy for }H\text{ from }q\}.\] The family of these auxiliary games, for all pairs and starting positions, is countable and uniformly coded from \(P\) and the original codes. The strategic-replacement and effective-treeability hypotheses used in the source provide the winner-index sets and strategies. This use should be explicit; \(D\) is not claimed to be recursive in \(P\) alone.

Equation reference: eq:D. Two elementary observations will be used. First, if I can force a visit to \(P\) from \(q\), then I wins \(W_{i,n}(P)\) from \(q\), so \(q\in\Phi(P)=P\). In particular, \(P\) is closed under extension. Second, \[
 P\subseteq D,\qquad D\setminus P\subseteq T(C).\] For the second inclusion, if \(q\notin T(C)\), membership in the closed part \(C\) is already impossible. Winning \(H\) from \(q\) then means forcing a visit to \(P\), which puts \(q\) in \(P\).

If I wins \(H\) from the current position \(p\notin P\), form \(Q'\) from the histories extending \(p\) all of whose intermediate positions remain in \(D\). At an I position in \(D\), a winning strategy has a next move in \(D\). At a II position in \(D\), every legal next move lies in \(D\), by restricting an I-winning strategy after that move. Thus \(Q'\) is a pruned I quasi-strategy. It does not restrict II.

There is no I-winning strategy for \(G(W_{i,n}(P),Q')\), since such a strategy would win the unrestricted game, contradicting \(p\notin P\). By determinacy and treeability, choose a II-winning strategy for this restricted game. As long as I stays in \(Q'\), follow it. Any infinite play that stays in \(Q'\) avoids \(P\) and lies outside \(A_i\cap C\) by the II-winning property. Equation [eq:D] (#eq:D) puts all its prefixes in \(T(C)\), so it actually lies in \(C\setminus A_i\), hence outside \(W\) by disjointness or layered compatibility.

If I leaves \(Q'\) at a position \(q\), then \(q\notin D\), and therefore \(q\notin P\). Now use a II-winning strategy for \(H\) from \(q\). This additional escape phase avoids \(P\) and must reach a finite position outside \(T(C)\), since otherwise it would lose \(H\). At that position, and only then, continue with the paper’s next pair \((i',n')\).

The countable auxiliary strategies can be collected by the same strategic-replacement hypotheses. If infinitely many pairs are processed, the resulting play avoids every \(C_{i,n}\) and hence \(W\). If the process eventually remains in one \(Q'\), the preceding argument gives a play outside \(W\). An escape phase cannot itself last forever along a II-winning strategy for \(H\). This proves the corrected local II strategy.

This repair verifies the mathematical strategy argument underlying Lemma 72. It adds a winning-position construction and an escape phase; merely relabeling the old \(Q\) is insufficient. Full weak-theory formalization, the reversal proof and all subsequent applications still require separate acceptance checks. Author adoption is neither requested nor observed.

# Scope clarifications and disposition

The printed stage relation (5.1) and Definition 63 on p. 360 (PDF 48) reverse the usual arguments; the later construction on p. 367 (PDF 55) uses \(W_a=\Phi(W_{<a})\) with the usual initial-segment direction. The standard consistent formulation is \[a\leq_\Phi b\ \Longleftrightarrow\
 a,b\in\Phi^\infty\ \wedge\
 \forall\eta\,(b\in\Phi^\eta\Rightarrow a\in\Phi^\eta),
 \qquad a\leq_\Phi b\ \Longleftrightarrow\ a\in\Phi(W_{<b}).\] This is recorded as a notation clarification, not a separate severity-counted finding. Problem 1 uses a one-stage fixed point and remains valid independently of this transposition. Similarly, the introductory one-sided wording of Wadge reducibility is not treated as a scientific finding because the full operational definition appears in §2.
