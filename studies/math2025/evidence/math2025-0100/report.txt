Abstract: One Major and two Minor findings are supported in the acquired author version. The Major concerns the Suzuki-case centralizer calculation used in the core degree argument; an explicit countercalculation and a general repair are given. The Minor findings concern the scope of an equivariant edge-slide lemma and a homotopy lemma for general Lie groups. Neither supplies a counterexample to the principal fixed-point theorem. The corrected Suzuki construction satisfies the properties needed downstream, and the two narrower hypotheses hold in the indicated main applications. This is an initial review, with scientific acceptance pending.

Theorem A asserts the fixed-point property for finite-group actions on finite contractible two-dimensional complexes. The operative notion is the admissible \(G\)-CW action specified in Section 2, not an arbitrary unstructured topological action. The stronger intermediate conclusion is that a fixed-point-free finite acyclic \(G\)-complex has a nontrivial unitary representation of its fundamental group. Theorem B supplies this for \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(2^n)\), \(n\geq2\); \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(3^n)\), odd \(n\geq3\); the stated prime-power cases \(q\equiv11,19\pmod{24}\); and \(\operatorname{Sz}(2^n)\), odd \(n\geq3\). Theorem C is an external result from Part II for \(q>5\), \(q\equiv5,13\pmod{24}\). The exceptional \(q=5\) is covered through \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(5)\cong\operatorname{PSL}_2(4)\). This review does not independently certify Part II.

The internal chain examined is the reduction in Theorem 3.8, Brown’s extension and the representation quotient in Section 5, the character and centralizer construction in Section 7, the dimension and differential arguments in Sections 8–9, the degree computation in Section 10, and the final representation-extension argument in Section 11. The calculations below use \([R,S]=RS-SR\) for a matrix commutator, not the multiplicative group commutator.

# Problem 1: Suzuki centralizers in the selected basis

Classification: Major; demonstrated invalid core proof calculation, with a verified local repair. The location is Proposition 7.10, Suzuki case, p. 21, especially the displayed \(C\) and the following displayed formula for \(C_{\mathrm U(m)}(\rho_0(G_{v_1}))\); the author TeX has the relevant construction at lines 1490–1518, including \(C\) at line 1510 and the centralizer calculation at lines 1513–1518. Parts (ii)–(v) are used by Propositions 10.1–10.3 on pp. 24–25, and thus by Theorem 11.1 and Theorem B on p. 26.

## All parameters and the false equality

Set \[q=2^n,\quad n\geq3\text{ odd},\qquad r=\sqrt{2q},\quad
 h=r/2,\quad s=q/2-1,\quad N=sh,\quad m=(q-1)h=2N+h.\] Here \(h\geq2\). In the source \(J_d\) is the scalar antidiagonal permutation matrix of size \(d\), not a reversal of blocks that preserves coordinates inside each block.

The selected dihedral representative is \[X=\rho_0(x)=
 \operatorname{diag}(\xi I_h,\xi^2 I_h,\ldots,\xi^{q-2}I_h,I_h),
 \qquad Y=\rho_0(y)=J_{2N}\oplus(-I_h),
 \quad \xi=e^{2\pi i/(q-1)} .\]

These matrices have exactly the character used in the proof and satisfy the dihedral relations. In particular all \(q-1\) eigenvalues of \(X\) are distinct as blocks. Write \[A=2^{-1/2}
 \begin{pmatrix}I_N&J_N\\ I_N&-J_N\end{pmatrix}\oplus I_h .\] The printed centralizer of \(\langle X,Y\rangle\) consists of matrices \[\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_s,a_s,\ldots,a_1,b),
 \qquad a_i,b\in\mathrm U(h).
 \tag{1}\] This is false in the displayed basis: the internal \(J_h\) conjugations have been omitted.

For an admissible instance take \(q=8\), \(r=4\), \(h=2\), \(N=6\), and \(m=14\). Choose \(a_1=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) and \(a_2=a_3=b=I_2\). The matrix \[H_{\rm bad}=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,I_2,I_2,I_2,I_2,a_1,I_2)\] belongs to (1) and commutes with \(X\), but \[[H_{\rm bad},Y]_{1,12}=2\ne0.\] For clarity, \(Y_{1,12}=1\), whereas the relevant diagonal entries of \(H_{\rm bad}\) are \(1\) and \(-1\). This is a direct counterexample to the printed centralizer equality in the actual allowed Suzuki parameters. It is not a counterexample to a fixed-point theorem.

There is also a dimensional error in the printed \(C=A^{-1}(I_N\oplus J_N\oplus I_1)A\): its middle matrix has size \(2N+1\), whereas \(A\) has size \(2N+h\). For \(q=8\) these are 13 and 14. This dimensional slip alone is not the basis of the Major classification; the false commutant formula and its use in the core calculation are substantive.

## Complete repair of the required block calculation

The actual centralizer is \[H=C_{\mathrm U(m)}(\langle X,Y\rangle)
 =\{\operatorname{diag}(P,J_NPJ_N,b):
       P=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_s),\ a_i,b\in\mathrm U(h)\}.
 \tag{2}\] Indeed commuting with \(X\) first forces block diagonality with \(h\)-dimensional blocks. Commuting with \(J_{2N}\) then identifies the opposite blocks by conjugation by \(J_h\), exactly (2). The last block is unrestricted because \(Y\) is \(-I_h\) there.

Replace \(C\) by \[D=I_N\oplus J_N\oplus I_h,\qquad C=A^{-1}DA.
 \tag{3}\] Both \(A\) and \(C\) are unitary. Direct multiplication gives \[AYA^{-1}=I_N\oplus(-I_{N+h}).\] Since \(D\) commutes with this diagonal matrix, \(C\) commutes with \(Y\), proving (iv). If \(K=C_{\mathrm U(m)}(X)=\mathrm U(h)^{q-1}\), the block splitting at \(N=sh\) gives \(K\subseteq\mathrm U(N)\times\mathrm U(N+h)\). Thus \(A^{-1}KA\subseteq C_{\mathrm U(m)}(Y)\), proving (iii).

For (v) we have \[AC=DA=2^{-1/2}
 \begin{pmatrix}I_N&J_N\\ J_N&-I_N\end{pmatrix}\oplus I_h .
 \tag{4}\] The upper right block of \((AC)\operatorname{diag}(P,J_NPJ_N,b)\) is \(PJ_N/\sqrt2\), and its lower left block is \(J_NP/\sqrt2\); the same blocks occur in the reverse product. The diagonal blocks also agree. Hence (4) commutes with every member of the full centralizer (2), as required. Finally, \[\dim H=(s+1)h^2=q^2/4,\qquad
 \dim K=(q-1)h^2=(q-1)q/2,\qquad
 \dim K-\dim H=q(q-2)/4>0.\] This proves (ii) with the original strict inequality. The Borel irreducibility used for (i) is unaffected: the character-norm computation from Proposition 7.8 equals one. All of these repairs hold for every originally allowed \(q\), not only \(q=8\).

## Impact, counterevidence, and limits

The proof on p. 25 expressly invokes (v) to produce the equivariant homotopy from \(Y_0\) to \(Z\). The original displayed centralizer is therefore part of the main degree argument. This dependency supports Major under the supplied core-contribution standard, even though the repair is local and complete at this step. The abstract group type and its dimension were correct, and the errors do not refute Proposition 7.10’s existential conclusion or Theorems A/B. Equations (2)–(4) repair the needed construction without narrowing \(q\) or changing the representation character. They do not independently reprove the external character classification on which the construction relies. Exact reviewer-written SymPy calculations checked the \(q=8\) commutator, all 28 basis elements of the cyclic commutant algebra, and all 16 basis elements of the corrected dihedral commutant algebra; the preceding general proof supplies the unbounded statement.

# Problem 2: edge slides require different edge orbits

Classification: Minor; false auxiliary lemma in its stated generality. Lemma 3.9, p. 7, author TeX lines 564–572, assumes a finite \(G\)-graph with adjacent edges \(e=\{u,v\}\), \(e'=\{v,w\}\) and \(G_{e'}\subseteq G_e\). It claims that removing \(G\cdot e'\) and attaching an orbit of edges from \(u\) to \(w\) preserves the \(G\)-homotopy type. No different-orbits hypothesis appears.

Let \(G=C_4\) act by rotation on the four-cycle with vertices \(v_i\) and distinct edge cells \(e_i\) from \(v_i\) to \(v_{i+1}\), indices modulo four. This is a finite admissible \(G\)-CW graph. Set \(u=v_0\), \(v=v_1\), \(w=v_2\), \(e=e_0\), \(e'=e_1\). All edge stabilizers are trivial, so the stated inclusion holds; the adjacent vertices and edge cells are distinct. However \(G\cdot e=G\cdot e'\) consists of all four edges. After removing that orbit, the prescribed new free orbit has four distinct cells from \(v_i\) to \(v_{i+2}\). Two parallel cells join \(v_0\) to \(v_2\), and two join \(v_1\) to \(v_3\). Consequently the original graph is connected and the new graph has two components: \[\operatorname{rank}H_0(X^1;\mathbb Z)=1,\qquad
 \operatorname{rank}H_0(Y^1;\mathbb Z)=2.\] They are not even nonequivariantly homotopy equivalent. The proposed homotopy in the proof travels along \(e\), which has been removed in this example.

A sufficient repair is to require \(G\cdot e\ne G\cdot e'\). Then the entire orbit of \(e\) remains in \(X^1-G\cdot e'\). Move the first attaching endpoint from \(v\) to \(u\) along this retained edge and keep \(w\) fixed. Since \(G_{e'}\subseteq G_e\), that path is fixed by \(G_{e'}\) and extends to a well-defined \(G\)-homotopy on the attaching orbit. Equivariant attachment along homotopic maps gives the claimed equivalence.

The use in Proposition 3.10, pp. 7–8, concerns rearranging distinct edge orbits; even in the Suzuki case its two orbits of the same stabilizer type are different orbits. No failure of that application or of Theorem B has been established. Thus this supported scope defect is Minor. The repair changes the lemma’s hypotheses and cannot justify the false same-orbit case.

# Problem 3: an unstated path-component condition

Classification: Minor; false first assertion of an auxiliary homotopy lemma. Lemma 5.9, p. 14, author TeX lines 1012–1017, first claims that the maps on \(\mathcal M\) induced by \(ww'\) and \(w i_v(g)w'\) are homotopic for a general target Lie group \(\mathcal G\). Its proof chooses a path from \(1\) to \(\rho_0(g)\) without a hypothesis guaranteeing that path. The connected double-centralizer condition is stated only for the subsequent quotient assertion.

Take \(G=C_2\) acting trivially on a one-edge tree \(X^1\), with root vertex \(v_0\). Every vertex and edge stabilizer is \(G\), and Brown’s extension has \(\Gamma=G\). Choose the compact zero-dimensional Lie group \(\mathcal G=\{1,-1\}\) and the nontrivial representation \(\rho_0:G\to\mathcal G\). Then \(\mathcal M=C_{\mathcal G}(\rho_0(G))=\{1,-1\}\) is nonempty. The normalized vertex gauge group is also \(\{1,-1\}\), acts freely, and has a one-point quotient, so the preceding moduli setup causes no obstruction. For \(w=w'=1\) and nonidentity \(g\in G_{v_0}\), the two maps on \(\mathcal M\) are respectively the constant maps \(1\) and \(-1\). They cannot be homotopic: restricting a proposed homotopy to any point of \(\mathcal M\) would produce a path between distinct points of a discrete space.

For the first assertion it suffices to add \(\rho_0(g)\in\mathcal G^\circ\), or to assume \(\mathcal G\) connected. For the quotient assertion the chosen path must lie in the identity component of the double centralizer; the lemma’s printed connectedness hypothesis already ensures this. This is not a counterexample to that second conditional assertion.

All uses in the main degree proof have \(\mathcal G=\mathrm U(m)\). This target and its subgroup centralizers, including the double centralizers, are connected; the finite-dimensional commutant decomposition gives products of unitary groups. Theorem 4.5 is therefore adequate for the application in Proposition 10.5. No main-theorem failure follows from this general-Lie-group overstatement. The repair does not assert that arbitrary disconnected targets have the claimed homotopies.

# Checks of the remaining proof chain

## Reduction, representations, and quotient

The definitions in Sections 2–3 specify the family of solvable stabilizers and admissible cells. For Theorem 3.8, the reverse induction on nontrivial stabilizers removes excess cells in \(X^H\) while retaining the Oliver–Segev tree. Cells outside that tree have exact stabilizer \(H\), so the residual \(N_G(H)/H\) action on them is free. Equivariant extension of the retraction over their zero-, one-, and two-cells is possible because the target tree is contractible. Conjugate fixed sets intersect in already retained higher-stabilizer sets, so the retractions glue. The pushout preserves homology, while the retraction on the one-skeleton supplies the needed epimorphism on \(\pi_1\). An isomorphism of fundamental groups is neither proved nor required. The orbit Euler characteristic then yields \(k+1\) free two-cell orbits after \(k\) extra free one-cell orbits.

The relations in Theorem 5.2 are consistent with the ordered products defining \(\tau_v\) and with the inverses in the edge formula. Root normalization in Theorem 5.3 makes the compact gauge action free; equality of its orbits is equality of the represented homomorphisms, not merely equality of conjugacy classes. The unitary centralizers give compact connected smooth factors and an orientable quotient. In Proposition 5.5 every group element fixes a vertex: its cyclic subgroup is solvable, and the required fixed graph is nonempty. Hence a universal representation has the same character as \(\rho_0\). Irreducibility at the root Borel subgroup makes the intertwiner scalar and gives the unique universal point in the normalized quotient.

## Characters, dimensions, and differentials

For the even, odd, and Suzuki characters, the restrictions to the Borel have squared norms \[\frac{(q-1)^2+(q-1)}{q(q-1)}=1,\quad
 \frac{(q-1)^2/4+(q-1)(q+1)/4}{q(q-1)/2}=1,\] \[\frac{(q-1)^2(2q)/4+(q-1)(2q)/4+q(q-1)(2q)/4}
 {q^2(q-1)}=1.\] The respective positive centralizer-dimension gaps are \(q/2-1\), \((q-3)/4\), and \(q(q-2)/4\) on the allowed parameter ranges. The non-Suzuki block calculations were checked against the displayed matrices. The Suzuki correction is given in Problem 1.

Lemma 8.1 uses the split exact cellular chain sequence over \(\mathbb C\) for a finite group and the resulting identity of permutation characters. Frobenius reciprocity gives Corollary 8.2. The real dimension of a unitary centralizer is the sum of the squares of character multiplicities, so there is no complex-versus-real dimension mismatch. The dimensions of the global and root-Borel centralizers are both one and cancel; the \(k+1\) free two-cell orbits then give \(\dim\overline{\mathcal M}_k=(k+1)m^2\).

For Lemma 9.1, acyclicity implies surjectivity of the integral cellular coboundary into degree two. Applying the group-ring involution to the resulting identity gives \[1=\sum_e s_eN(G_e)x_e,\qquad
 N(G_e)=\sum_{h\in G_e}h .\] On any representation this implies \(V=\sum_es_eV^{G_e}\). No division in the integral group ring is assumed. Applied to the real adjoint representation, the identity gives surjectivity of the differential in Proposition 9.2 through the explicit derivative formula in Theorem 5.7. The latter follows by differentiating the ordered product; the signs and adjoint transports telescope as stated. Lemma 5.8 gives \(dX=A\,dW\) because commutator derivatives vanish at a representation trivial on \(N\). The quotient map is a submersion; together with equal dimensions this transfers regularity to Corollary 9.4.

## Degree, nontriviality, and Theorem A

In Proposition 10.1, dropping the first edge factor and the corresponding vertex gauge factor leaves a quotient whose dimension is smaller than \(\dim\mathrm U(m)\) by the positive gap just calculated. The remaining compact gauge action is free, and Sard’s theorem gives nonsurjectivity. In Proposition 10.2 the substitution \(\widehat\tau_{\eta_1}=\tau_{\eta_1}C^{-1}A^{-1}\tau_{\eta_0}A\) belongs to the required centralizer by (iii)–(iv); the matrix order in Table 5, including the conjugation tail when \(q\equiv11\pmod{24}\), agrees with \(T(\widehat\tau)=Z(\tau)\). With Problem 1 repaired, (v) and connected double centralizers supply the equivariant homotopy in Proposition 10.3.

The actual source and target of every degree argument are compact oriented connected manifolds without boundary of equal dimension. The informal general degree paragraph omits some of these qualifications, but they hold in its applications. The free-edge factors give \(\overline{\mathcal M}_k=\overline{\mathcal M}_0\times\mathrm U(m)^k\). The first factor has degree zero, so the product has degree zero regardless of the orientation signs of inversion on the other factors. Lemma 5.9, with its valid unitary hypotheses, removes vertex constants by homotopy, not by an equality of group words. Thus Proposition 10.5 obtains degree zero for the required word map.

Theorem 11.1 correctly needs only one regular preimage of the identity. If it were the sole preimage, the identity would be a regular value with signed degree \(1\) or \(-1\), contradicting degree zero. The second point is nonuniversal by Proposition 5.5 and yields a nontrivial representation of the kernel quotient. Acyclicity supplies the normal-generation condition modulo \([N,N]\).

Finally \(Y=X^N\) in the proof of Theorem A is an acyclic subcomplex with an essential action of \(G/N\). A lower-dimensional finite acyclic graph would be a tree and have a fixed point, so it cannot create an unhandled fixed-point-free case. Theorem B and the external Theorem C cover the classified groups. Proposition 11.2 gives a balanced relative presentation with nonsingular exponent-sum matrix; the relative acyclic chain complex explains that condition. Gerstenhaber–Rothaus applies to the compact connected unitary target and extends the nontrivial representation of \(\pi_1(Y)\) to \(\pi_1(X)\). Injectivity of \(\pi_1(Y)\to\pi_1(X)\) is not assumed: nontriviality of its represented restriction suffices. A finite prime-power enumeration through \(10000\) checked 632 admissible cases as a supplementary residue check, not as a proof of the unbounded classification.
