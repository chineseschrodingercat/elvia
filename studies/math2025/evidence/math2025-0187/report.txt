Abstract: The acquired arXiv version is reviewed from its definitions through the height bound, Galois-orbit deduction and reduction for lines. The principal construction of a single relation valid at all relevant non-archimedean places is supported: the finite product is indexed by signed divisors of an isogeny degree, not by the number of places. Three local concerns are established. A closed convergence boundary admits an undefined evaluation; the global-relation lemma fails to exclude poles of its parameter; and the optional effectivity argument incorrectly asserts descent to a rank-two rational differential system. The last concern has an explicit admissible cover with incompatible local monodromies above one parameter value. The main proof uses the general G-function theorem and survives these scoped objections. The assessment is Minor; scientific adjudication remains pending.

# Positive verification of the main construction

## Periods and isogeny relations

The symbolic normalization in Lemma 3.1, pp. 14–15, is consistent: \[\alpha^2=E_6/E_4,\qquad u=\alpha^{-1},\qquad
 \psi^*\omega=\alpha\,\widetilde\omega.\] The displayed initial coefficients of \(\alpha\) need correction, as discussed below, but the symbolic proof uses the correct ratio.

The regularity of the second period at the nodal limit can also be checked directly. In the coordinate change \[x=u^2\widetilde x+r,\qquad
 y=u^3\widetilde y+u^2s\widetilde x+t,\] comparison of \(a_1=1\), \(a_2=a_3=0\) on the two Weierstrass models gives \[s=(u-1)/2,\quad r=(u^2-1)/12,\quad t=-r/2.\] Consequently, for \(\eta=(x+1/12)\omega\), \[\psi^*\eta
 =u(\widetilde x+1/12)\widetilde\omega.\] Tate’s Laurent expansion on a fixed annulus has constant term \(-2\sum_{n\geq1}\sigma_1(n)q^n\) for \(\widetilde x\). Contour integration therefore gives the additional check \[G=\left(\frac{E_2}{12\alpha}\right)\circ\theta,\qquad G(0)=1/12,\] where \(E_2=1-24\sum_{n\geq1}\sigma_1(n)q^n\) and \(\theta\) is the inverse series of \(1/j\). This verifies holomorphy at zero and rational coefficients, rather than inferring absence of a pole only from a rational linear combination of \(F\) and \(F'\). The corresponding Tate formula and invariant differential were checked in the acquired primary reference [Tate].

For the complex calculation, the pullback matrix of an isogeny is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) with \(ad=M\); the homology matrix has determinant \(M\) as well. The Legendre relation yields \[-aG(s_2)F(s_1)+bF(s_2)F(s_1)+dF(s_2)G(s_1)=\frac r{2\pi i}.\] When \(r=0\), this is already a nonzero algebraic relation. Otherwise a second isogeny eliminates \(1/(2\pi i)\). The monomial with coefficient \(ra'\) remains nonzero even after \(Y_1=Y_3\), \(Z_1=Z_3\). This is precisely the noncollapse needed when the two coordinate pairs share one coordinate; it does not require four distinct coordinates.

For a non-archimedean embedding, the Tate lift is multiplication by an integer \(m\) dividing \(M\), with either sign permitted. Pullback of \(du/u\) gives \[aF(s_1)=mF(s_2).\] Thus \[P_{\rm fin}(Y_1,Y_2)=
       \prod_{\substack{m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}\\m\mid M}}
                  (aY_1-mY_2)\] is nonzero, homogeneous, and has degree \(2d(M)\). The scalar \(a\) is algebraic and fixed independently of the embedding. No factor indexed by an unbounded set of places is introduced. Cyclicity is unnecessary for this particular step. The toric case \(q_1=q_2=0\) in Proposition 4.6 can be handled directly by the group maps \(z\mapsto z^m\); the later elliptic application has nonzero Tate parameters.

## The covering construction and the global product

Lemma 5.1, pp. 26–27, uses a function with its only zero at the chosen degeneration point, takes a root to remove its ramification, and passes to a Galois closure. The resulting simple zeros of \(x\) and the automorphisms over \(x\) are essential: a point small in one embedding may be near a different zero than it is in another embedding.

In Lemma 5.5 the Taylor coordinates of all branches are algebraic. Their coefficient bounds and the separation of the finitely many zeros give radii positive everywhere and at least one almost everywhere. The constructed branches account for all \(\deg(x)\) preimages, which is the reason no extra branch is missed. Rational identities with a presentation denominator should be continued as meromorphic identities across removable zeros of that denominator. This supplies the intended equality even where a particular quotient presentation is \(0/0\).

The additional rational G-function \[H(X)=\frac{\zeta}{\zeta-X}\] enforces \[R'_\iota=\min\{1,R(H^\iota),R(F_\lambda^\iota),
                        R(G_\lambda^\iota):\lambda\in\Lambda\}
       \leq r_\iota.\] Weak approximation can make \(\zeta\) sufficiently small at the finitely many constrained embeddings. At every remaining embedding, the minimum with one already supplies the required bound. No interpretation of a non-archimedean value of \(G_\lambda\) is used to prove the local vanishing.

There is a useful field-bookkeeping clarification in Lemma 5.8, pp. 35–36. For each \(k\), choose a field \(\widehat K_k/K(s)\) of degree at most \(144\) defining the two transported isogenies, and construct its factors over that field. Then place all factors in their compositum. One need not assert that a single field of degree \(144\) defines all the isogenies simultaneously. Every embedding of the compositum restricts to an embedding of each \(\widehat K_k\), so the appropriate factor still vanishes. The degree estimate is the sum of the individual bounds: \[\deg Q\leq
 288\ell[K(s):\mathbb Q]+2\ell\,d(MT^2).\] This preserves the stated estimate without a degree bound on the whole compositum.

Generic isogeny representatives do not merge distinct original coordinates for a fixed \(k\), because of Hodge genericity. Consequently each substituted factor is nonzero, including when one coordinate is shared by the exceptional pairs. The product is nonzero in a polynomial domain even when variables from different \(k\)’s coincide.

## Nontriviality and the height-to-orbit deduction

In Section 5.I, nonisotriviality and the normal-monodromy theorem give the full product \(\operatorname{SL}_2^\Lambda\), not a diagonal subgroup: a connected normal subgroup of the product having nontrivial projection to every simple factor must be the whole product. Analytic continuation of a period matrix along loops fills a Zariski-dense subset of its determinant torsor. It follows that the graph closure over the base is the product of that base with the torsors. Projection to the first columns is dominant, giving the claimed algebraic independence of the \(F_\lambda,G_\lambda\).

The substitution of the nonzero rational function \(H\) in Corollary 5.12 does not create a hidden zero polynomial. If the original polynomial is homogeneous of total degree \(d\), a fixed monomial in the \(Y_\lambda,Z_\lambda\) has a uniquely determined power of the extra variable \(W\). After \(W=H(X)\) that coefficient remains nonzero. This explains why homogeneity is required here.

For Lemma 5.7, basis-change coefficients over \(K(C_0)\) become algebraic functions of \(X\) and need not lie in \(K(X)\). Closure of G-functions under multiplication by algebraic functions supplies the intended conclusion; any finite Laurent pole in an intermediate coefficient is cleared before using the already established holomorphy. This correction is distinct from the false rank-two descent assertion in Problem 3.

Finally, put \(D=[K(s):K]\) and assume \(M\geq N\). The height estimate and isogeny bound give, after choosing \(\epsilon\) appropriately, \[M\leq M'\leq C\max\{D^a,M^{1/2}\}.\] Here \(M\leq M'\) follows because, for non-CM elliptic curves, \(\operatorname{Hom}(E_1,E_2)\) has rank one and a cyclic isogeny is primitive: a nontrivial integer multiple has a kernel containing full torsion and cannot be cyclic. If \(M>C^2\), the second term cannot dominate; the remaining case is absorbed into a constant since \(D\geq1\). This gives the required polynomial lower bound for \(D\). The finite covering degree controls descent to the original point. Points with a CM curve in either isogenous pair have two CM coordinates and are exactly excluded by \(\Sigma\).

For lines with constant coordinates, projection to nonconstant coordinates is injective on the line and its image intersects infinity. The residual mixed modular relations are the Habegger–Pila modular Mordell–Lang case. A constant CM coordinate or a relation among two constant coordinates would contradict the stated Hodge-generic hypothesis. The unequal degrees zero and positive in a constant/nonconstant pair permit the Habegger–Pila height comparison. Together with the mixed-pair orbit bound and cyclic minimality, the isogeny estimate also controls the second non-CM modular degree, as required for the full special complexity. The CM-coordinate cases belong to the imported non-strongly-special argument. The bounded primary-reference checks support these isogeny and modular-orbit inputs [GR, HP]; the complete point-counting theorem is an imported dependency.

# Confirmed local concerns

Problem 1 (A convergence boundary without convergence: Minor).  Lemma 2.1, p. 8, assumes only \(g\in K[[X]]\) and \(|x|\leq R^\dagger(g)\), and then evaluates \(g(x)\). These hypotheses do not ensure that the evaluation exists.

Take \(K=\mathbb Q_p\), \(g(X)=\sum_{n\geq0}X^n\), and \(x=1\). The coefficient definition on p. 7 gives \(R^\dagger(g)=R(g)=1\). Thus the printed closed inequality holds. But the summands at \(x=1\) do not tend to zero; successive partial sums differ by one, so the series is not Cauchy in \(\mathbb Q_p\). This is a direct failure of the evaluation domain, not a numerical violation inferred from analytic continuation.

Replace the closed condition by \(|x|<R^\dagger(g)\), or retain the boundary only with an explicit convergence assumption. In the strict interior, \[|b_nx^n|\leq (|x|/R^\dagger(g))^n\longrightarrow0,\] and the source’s coefficient estimate proves the stated inequality. If convergence is separately assumed on the boundary, the same estimate passes to the limit. The uses in Lemma 2.2 and Section 5 all have a strict radius inequality. Their actual required estimates survive, which limits this finding to the preliminary statement.

Problem 2 (The global-relation parameter can be infinite: Minor).  Lemma 5.8, p. 34, quantifies over all \(s\in C_0^*(\overline{\mathbb Q})\), but concludes that there is a global relation at \(x(s)\). The definition on p. 9 requires a finite algebraic parameter. The construction of \(C_0^*\) on p. 27 does not remove poles of \(x\).

Here is an example within the paper’s permitted geometry. Let \[(J_1,J_2,J_3)=
 \left(\frac{2048}{3},\frac{35152}{9},
                       \frac{28756228}{3}\right),\qquad
 \nu(t)=(J_1+t,J_2+t,J_3+t).\] Take \(C\) to be this line, \(C_4=\mathbb P^1_t\), and \(x=1/t\). All coordinates are nonconstant, the line meets full infinity, and no coordinate pair is identical. It is Hodge generic: a nontrivial classical modular correspondence is irreducible of degree greater than one in each coordinate and cannot contain a distinct affine line. The sole zero of \(x\) is the simple zero at infinity, and \(x:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^1\) is Galois of degree one. These are exactly the requirements in Lemma 5.1.

At \(t=0\), the following smooth elliptic curves supply the exceptional isogenies: \[\begin{aligned}
 E_1&:y^2=z^3+z^2+z,\\
 E_2&:y^2=z^3-2z^2-3z,\\
 E_3&:y^2=z^3-14z^2+z.
\end{aligned}\] The quotient of \(E_1\) by \((0,0)\) is \(E_2\). After translating the point \((3,0)\) of \(E_2\) to the origin, its equation has coefficients \(A=7\), \(B=12\); the next degree-two quotient has \(A=-14\), \(B=1\). Thus there are isogenies \(E_1\to E_2\) of degree two and \(E_1\to E_3\) of degree four. The formulas \[c_4=16(A^2-3B),\quad
 \Delta=16B^2(A^2-4B),\quad
 j=256\frac{(A^2-3B)^3}{B^2(A^2-4B)}\] give exactly \(J_1,J_2,J_3\) above. None equals zero or \(1728\), and all are nonintegral rational numbers, hence non-CM. The \(1/j\) family fibers are isomorphic to these curves over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\), so the required isogenies exist for the actual families.

Therefore \(0\in C_0^*(\overline{\mathbb Q})\) and the distinct-pair hypotheses hold, while \(x(0)=\infty\). The promised relation at this parameter is not defined. An implicit reading that evaluates only finite parameters is precisely the missing restriction, not a consequence of the printed \(C_0^*\) definition.

Remove the poles of \(x\) before Lemma 5.8. There are finitely many, so their fixed heights can be treated separately in height bounds. For the non-CM cyclic-isogeny orbit bound, each fixed pair has at most one cyclic degree, so its finitely many exceptions can also be absorbed into the constants. If projective height is used, \(h(\infty)=0\) already handles the height assertion at a pole, but it does not define a G-function evaluation there. No failure of the nonexceptional global product or of the main finiteness conclusion follows.

Problem 3 (Rank-two descent in the effectivity remark: Minor).  Remark 5.14, p. 39, asserts that every column \((F_\lambda,G_\lambda)^{\mathsf T}\) satisfies a rank-two system over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}(X)\) because the differential forms are a basis over the function field of \(C_0\). A basis over \(K(C_0)\) does not imply descent to \(K(x)\). The asserted descent fails for an allowed cover.

Begin with the line \(C=\{(u,u+1,u+2)\}\) and set \[x_1(u)=\frac1{u(1-u)}.\] Its only zero is at \(u=\infty\), with order two. The normalization of the square-root base change used in Lemma 5.1 is explicitly \[C_4=\mathbb P^1_t,\qquad
 u=\frac1{1+t^2},\qquad x=t+t^{-1},\qquad x^2=x_1(u).\] This degree-two map \(x\) is Galois, with involution \(t\mapsto t^{-1}\). Its two zeros \(\pm i\) are simple and map to \(u=\infty\); take \(K=\mathbb Q(i)\). Thus this is an instance of the construction, not a different choice of base outside its hypotheses. The six possible \(j\) functions are \[u,\ u+1,\ u+2,\ 1-u,\ 2-u,\ 3-u.\] They are distinct affine functions of \(u\), so no two are generically isogenous by the same modular-correspondence argument. In particular the representative with \(j=u\) is present.

For this representative, the actual \(1/j\) family has invariants \[c_4=\frac{j}{j-1728},\qquad
 c_6=-\frac{j}{j-1728},\qquad
 \Delta=\frac{j^2}{(j-1728)^3}.\] At \(t=0\), \(j=u=1\) and the elliptic family is smooth; its local rank-two monodromy is the identity. At \(t=\infty\), put \(r=1/t\). Then \(j=r^2/(1+r^2)\), and the orders of \(c_4,c_6,\Delta\) are \(2,2,4\).

One can verify the latter monodromy without relying on a table of fiber types. Write the short Weierstrass equation as \(Y^2=Z^3+a(r)Z+b(r)\), with \(\operatorname{ord}(a)=
\operatorname{ord}(b)=2\) and nonzero leading coefficient of \(b\). After \(r=v^3\), \(Z=v^2Z_1\), \(Y=v^3Y_1\), the family has good limit \(Y_1^2=Z_1^3+b_0\) with \(b_0\ne0\). The deck transformation acts on this elliptic curve by \[(Z_1,Y_1)\longmapsto(\zeta_3 Z_1,Y_1),\] giving nontrivial order-three monodromy on its first cohomology.

Both \(t=0\) and \(t=\infty\) lie unramified above \(x=\infty\). A connection pulled back from a rank-two system over \(\mathbb C(x)\) would have conjugate local monodromies at these two branches. Identity and nontrivial order-three monodromy cannot be conjugate. Nor can the claimed equation hold accidentally only for the chosen period column: first-column algebraic independence over the algebraic extension \(\mathbb C(C_4)/\mathbb C(x)\) forces a rational coefficient matrix for that column to equal the actual Gauss–Manin matrix.

This refutes the rank-two shortcut in the optional effectivity remark. Proposition 5.13 itself applies Theorem 2.8 to G-functions, which does not require that shortcut. A correct rational system can be constructed by restriction of scalars: a rank-two differential module over \(K(C_4)\) gives rank \(2\deg(x)\) over \(K(x)\), with the corresponding algebraic multiples of the period functions. An effective treatment must supply this enlarged system and its relation ideal and singularities. This review does not claim to have computed all effective constants. The established impact is on the auxiliary rank and descent assertion, not the main height theorem.

# Further corrections and limits

The height-machine display on p. 39 needs an additive constant or the convention \(h^+=\max\{1,h\}\). This is visible within the isogeny example of Problem 2: keep its line and point \(t=0\), but choose \(x=1/(t+1)\). All the covering conditions still hold, and the same exceptional isogenies occur at \(x=1\). Then \(h(x)=0\) and \(h(J_1)=\log 2048>0\), so the displayed \[|h(x)-c_{22}h(j)|\leq c_{23}h(x)\] fails for every positive \(c_{22}\). The needed valid comparison is \(h(j(s))\leq A h(x(s))+B\) for constants fixed by the curve and the two nonconstant maps. The isogeny estimate already has \(D\geq1\) in its maximum, so this replacement supplies the same subsequent bound. The missing bounded-height convention is recorded explicitly, without asserting that the final exponent calculation fails.

Other corrections are retained as display or proof clarifications. The coefficient formula defining \(R^\dagger\) is the operative one; the alternative infimum on p. 7 has its scaling reversed and can also be affected by a discrete value group. It should not be repaired merely by reciprocating that infimum for every complete field. The strict growth bound at \(n=0\) in the G-function definition needs the customary harmless multiplicative constant. The correct initial expansion is \[\alpha=1-372q+10692q^2+\cdots;\] the printed \(1+372q+127692q^2+\cdots\) belongs to \(\alpha^{-1}\). The subsequent symbolic ratio and invariant differential calculation are consistent with the corrected \(\alpha\). The missing negation in the explanatory sentence about diagonal hyperplanes on p. 2 is likewise not used to reverse the actual Hodge-generic hypothesis in the theorem.

For Theorem 2.8, enlarge the finite exceptional set in its proof to include poles of the differential system. The homogeneous-ideal elimination in Lemma 2.9 is supported away from the finitely many leading-coefficient zeros. The theorem about global relations itself, including the vacuous-convergence case mentioned in Remark 2.7, remains an explicitly imported arithmetic result. No unproved transcendence statement is substituted for it.
