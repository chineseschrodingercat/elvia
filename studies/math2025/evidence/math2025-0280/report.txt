# F1. Minor: the short-length statement needs a restriction

Location. Theorem 4(1), p. 4; its denominator remark, p. 5; the actual short-length result, Theorem 49, p. 47.

Theorem 4(1) allows some fixed \(\varepsilon_0>0\), whereas Theorem 49 explicitly requires \(\varepsilon_0\in(0,1)\). The difference matters. Take the exact sequence \[\varepsilon_0=1,\qquad L(g)=g^{-1}.\] It satisfies both printed hypotheses: \(L(g)\ge(1-\varepsilon_0)\log g=0\) and \(L(g)^2/g=g^{-3}\to0\).

For completeness, the zero-denominator problem occurs with probability tending to one; it is not confined to an exceptional surface. For \(0<L\le1\), the collar lemma, Theorem 13, makes every primitive geodesic of length at most \(L\) simple. Theorems 8, 10 and 12 give \[\mathbb E_g N_{\rm nsep}^{s}(X,L)
 =\frac1{2V_g}\int_0^L xV_{g-1,2}(x,x)\,dx
 \le C L^2,\] because \(V_{g-1,2}/V_g=O(1)\) and \(\sinh(x/2)/(x/2)\) is bounded for \(0\le x\le1\). For separating curves, Theorem 8 has constants at most one. Theorems 11 and 12 therefore give \[\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor g/2\rfloor}\mathbb E_g N_{\rm sep,i}^{s}(X,L)
 \le C L^2\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor g/2\rfloor}
                   \frac{V_{i,1}V_{g-i,1}}{V_g}
 =O(L^2/g).\] Consequently \(\mathbb E_gN(X,L)\le CL^2\), and Markov’s inequality yields \[\mathbb P_g\{N(X,g^{-1})\ge1\}\le Cg^{-2}\longrightarrow0.\] On the complementary event both numerator and denominator of the displayed ratio in Theorem 4(1) are zero. No value for that ratio is specified. If the event is read as requiring a defined ratio, consistently with the restriction to \(N>0\) in the p. 5 remark, its probability is at most \(Cg^{-2}\), rather than tending to one.

Counterevidence and effect. The p. 5 argument expressly invokes \(L(g)\to\infty\); Theorem 49 has the missing restriction. Thus the defect is a boundary quantifier and well-posedness error in the introductory formulation. It does not refute the growing-length transition proved by the subsequent estimates.

Repair boundary. Replace \(\varepsilon_0>0\) in Theorem 4(1) by \(0<\varepsilon_0<1\), as in Theorem 49. This implies divergence of \(L(g)\) and makes the discarded \(N=0\) event negligible. A formulation for all diverging \(L=o(\sqrt g)\) can also combine the shorter-than-logarithmic equality of counts on p. 4 with the p. 5 nonemptiness result. A claim covering bounded or vanishing lengths requires an explicit empty-count convention or a different statement.

# F2. Minor: the spectral-mean calculation changes the sign of an exponent

Location. Proof of Theorem 30, p. 24, the line containing \(g^{3-\varepsilon}\), immediately after the sum of integrals.

The preceding expression contains \[\sum_{j=1}^{2g-3}\int_{1/2}^1
       \frac{t^s}{s}\frac{c(\varepsilon)}j\,g^{3-4s+\varepsilon}\,ds.\] Factoring the powers of \(g\) produces \(g^{3+\varepsilon}\), not \(g^{3-\varepsilon}\). This is a false asymptotic comparison, not merely a choice of a differently named constant.

Here is an admissible sequence testing the comparison directly. Fix \(\varepsilon=1/10\), set \(t=3g^4>2g^4\), and write \[H_g=\sum_{j=1}^{2g-3}j^{-1},\quad
 I=\int_{1/2}^1 3^s\,ds>0,\quad
 J=\int_{1/2}^1 \frac{3^s}{s}\,ds>0.\] The actual integral term is \(c(\varepsilon)g^{3+\varepsilon}H_gJ\); the printed replacement is \(c(\varepsilon)g^{3-\varepsilon}H_gI\). The accompanying term \(g\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt t)\) has size \(g^3/\log g\). Since \(H_g\asymp\log g\), the ratio of the actual full upper expression to the printed comparison expression has order \(g^\varepsilon(\log g)^2\to\infty\). A multiplicative constant independent of \(g\) cannot repair the printed sign.

Counterevidence and repair. The following line on p. 24 already uses \(g^{-1+\varepsilon}\), which is the result of the correct plus sign. With that sign, the remaining estimate is valid uniformly for \(t>2g^4\): \[\frac{\log t}{\log t-4\log g}
 \le 1+\frac{4\log g}{\log2}.\] The harmonic sum and this logarithmic loss can be absorbed in the stated small powers of \(g\). Also \(g\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt t)=O(g^{-1}\operatorname{Li}(t))\) in this range. This recovers Theorem 30 and, after the stated choice of a smaller \(\varepsilon\), Theorem 3. In the latter application \(gt^{-1/6}\le g^{-1}\) for \(t>g^{12}\). The error therefore has no demonstrated effect on those theorem conclusions.

# F3. Minor: two recursion formulas have excluded-index cases

## The general \(j\) reindexing

Location. Equation (46), p. 35, and the first display on p. 37. The old and new indices satisfy \[L_{\rm new}=d_1+d_j+L_{\rm old}-1.\] Thus the coefficient must be \(a_{L-d_1-d_j+1}\). The manuscript prints \(d_2\) in place of \(d_j\).

Take the stable, top-dimensional input \[(g,n)=(0,4),\qquad d=(0,0,1,0),\qquad j=3.\] Here \(|d|=1=3g-3+n\), and both sums have only the new index \(L=0\). The target volume is \(V_{0,3}=1\), since the moduli space of a sphere with three labelled points is a point. As \(a_0=1/2\) and \(a_1=\pi^2/12\), the original sum equals \[8(2d_3+1)a_0V_{0,3}=12,\] whereas the printed reindexed sum equals \(24a_1=2\pi^2\ne12\). This verifies the discrepancy within every degree and stability constraint.

## The zero-index step

Location. The last inequality in (51), p. 37. For \[g=1,\qquad n+1=2,\qquad d'=(2,0),\qquad j=2,\] one has \(|d'|=2=3g-3+(n+1)\), so the input is allowed. Using the source’s explicit convention that negative-index intersection numbers are zero, the sum in the penultimate line of (51) is \[4\bigl([\tau_{-1}]_{1,1}+[\tau_0]_{1,1}\bigr)=4V_{1,1}.\] Its alleged lower bound in the last line is \(8V_{1,1}\). The inequality is false because \(V_{1,1}>0\). A negative-index zero cannot be compared using monotonicity among nonnegative indices.

This counterexample concerns that particular step. It does not prove the entire inequality (52) false: positive terms discarded earlier may compensate for it.

## Counterevidence and the complete repair needed by the main proof

The actual use in (53) chooses \(j=2\) and \[a=\left\lfloor\frac{d_1+2}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad
 b=\left\lceil\frac{d_1+2}{2}\right\rceil\ge1,\qquad a+b=d_1+2.\] Thus the wrong general-\(j\) subscript and the \(d'_j=0\) comparison are both avoided. Correct the general subscript, and restrict that monotonicity step to \(d'_j\ge1\). Weak inequalities suffice for zero or empty recursion summands; the individual strict inequalities on p. 36 are not needed.

One further degree boundary deserves an explicit argument. If \(|d|=m:=3g-3+n\), the proposed split vector \((a,b,d_2,\ldots,d_n)\) has degree \(m+2\), above the new dimension \(m+1\). It is unnecessary to invent an unstated convention for that value. Set \[F_r=[\tau_r\tau_b\tau_{d_2}\cdots\tau_{d_n}]^{A,2}_{g,n+1}.\] The terms \(F_0,\ldots,F_{a-1}\) are legal. Telescope the differences for \(r<a-1\), and retain \(F_{a-1}\). At the top degree its defining sum has only the \(a_0\) term: \[F_{a-1}=8(2b+1)a_0[\tau_{d_1}\cdots\tau_{d_n}]_{g,n}.\] The other \(a-1\) differences have at least the same lower bound, by monotonicity among legal nonnegative indices. Below the top degree, \(F_a\) is legal and nonnegative, so the usual telescoping argument applies. Both cases give the required estimate \[[\tau_{d_1}\cdots\tau_{d_n}]_{g,n}
 \le
 \frac{[\tau_0\tau_b\tau_{d_2}\cdots\tau_{d_n}]_{g,n+1}}
      {8a_0a(2b+1)}.\] For \(d_1>0\), \(8a_0a(2b+1)\ge2d_1^2\), exactly as required for the subsequent iteration. Zero indices require no division or iteration. These local formula errors therefore do not invalidate the large-index bound in Theorems 41 and 5.

# F4. Minor: the second-moment proof loses factors two and four

Location. Lemma 61, p. 55, the expansions following So and Similarly. Compare the correct first-moment calculation on pp. 45–46.

Define \[I(L)=\int_0^L\frac{\sinh^2(x/2)}x\,dx.\] The exact integration formulas and the volume estimates printed on p. 55 give \[\mathbb E_g N_{\rm nsep}^{s}(X,L)
 =2I(L)\bigl(1+O(L^2/g)\bigr),
 \qquad
 \mathbb E_gY_1(X,L)=4I(L)^2\bigl(1+O(L^2/g)\bigr)\] when \(L\to\infty\) and \(L^2/g\to0\). Indeed, each repeated boundary variable contributes \[\left(\frac{\sinh(x/2)}{x/2}\right)^2
       =\frac{4\sinh^2(x/2)}{x^2}.\] The single-curve prefactor \(1/2\) therefore leaves a factor two. The two-curve prefactor \(1/4\), multiplied by the two factors four, leaves a factor four. The printed proof instead gives \(I(L)\) and \(I(L)^2\).

The sequence \(L(g)=\log g\) meets all hypotheses and has \(L^2/g\to0\). Along it, the ratios of the actual leading expressions to the printed ones tend to two and four. Neither difference is absorbed by the vanishing relative error.

Counterevidence, effect and repair. Lemma 48 already has the correct mean \(\mathbb E_gN_{\rm nsep}^{s}\sim\operatorname{Li}(e^L)/2\), consistent with \(I(L)\sim\operatorname{Li}(e^L)/4\). Restoring both factors preserves the leading-term cancellation: \[\left|\mathbb E_gY_1-(\mathbb E_gN_{\rm nsep}^{s})^2\right|
 \le C I(L)^2\frac{L^2}{g}
 =O(e^{2L}/g).\] Thus Lemma 61’s variance order, and its use in Theorem 59, remain correct. The confirmed effect is an erroneous normalization in intermediate formulas, not a failure of concentration.

# Core arguments independently checked

## Prime-geodesic estimates

The orientation convention is consistent: \(\pi_X(e^L)\) counts oriented primitive geodesics and \(N(X,L)=\pi_X(e^L)/2\). The real Fourier transforms of the two smoothing functions in Section 3 have the required opposite signs. Their imaginary-axis terms use a uniform \(\cosh\) estimate for \(s_j\in[1/2,1]\). The short-iterate contribution is harmonic, \(\sum_{k\le\epsilon/\ell}1/k\), yielding \(\log(\epsilon/\ell)\). It is not replaced by an unjustified uniform bound near a pinched geodesic.

With \(T=\log t\), the choice \(\epsilon=e^{-T/6}\) balances \[g\epsilon e^T
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 g\epsilon^{-2}e^{T/2}\] at \(ge^{5T/6}\); the remaining short-geodesic logarithmic term has scale \(e^{2T/3}\). The extra \(\epsilon T\) is bounded. The source images confirm absolute-value bars in (15), (21), (35) and (36); their omission from extracted text is not a mathematical defect.

The expectation argument keeps the \(j\)-factor in the spectral counting event: \(\lambda_j\le1/4-r^2\) forces at least \(j\) contributing eigenvalues. Uniformity over \(0\le r<1/2\) permits a common small-exponent choice. The layer-cake identity has derivative \(d\operatorname{Li}(t^s)/ds=t^s/s\). Together with the harmonic sum and the correction in F2 it gives the required mean bound. The integrability of \(1/\operatorname{sys}(X)\), the small-eigenvalue estimate and the trace formula remain cited external inputs.

## Uniformity of the intersection and volume bounds

Put \(q=2g-2+n\ge1\), \(m=3g-3+n\le3q/2\) and \(B=120/(10-\pi^2)\). The proof uses the uniform volume increment \(V_{g,n+1}/V_{g,n}\le Bq\) from Theorem 10(1), rather than a fixed-\(n\) asymptotic outside its scope.

For \(h_i=2d_i^2/(3Bq)>1\), select \(p_i=1+\lfloor\log_4h_i\rfloor\). Writing \(s=\#\{i:h_i>1\}\), the concavity calculation yields \[|p|\le s+\frac{m}{Bs}<2q.\] Here \(s\le q\): for \(g\ge1\), use \(s\le n\le q\); for \(g=0\), use \(s\le|d|\le n-3=q-1\). The product estimate in (57) is equivalent, for \(x=\log_4h>0\) and \(p=1+\lfloor x\rfloor\), to \((p-x)^2\le p\). This follows from \(0<p-x\le1\). Moreover \(\sup_{h\ge1}h^{1-\frac12\log_4h}=2\), giving a fully uniform constant in Theorem 5.

The passage to volumes preserves all factors depending on the number of selected boundary variables. For integer \(k\ge1\), \[\frac1{(2d+2k+1)^{2k}(2d+1)!}
 \le\frac1{(2d+2k+1)!},
 \qquad
 \sum_{d\ge0}\frac{x^{2d}}{(2d+2k+1)!}
 \le\frac{\sinh x}{x^{2k+1}}.\] The extension of the coefficient sum is justified by nonnegativity. Rescaling \(2x\) to \(x\) gives the stated factor four in the constant. Choose precisely the subset for which \(x_i^2>c(k)q\); use the baseline volume inequality for an empty subset. Zero boundary lengths have their continuous values. If \(k\) is real, first use \(\lceil k\rceil\) and then lower the exponent of factors belonging to \([0,1]\). The all-zero-degree case in Theorem 5 is the baseline bound \([\tau_0\cdots\tau_0]/V=1\), without literal division by zero.

## The two length regimes and the tuple argument

For \(L^2/g\to\infty\), the volume estimate gives \(\mathbb E_gN^s\ll_k\operatorname{Li}(e^L)(g/L^2)^{k+1}\). Markov’s inequality loses one power, as stated. On the PGT good set, \(N\ge\operatorname{Li}(e^L)/4\), since \(ge^{-L/4}\to0\). For \(12\log g<L=o(\sqrt g)\), the mean of the nonseparating count and the expected PGT bound control the nonnegative difference \(N-N_{\rm nsep}^s\); the same denominator bound completes the argument.

For \(L=O(\log g)\) and \(L\to\infty\), the large-complexity cutoff can be fixed in terms of the constant in \(L\le C\log g\). The factors \(T^4e^{5T}/g^m\) are then negligible for the chosen large \(m\), and the fixed-complexity polynomial is absorbed into a small exponential loss. After F4, the decomposition into the diagonal, \(Y_1\), separating pairs and intersecting pairs gives relative variance \[O_C\!\left(Le^{-L}+(\log g)^2g^{-1/2}\right).\] Chebyshev and Markov inequalities have the stated limiting effects. The distinct-pair convention is supplied on p. 48 and explicitly retained in Definition 60; diagonal pairs are counted separately.

The appendix’s integer length-bin induction gives \(m^k(1+L)^{k-1}e^L\). For \(n=0\), Corollary 15 supplies the initial bound directly, without a boundary-doubling construction. In the boundary-shortening application, a negative reduced length yields zero tuples; otherwise its polynomial factor is bounded by \((1+L)^{k-1}\). The full geometric shortening argument cited from [WX22] remains an external dependency, not an independently reconstructed theorem in this review.

# Verification limits and attribution

Arbitrary-\(A\) scope. Theorem 33 is stated with a constant independent of \(A\). The optimization on p. 30 uses \(\epsilon=\sqrt A e^{-T/4}<1\), directly covering \(T>2\log A\). This review did not complete a proof, from the supplied estimates alone, uniform in a growing \(A\) throughout the remaining transition range. It also did not find a surface contradicting Theorem 33. For example, setting \(R=\sqrt A e^{-T/4}\), the fixed-smoothing and crude-counting bounds leave an unclosed comparison when \(1\ll R\ll T\). This is recorded as a verification limit, not a confirmed finding.

The main random-surface application uses one fixed universal \(A=A_0\) from Theorem 32. Its remaining bounded \(T\)-interval can be handled uniformly in \(X,g\) by Corollary 15 and the finite small-spectrum bound. The endpoint terms in partial integration are also bounded with this fixed \(A_0\). Thus no arbitrary-\(A\) interpolation is required for that application.

The full external results of Selberg, Wolpert, Mirzakhani, Otal–Rosas, Monk, [WX22], [LW24] and the cited Weil–Petersson volume asymptotics were not reproved. In particular, the orbital-subsurface estimates on pp. 51–53 and the geometric construction in Theorem 65 are retained as explicit dependencies. The present assessment is not an independent verification of all those references.
